% ---------------------------------------------------------------- [A] abstract (replacement)
Validation on our own simulator uses a continuous 15-minute schedule and an adopted $0.02$~mag
detectability floor, so the numbers above are benchmark values rather than \Rom{} yield forecasts.
On RMDC26, a simulation of the survey made independently with GULLS for the Roman Microlensing Data
Challenge 2026 ($56,975$ selected events, none used for training), the shipped checkpoint flags $35.6\%$ of single lenses as anomalous,
mostly because that simulation's F146 observing pauses are absent from our training seasons.
Fine-tuning the same network on our own simulations with gap augmentation, finite-source single lenses
and the measured pauses raises planetary recall at a $5.2\%$ single-lens false-alarm
budget from $0.214$ to $0.349$--$0.391$ per simulated event across three
training seeds ($0.144$ to $0.385$--$0.407$ weighted by event rate); at
its own threshold, chosen on our simulations, the recommended checkpoint flags $2.4\%$ of single
lenses. RMDC26 also guided the diagnosis and the
choice of checkpoint, so these values are optimistic for it. Applying our labelling policy to RMDC26's
noise-free curves, $44$--$46\%$ of its selected planetary events
carry no anomaly the policy would claim within one season, so we report recall against both label sets.
Code, weights, and reproducibility artifacts are released openly.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- [B] Sec. limits, replacement paragraph
\paragraph{The schedule, not the sampling rate, is what the shipped model cannot survive.}
Our training grid is continuous within a season. The survey as simulated in the Roman Microlensing Data Challenge 2026 (RMDC26) release of
the Roman Galactic Exoplanet Survey Project Infrastructure Team \citep{RGESPIT2026} is not: following the
survey design \citep{STScI2025GBTDS,ROTAC2025}, F146 pauses for $43$--$44$~h
per $70.7$-day season, in six or seven pauses whose phases differ between its six
high-cadence seasons (only those near days $1.0$, $35.2$ and
$69.5$ recur), and the season is $1.3$ days shorter than our 72-day
window. Essentially none of our training events contains an empty mid-season F146 bin, so the model's
only prior for an empty token is the unrevealed future of a truncated season. Imposing the measured
pauses on our own held-out seasons, with nothing else changed, collapses the shipped model
(Table~\ref{tab:gap}): single-lens recall falls from $0.94$ to
$0.03$, mostly to \texttt{NonPSPL}, and \texttt{Flat} recall from
$0.96$ to $0.17$, mostly to \texttt{PeriodicVar}; the eruptive
and long-period variables and \texttt{NonPSPL} itself also degrade, and \texttt{NonPSPL} precision
falls from $0.72$ to $0.09$. A single gap of half an hour costs
nothing, but one gap of 1--2~h placed at mid-season already lowers single-lens recall from $0.93$ to
$0.86$ ($100$ events per class; the cost depends on where the gap falls), the lost events
going to \texttt{NonPSPL} and \texttt{PeriodicVar}. The remedy is an augmentation, not a new model:
blanking one to eight contiguous runs of 1--12~h in every band during a 12-epoch warm-start fine-tune of
the shipped weights raises held-out average precision under the measured pauses from
$0.169$ to $0.910$, while clean-data average precision falls only from
$0.959$ to $0.951$. Training on the pauses themselves does better still,
provided they are the right pauses: drawing one of RMDC26's six measured seasons per training example gives
$0.921$ under the measured pauses against $0.911$ for random gaps, with clean
average precision $0.948$ against $0.952$, whereas training on a single season's
pauses scores only $0.568$ across the six seasons, its own included. Both schedule arms
train with the caustic-in-gap relabel off. That rule relabels a binary as \texttt{PSPL} when the bins
around its recorded onset are blanked, but the onset records when the anomaly first becomes detectable,
not where the caustic is; with a fixed mask and the legacy 7.2-day onset grid it made
$20.1\%$ of the \texttt{NonPSPL}-labelled training events eligible for relabelling, while
with random gaps it makes no measurable difference ($0.910$ with it, $0.911$
without). We later stored each event's noise-free residual against its best single-lens fit per 2-hour bin
(one natural-prior training shard, $7,403$ events, $4$ presentations each) and applied the
label rule to the bins that survive: under the measured pauses the rule gives $10.5\%$ of
binary presentations a single-lens label although their surviving anomaly would still be claimed, while
with the rule off $1.4\%$ keep a binary label that the surviving bins do not support. The shipped checkpoint's input contract is therefore \emph{continuous F146}; for a gapped
schedule, use the recommended checkpoint of \S\ref{sec:gulls} at its own threshold.

\section{Cross-simulator validation on an independent \Rom{} simulation}\label{sec:gulls}

Every number so far comes from our own simulator. RMDC26 \citep{RGESPIT2026} is a simulation of the
survey made by a different group with GULLS \citep{Penny2013,Penny2019} and the SynthPop Galactic model
\citep{Kluter2025}, with its own implementation of the observing schedule, photometry and Galactic
population, released openly for testing and development. We use it to test transfer: no RMDC26 event entered
training and no operating threshold was chosen on it. It was, however, used to diagnose the residual
false alarms, to set the upper end of the single-lens source-size prior, and to choose among the
$14$ fine-tuned checkpoints scored on it (one seed each; two further seeds of the chosen
recipe were trained afterwards), and the recommendation below
was made after seeing both its per-event and its rate-weighted results, so the numbers for the recommended
checkpoint are the best of several on this set and are optimistic.

\subsection{Data, selection, and input contract}

RMDC26 contains $377,999$ events: $299,466$ single lenses (1S1L),
$35,647$ planetary lenses (1S2L) and $42,886$ planetary lenses with a
second source (2S2L). Every 1S2L and 2S2L event carries a planetary companion (median mass ratio
$1.4\times10^{-4}$ and $1.2\times10^{-4}$ on the scored events; similar but not identical
distributions, KS $p \sim 10^{-18}$), the source is flagged binary in $55\%$
of scored 2S2L events, and both planetary classes are pre-selected by GULLS' own detection statistic
($60$ or more for every such event). RMDC26 ships no pure binary-source class, so the
\citet{Gaudi1998} degeneracy of \S\ref{sec:sims} remains untested, and a \texttt{NonPSPL} call on a 2S2L
event may respond to its second source rather than its planet. RMDC26's anomalies are all planetary,
whereas $82\%$ of our \texttt{NonPSPL} evaluation events are
stellar-mass-ratio binaries (median $q = 0.12$), so transfer recall is not comparable to
the in-distribution completeness of \S\ref{sec:results}.

Selection uses catalogue parameters, never the model's output, and is applied identically to the
three classes. We keep events whose host single-lens peak amplitude, computed from the catalogue impact
parameter and blend fraction, is at least $0.1$~mag (the median single-lens event is
$0.063$~mag and $41\%$ pass) and whose Einstein timescale lies in
our prior's support, $1 \le t_{\rm E} \le 300$~d ($33\%$ of RMDC26 single lenses are
sub-day, against $0.31\%$ and $0.15\%$ of the planetary classes).
That leaves $100,935$ events. Of these, $56.4\%$ ($56,975$:
$33,353$ 1S1L, $11,388$ 1S2L, $12,234$ 2S2L) peak inside one of the
six high-cadence seasons and are scored; $22.0\%$ peak in one of the
$109$--$120$-day gaps between seasons (\S\ref{sec:gulls:seasons}),
$3.6\%$ before or after the survey, $17.9\%$ in one of the four
low-cadence seasons (outside our support), and $84$ lack a usable baseline. The
amplitude cut is on the \emph{host} lens: it removes most wide-orbit planets, whose host is barely
magnified ($10\%$ of 1S2L events with $s > 2$ pass, against $85\%$ at
$0.5 \le s < 2$), so the scored planetary events are dominated by resonant and central-caustic
geometries. Some scored inputs lie outside our training priors: the baseline is brighter than our
20~mag limit for $10\%$ of single lenses and $21$--$23\%$
of planetary events, the blend fraction is below our prior's minimum for
$21$--$35\%$ of events, and the scored single lenses are much shorter than our
prior's: $38\%$ have $t_{\rm E} < 3$~d ($16\%$ weighted by event rate),
against $1.8\%$ of our prior's mass (median $t_{\rm E}$ $4.3$~d, against
$13.5$~d for 1S2L).

The model is given the 72-day window starting at the season boundary in all three bands. The
baseline is the median F146 magnitude more than $5\,t_{\rm E}$ from the peak over the whole survey,
using the catalogue $t_0$ and $t_{\rm E}$; a real-time pipeline would have neither. Observations are pooled
to one value per 15-minute epoch before binning, as the training cache is built; binning by raw
observation instead moves the shipped checkpoint's single-lens false-alarm rate from $35.6\%$
to $45.6\%$, so the operating point is sensitive to this choice. Two sampling differences survive preprocessing:
the pauses, and partial bin occupancy, since RMDC26's colour visits displace one of eight F146 epochs
in about $35\%$ of the 2-h bins, an occupancy our training data never contain.
Setting that occupancy to full on $6,024$ real RMDC26 inputs lowers the recommended checkpoint's
single-lens false alarms from $6.8\%$ to $5.9\%$ and its planetary recall from
$0.42$ to $0.33$: the model reads partial occupancy as mild evidence of an anomaly.
(The pattern cannot be imposed faithfully on our binned simulations, because a bin's features would
still come from all its epochs; teaching it would need augmentation before binning.)

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:gulls:results}

Table~\ref{tab:gulls} gives the single-lens false-alarm rate at the frozen operating threshold and
planetary recall at matched single-lens false-alarm budgets, on the identical $56,975$ events for
every checkpoint; the matched-budget comparison is the one a threshold shift cannot fake. Rates are per
simulated event. RMDC26 also gives each event an event-rate weight, which the release recommends for
representative statistics, and because the unweighted sample
over-represents short single lenses while false alarms rise with timescale (for the recommended checkpoint
from $5.3\%$ of single lenses at $1 \le t_{\rm E} < 3$~d to $11.7\%$ at
30--300~d), the weighted rates differ; the table gives both, and we say where a conclusion depends on
the weighting. The intervals quoted below come from a bootstrap over events, with the classes resampled
separately; they cover the evaluation sample, not training variation.

The shipped checkpoint flags $35.6\%$ of single lenses. Every flagged event is, by
construction, called \texttt{NonPSPL}; across all single lenses the model's first choice splits between
\texttt{NonPSPL} and \texttt{PeriodicVar} with almost none called \texttt{PSPL}, the signature of the
empty mid-season tokens. Gap augmentation brings the false-alarm rate to $11.6\%$. Its
residual false alarms rise monotonically with $\rho/|u_0|$, the ratio of source radius to impact
parameter, from $0.046$ for point-like sources to $0.674$ above 3.
The high-ratio bins are high-magnification events with very small $|u_0|$, not large sources: only
$43$ scored single lenses have $\rho > 1$ (99th percentile $0.29$). The
likely cause is in our generator: its single lenses were point sources while its binaries sampled a
finite source, so a finite-source single lens, whose rounded peak resembles no training \texttt{PSPL},
was read as anomalous. Adding finite-source single lenses (a uniform, achromatic disc computed with
\textsc{VBBinaryLensing}'s \texttt{ESPLMag2} \citep{Bozza2018}; $\rho$ log-uniform up to 5, binaries
unchanged) and fine-tuning for a further 12 epochs from the gap-augmented checkpoint lowers the rate to
$4.8\%$ and the largest-ratio bin to $0.288$, while planetary recall at the
$5.2\%$ budget rises from $0.295$ to $0.390$.

That comparison mixes two changes, the physics and 12 further epochs on a larger pool rich in
high-magnification single lenses. A control with the same pool shape, recipe and epochs but point-source
single lenses separates them (its events are a different random draw from the same priors): the extra
training alone lowers false alarms to $6.4\%$ and raises recall at the budget to
$0.361$, and the finite-source single lenses then take them to $4.8\%$ and
$0.390$ (mean recall over false-alarm rates up to 30\%: $0.490$ and
$0.539$). Most of the gain over gap augmentation is therefore the extra training. The
physics lowers false alarms in every $\rho/|u_0|$ bin and most, in rate, in the two largest-ratio bins
(from $0.410$ and $0.513$ to $0.280$ and $0.288$),
but part of the reduction falls where finite size cannot matter, so not all of it is the physics. On our
own finite-source held-out only the physics restores single-lens recall for sources larger than the
impact parameter ($0.22$ and $0.16$ for the control, $0.66$
and $0.84$ with finite sources, in the 1--3 and $> 3$ bins). Weighted by event rate its
false-alarm reduction holds ($-1.0$ points, interval $-1.5$ to
$-0.4$) but its recall gain at the budget is not resolved ($+0.010$, interval
$-0.010$ to $0.027$), and both differences are smaller than the training-seed
range measured below. Widening the \emph{binary} source-size prior at the same
time was worse: false alarms $8.7\%$ instead of $4.8\%$, recall
$0.344$ instead of $0.390$. The largest-ratio bins remain near
$0.288$, so the missing physics explains only part of the residual. Among the scored single
lenses, false alarms are $11.8\%$ for baselines brighter than our 20~mag limit against
$4.1\%$ inside it, which makes out-of-support brightness a candidate for part of the rest.

Training on the measured per-season pauses instead of random gaps also transfers, under both weightings.
On one pool, with everything else fixed, it lowers single-lens false alarms from
$12.8\%$ to $10.0\%$ and raises recall at the $5.2\%$
budget from $0.279$ to $0.352$ (weighted by event rate, from
$0.334$ to $0.382$; interval of the gain $0.033$ to
$0.061$), and it is ahead in $6$ of the $6$ seasons
scored separately. That arm starts from the shipped weights on a natural-prior pool.

Combining the two gives the recommended checkpoint, the row marked (rec.) in Table~\ref{tab:gulls}: the
finite-source recipe trained on the measured pauses with the caustic-in-gap relabel off. It also uses a
smooth finite-source magnification (\textsc{VBBinaryLensing}'s \texttt{ESPLMag} in place of
\texttt{ESPLMag2}, which leaves steps of a few millimagnitudes where it hands over to the point-source
formula), and its truncation augmentation evaluates finite-source single lenses with that magnification.
Those two changes together do nothing measurable: the finite-source recipe rerun with them flags
$4.8\%$ of single lenses ($4.8\%$ for the original), its planetary recall is
within $0.001$ of the original's at every budget, and its false-alarm rate in every
$\rho/|u_0|$ bin within $0.001$. Per simulated event the recommended checkpoint's
planetary recall is within $0.008$ of the finite-source checkpoint's at every budget, and
at the frozen threshold it flags more single lenses ($6.3\%$ against $4.8\%$),
in every $\rho/|u_0|$ bin, though by less than its own seeds differ (below). Weighted by event rate it is ahead at every budget, by
$0.017$--$0.024$ for 1S2L (every interval above zero), and at the
$5.2\%$ budget by more for 2S2L ($0.450$ against $0.405$), and on our own held-out events with
the measured pauses imposed it is also better (average precision $0.893$ against
$0.874$; clean $0.929$ against $0.931$). This training run
therefore matches or beats the finite-source checkpoint at every matched budget under both weightings,
and we chose it on that basis. Two further seeds of the same recipe, trained after the choice (the last
two rows of Table~\ref{tab:gulls}), do not: per simulated event they trail the finite-source checkpoint
by $0.032$--$0.041$ at the $5.2\%$ budget, and weighted by event rate
they tie it. On our own held-out events with the measured pauses, which involve no RMDC26 selection, all
three seeds score at least as well as the finite-source checkpoint (average precision
$0.876$--$0.894$ against $0.874$). RMDC26 therefore does not separate
the two recipes. We keep the first seed as the recommended checkpoint, trained on the survey's measured
pauses, and \S\S\ref{sec:gulls:labels}--\ref{sec:gulls:cascade} use it; its RMDC26 numbers are the best
of the three seeds, in both weightings.

Other changes did not help. Matching GULLS' noise, whose F146 errors equal ours for
sources brighter than 20th magnitude and reach $2.64$ times ours at 24--25~mag (a
$7$-fold background variance reproduces this; a global factor does not), gave a
checkpoint that was worse at every matched budget and in false alarms ($12.1\%$; recall
$0.298$), although its recall at the frozen threshold was higher; with a single seed and a
single noise level we do not attribute a mechanism. Recording the anomaly onset on a finer grid (an
attempt made before the off-by-one error in its first implementation was found) moved recall at the
$5.2\%$ budget from $0.390$ to $0.375$.

The three seeds of the recommended recipe also measure the training noise behind every comparison in this
section. At the $5.2\%$ budget they reach planetary recall $0.349$--$0.391$
per simulated event and $0.385$--$0.407$ weighted by event rate, and they flag
$4.7$--$7.3\%$ of single lenses at the frozen threshold. The paired intervals quoted
above cover only the sampling of events: between two seeds of one recipe they exclude zero (seed 2
against seed 1: $-0.042$ per event, interval $-0.049$ to $-0.033$). We
therefore do not interpret a difference between single training runs that is smaller than the seed range
in the same weighting ($0.042$ per event and $0.022$ weighted, at the
$5.2\%$ budget). The extra training and the measured pauses pass this test in both
weightings; the finite-source physics passes it in the false alarms of the two largest-ratio bins, where
the seeds differ by at most $0.083$, but not in recall; the recommended recipe's lead over
the finite-source recipe does not pass it. Two fine-tunes of one recipe on different pools with the same
seed differ by $0.014$--$0.023$ per event across the three budgets, within the seed
range.

Removing the colour bands lowers planetary recall at matched budget by
$0.042$--$0.068$ for each of the $4$ checkpoints ablated.
Most of the gain comes from events without a claimable anomaly: for the recommended checkpoint, colour
raises recall at the $5.2\%$ budget from $0.546$ to $0.575$ on
planetary events whose anomaly our policy can detect in F146, but the flag rate from
$0.174$ to $0.248$ on events without one. We have not identified the mechanism
(class-dependent colours in RMDC26, or chromatic effects our F146 policy cannot see), so this is not
evidence that the colour bands carry anomaly information across simulators.

\paragraph{Operating point.} The frozen threshold was chosen on gap-free, point-source data. We chose
the recommended checkpoint's threshold at the paper's 90\% purity target on our own simulated seasons
with the measured RMDC26 pauses imposed, without reading RMDC26, on a finite-source held-out pool whose
population-weighted \texttt{NonPSPL} prevalence ($6.0\%$) is close to our test set's
($5.6\%$). The paper's procedure takes the threshold on one 20\% validation slice; here that
slice gives $0.946$, while 200 random slices scatter between $0.947$ and
$0.965$ (68\% of them), which moves RMDC26's single-lens false-alarm rate between
$1.8$ and $3.1\%$; we therefore use the whole pool, which gives $0.956$.
At $0.956$ the checkpoint flags $2.4\%$ of RMDC26 single lenses ($3.5\%$
weighted by event rate) with planetary recall $0.29$ /
$0.33$ (1S2L / 2S2L; $0.36$ weighted, 1S2L); the single slice's
threshold would give $3.2\%$ and $0.32$. Calibrated the same way, the three
seeds of the recipe together span $2.4$--$3.0\%$ false alarms and recall
$0.259$--$0.294$. The finite-source checkpoint,
calibrated the same way, sits at a different point of the same trade-off ($0.957$:
$1.6\%$ and $0.24$). The gap-augmented checkpoint has no usable operating point on a
finite-source population: it reaches 90\% purity only on its few most confident events.

\subsection{What ``recall'' means across two label ontologies}\label{sec:gulls:labels}

GULLS assigns classes by generator, subject to its own detection cut; BinML's classes are observational
(\S\ref{sec:detcond}). Because RMDC26 ships each event's noise-free light curve and per-epoch error, the
two can be reconciled: we applied our labelling rule, the $\Delta\chi^2$ thresholds and the $0.02$~mag
floor against the best-fitting static point-source single-lens model on the noise-free F146 curve, with
GULLS' errors as $\sigma$ and single lenses labelled exactly as in training, to the first
$1,600$ scored single lenses and $2,500$ scored events of each planetary
class. Under that rule $44\%$ of 1S2L and
$46\%$ of 2S2L events carry no anomaly the policy would claim within one season:
$26\%$ of the planetary events fail the $\Delta\chi^2$ cut and $19\%$ pass
it but fall under the floor. Rescaling the $\Delta\chi^2$ cuts for RMDC26's $11\%$ more F146 epochs per
season moves these fractions by under a point. The figure depends on the floor, the single-season window
and our selection; it is not a statement about what a survey could detect. Recall against generator
labels mixes detectable and undetectable planets and is not bounded by the detectable fraction: the
recommended checkpoint flags $25\%$ of the planetary events without a detectable
anomaly. On those with one, its recall at the frozen threshold is $0.543$ /
$0.621$ (1S2L / 2S2L) against $0.416$ / $0.449$ on
generator labels, and $0.411$ / $0.480$ at its own operating
point. Recall tracks significance more closely than amplitude: it rises from $0.24$ for
anomalies with $160 \le \Delta\chi^2 < 500$ to $0.89$ above $10^5$.

The rule also bears on the floor. The checkpoint flags $37\%$ of the planetary events
whose anomaly is significant but below $0.02$~mag, at a similar rate however far below
($35$--$40\%$ across sub-floor amplitude bins); with F146
alone the rate falls to $16$--$25\%$, so much of it follows the
colour behaviour above. It also flags $5$ of the $20$ single
lenses whose static refit shows a significant sub-floor misfit (95\% interval
$11$--$47\%$; such misfits come from effects our static
point-source refit does not model, such as parallax): the response to sub-floor misfit is not specific
to planets. Lowering the floor from $0.02$ to
$0.005$~mag raises the fraction of 1S2L events with a claimable anomaly from $56\%$ to
$70\%$; raising it to $0.05$~mag lowers it to $40\%$. This is the
label side of the floor sensitivity; retraining under another floor is not covered here.

\subsection{Events whose peak falls between seasons}\label{sec:gulls:seasons}

The single-season input contract leaves out the $22.0\%$ of eligible events that peak in
one of the gaps between seasons, where nothing is observed. For the first $1,000$ such events of each
class we scored the high-cadence season on each side of the gap and kept the larger anomaly probability,
and applied the labelling rule of \S\ref{sec:gulls:labels} to each season, starting the single-lens refit
also at the in-window maximum because the host's peak lies outside the window. A high-cadence season
borders the gap for $76$, $91$ and $86\%$ of the
between-season 1S1L, 1S2L and 2S2L events; the rest border only low-cadence seasons. In those seasons a
claimable anomaly is visible for $44\%$ of the scorable 1S2L and $34\%$ of the
2S2L events, against $56\%$ and $54\%$ in-season, so the unobserved gap
costs $12$ and $20$ points of claimable anomalies. At the frozen threshold
the recommended checkpoint flags $0.7\%$ of these single lenses (at most $1.6\%$ at 95\%
confidence) and $20\%$ / $19\%$ of their planetary events, and recalls
$0.43$ / $0.47$ of those with a claimable anomaly in a wing, against
$0.543$ / $0.621$ in-season: the model is conservative when a season
holds only a wing of the event (we did not test why). Lowering the threshold until $5.2\%$ of this population's single lenses
are flagged ($0.69$, set on only $38$ exceedances) raises that recall to
$0.54$ / $0.61$, comparable to the in-season $0.53$ /
$0.62$ at the same budget, so scoring seasons around a gap would need its own
operating point. No model can recover anomalies that fall in the unobserved gaps; what a whole-mission
model would add is events whose light curves span seasons and the four low-cadence seasons
($17.9\%$ of eligible events), which are outside BinML's support.

\subsection{The partial-season cascade on RMDC26}\label{sec:gulls:cascade}
Section~\ref{sec:cascade} measures how early the model flags an anomaly, on our own simulator and for
eligible binaries only. We repeated its primary protocol on RMDC26 with the recommended checkpoint at the
frozen threshold. The high-cadence season of $3,000$ single lenses and $3,000$ binaries was
revealed in half-day steps, every cut was scored from F146 alone, and the alert is the first cut above the
threshold. The onset is the first cut at which our labelling rule (\S\ref{sec:gulls:labels}) is met on
GULLS' noise-free light curve with GULLS' errors, so it comes from a simulator we did not write. The
$1,667$ eligible events are the binaries whose anomaly is claimable over the whole season; nothing is
selected on the model's output.

Premature alerts stay rare: $2.2\%$ of the eligible events are flagged before the onset (95\%
interval $1.6$--$3.0\%$), against $1.4\%$ and $4.2\%$ for the
giant-planet and Neptune strata of the in-house scan ($138$ and $48$ events; shipped
model, our simulator), the only strata comparable with RMDC26's planetary anomalies. Alerts come later: the
median lag of the others is $+6.5$~d, against $+4.5$~d in-house. Detection does not
transfer: $61\%$ ($59$--$64\%$) of the eligible events are flagged by the end of the
season, against $89\%$ and $73\%$ in-house, in line with the full-season recall on
claimable anomalies ($0.543$ and $0.621$, \S\ref{sec:gulls:labels}).
Revealing all three bands lowers premature alerts to $1.1\%$, lengthens the lag to
$+11.5$~d and lowers detection to $59\%$. The in-house scan measured three bands
only for its whole population, mostly stellar binaries (premature alerts $1.6\%$ to
$1.0\%$, lag $+5.0$ to $+7.0$~d, detection
$89.0\%$ to $91.0\%$), so its planetary strata cannot be compared on this point.

Scanning single lenses gives the alert burden on a second simulator. $6.2\%$
of single lenses raise an alert at some point in the season ($0.86$ alerts per 1,000 events
per day). If 1\% of events were planetary binaries, $5\%$ of alerts would come from a claimable
anomaly, and $21\%$ at 5\%. At RMDC26's own rate-weighted planetary fraction, which
over-represents planets, the figure is $49\%$. These count single-lens contamination only;
RMDC26 contains no variable stars or flat sources (our synthetic ones raise no alert, \S\ref{sec:cascade},
but real variables are untested), so a real stream would be less pure. On our own simulator, with
continuous seasons and the shipped checkpoint, only $0.7\%$ of single lenses alert,
almost all of them binaries below the detectability policy; RMDC26's single lenses alert several times
as often. At the recalibrated
threshold single-lens alerts fall to $3.2\%$ and purity at 1\% rises to
$8\%$, but detection falls to $50\%$, premature alerts to $1.3\%$, and the
lag grows to $+8.0$~d. The cascade's low premature-alert rate therefore holds on another simulator,
but its alerts are not a follow-up list by themselves: at realistic prevalence they need a second stage that
removes single-lens contaminants.

\subsection{What this test does and does not establish}

It establishes that the shipped model's failure on a gapped schedule is a property of the schedule,
reproducible without any GULLS data and remediable by augmentation, preferably with the measured pauses;
that continued training on a pool rich in high-magnification single lenses removed most of the remaining
single-lens false alarms; and that finite-source single lenses, a physics our generator lacked, removed a
further part, most strongly in the largest $\rho/|u_0|$ bins. It does
not establish a \Rom{} planet yield: RMDC26's planetary population, detection cut and our amplitude
selection all differ from our training population; neither simulator contains real systematics; our
detectability policy uses a static point-source refit and a uniform source without limb darkening; the
fine-tunes' gap relabelling is approximate (\S\ref{sec:limits}); every fine-tune but the recommended recipe is a
single seed, and that recipe's seeds differ by more than several of the comparisons between recipes; some
conclusions depend on whether events are weighted by rate; and RMDC26 was used to choose among
checkpoints. It does not test the binary-source degeneracy, for which
RMDC26 has no class. Sub-day single lenses, outside our timescale prior, are flagged as anomalies by the
recommended checkpoint less often than in-support single lenses ($1.6\%$ of
$1,385$ unweighted and $5.7\%$ weighted by event rate, against
$6.3\%$ and $8.4\%$), and their most probable class is mostly
\texttt{PeriodicVar} ($60$--$93\%$ across checkpoints), with
$15\%$ read as microlensing of either kind; a model for free-floating planets needs sub-day
timescales in its prior, not a threshold change.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- [D] Discussion paragraph
BinML's input is one contiguous season. Of RMDC26's eligible events, that contract leaves out those
peaking between seasons, $27\%$ of single lenses but $9$--$10\%$
of planetary events, and those peaking in low-cadence seasons, $23\%$ and
$4$--$9\%$ (the release concentrates events in the high-cadence
seasons: $64$--$71\%$ of its catalogue peaks fall in them, although
they span $25\%$ of the mission). Scoring the high-cadence seasons around a gap makes
most of the former scorable at a false-alarm cost below one percent, but at the frozen threshold flags only
$20\%$ of their planetary events; a claimable anomaly survives in a wing for fewer planetary
events than in-season, and the operating point would have to be set separately (\S\ref{sec:gulls:seasons}).
Low-cadence seasons are outside the model's support. A whole-mission model, taking the full light curve, would address both and let the
partial-season cascade of \S\ref{sec:cascade} run across season boundaries. It needs a mission-length
simulation, labels over the full mission and a hierarchical input, and is the natural next version.
