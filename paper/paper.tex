\title{BinML: Detectability-Conditioned Anomaly Triage for Synthetic \\
\textit{Roman}-like Partial Light Curves}

\author[0009-0007-4447-6325]{Kunal Bhatia}
\affiliation{Independent Researcher}

\begin{abstract}
\Rom{}'s Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey will yield more microlensing light curves than anyone can inspect, and
their anomalies must be found while the events are in progress. We present \textbf{BinML}, a compact network that
classifies partial \Rom{}-like light curves in three bands into six classes, including one for detectable
binary-lens anomalies. Two labelling choices make the task well posed. Labels are conditioned on an adopted
detectability policy, so a binary whose perturbation falls below its thresholds is labelled a single lens, and on the
part of the season already observed, so a binary's label moves from flat to single lens to anomaly as evidence
arrives. On complete synthetic seasons BinML recovers $\bmlCompletenessPct\%$ of detectable anomalies at purity
$\bmlPurity$. Revealed half a day at a time in F146, it alerts within the season on $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of eligible
binaries and before a truth-informed anomaly onset on only $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$. On RMDC26, an independent
simulation of the survey, the released model fails, mostly because our training seasons lack the survey's F146
observing pauses: per simulated event it flags $\bmlGullsFaShipped\%$ of single lenses. Fine-tuning on the measured
pauses with finite-source single lenses lowers this to $\bmlGullsFaCombined\%$ and raises planetary recall; RMDC26
guided the choice of that checkpoint, so these values are optimistic. All results are synthetic benchmarks that
depend on the adopted detectability floor and anomaly prevalence; they are not yield forecasts. Code, weights and
artifacts are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gravitational microlensing complements other planet-detection methods because it responds to a
lens's mass rather than its light. It is sensitive to cold, low-mass planets far from their host
stars and to unbound planetary-mass objects drifting through the Galaxy---populations that transit
and radial-velocity surveys, biased toward hot and close-in planets, recover less readily
\citep{Mao1991,GouldLoeb1992,Suzuki2016}. The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will exploit
this sensitivity systematically. Its Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey (GBTDS) will monitor the
crowded bulge fields at high cadence in the near-infrared. It is predicted to detect of order a
thousand bound planets and to constrain the free-floating planet mass function
\citep{Spergel2015,Penny2019,Johnson2020,Sumi2023,Gaudi2012}, and the same data will yield
transiting planets \citep{Wilson2023}. In its design
\citep{ROTAC2025,STScI2025GBTDS}, the wide F146 filter samples the light curve every
$\sim12$~minutes; two auxiliary bands, F087 and F213, add colour information on staggered
$\sim$6-hour cycles.

That sensitivity brings an operational problem of scale. Genuine microlensing events are a small
minority of the survey light curves, detectable binary-lens anomalies are rarer still, and fitting
a full binary-lens model to every source is impractical. A cheap classifier can therefore rank
which partial light curves deserve inspection or detailed modelling. Timing matters to that task:
before a caustic perturbation appears, a binary lens can resemble an ordinary single lens. A model
evaluated only on complete seasons can assign an anomalous label before the evidence that defines
it has entered the observed window. Existing rapid-response systems already show the value of
low-latency anomaly detection in microlensing \citep{Udalski2003,Dominik2007,Dominik2008,Tsapras2009}.
Here we ask a narrower question: as an eligible synthetic binary is progressively revealed, when
does the classifier first cross an anomaly threshold relative to a truth-informed onset?

Machine learning has already been used at the scale relevant to microlensing surveys.
\citet{Wyrzykowski2015} searched 150 million OGLE-III sources and classified 3,718 candidates, while
\citet{Husseiniova2021} applied a machine-learning search to roughly 700 million VVV light curves;
both were completed-data searches. The random-forest microlensing classifier of \citet{Godines2019} and neural networks on
ground-based photometry \citep{Mroz2020} address related classification tasks. KMTNet's completed-event finder \citep{Kim2018EventFinder} and the subsequent
\textsc{AnomalyFinder} analyses \citep{Zang2022}, which search residuals from fitted single-lens
models, demonstrate systematic event and anomaly discovery in real survey data. For simulated high-cadence \Rom{} light curves,
\citet{Khakpash2021} developed morphology and model-fit features intended for rapid classification.
In the alert-broker setting, Fink's LIA module
performs real-time microlensing classification but does not include a binary-anomaly class
\citep{Moller2021}. For \Rom{}, \citet{Zhang2021} demonstrated real-time posterior inference for a
known binary-lens event; that is downstream parameter inference, not discovery in the full stellar
stream. Most closely, \citet{CrispimRomao2025} evaluated an isolation-forest alarm on drip-fed
synthetic LSST light curves containing microlensing, binary lenses, and variables. Their online
stage separates signal from constant sources and their physical classification is offline; our
contribution is a supervised six-class \Rom{}-like model with time-dependent labels. Related
time-domain work also studies early classification of explosive transients
\citep{Muthukrishna2019}. Architecturally we use standard time-series transformer components
\citep{Astromer2023,Vaswani2017}, and our evaluation shares the prior-shift concern highlighted by
PLAsTiCC \citep{Hlozek2020}.

Across this work we find no trained classifier that combines all of the elements BinML needs:
multi-band \Rom{}-cadence input, a binary and planetary anomaly class evaluated alongside the
variable-star populations that contaminate it, operation on partial rather than completed light
curves, and released weights. The searches above are single-band, completed-data, or
binary-blind; the \Rom{}-specific work stops at feature definition or at downstream inference
for an already-identified event. To our knowledge BinML is the first released model to occupy
that combination, which is what the rest of this paper evaluates.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we introduce \emph{detectability-conditioned labelling}
(\S\ref{sec:sims}): events are labelled by whether they cross adopted synthetic thresholds. Second, we build a
\emph{partial-light-curve cascade} (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) by making each label a function of how much of the season
has been revealed. Third, we evaluate the model at synthetic survey scale (\S\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:limits}), with
completeness at a fixed purity as the headline metric, a reweighting that undoes our own training-set subsampling,
and stress tests on independent, targeted and out-of-range samples. Fourth, we test transfer to RMDC26, an
independent simulation of the survey made with a different simulator (\S\ref{sec:gulls}); it exposes a failure of
the released model on the survey's observing schedule, which a fine-tune with gap augmentation reduces.

\paragraph{Principal results.} In order of priority, the findings that survive our checks are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Complete seasons.} BinML reaches completeness $\bmlCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlPurity$ on detectable
anomalies. It ranks them nearly as well as a truth-informed reference and, on a common test set, well above a fitted
single-lens residual statistic (\S\ref{sec:results}). The purity holds only at the simulated anomaly prevalence, and
both numbers move with the adopted detectability floor (\S\ref{sec:limits}).
\item \emph{Labels.} Trained on generator intent instead of the policy-conditioned labels, the same network loses
$\bmlAbLabelGap$ in macro-F1 against those labels and flags anomalies at a purity of only $\bmlAbLabelGenPur$. Each
version wins only on the labels it was trained on (\S\ref{sec:ablations}).
\item \emph{Observing pauses.} The survey's F146 pauses break the released model, and gap augmentation removes most
of the failure. The collapse reproduces on our own seasons with only the pauses imposed, and the repair is many times
the spread between training seeds (\S\ref{sec:limits}, \S\ref{sec:gulls}).
\item \emph{Streaming.} At the operating threshold, alerts rarely precede the anomaly. Revealed in F146,
$\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of eligible binaries alert within the season and $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ before a truth-informed
onset ($\bmlCascPrematurePersistPct\%$ under a stricter onset). Premature alerts stay rare on RMDC26, although fewer
of its anomalies alert within the season (\S\ref{sec:cascade}, \S\ref{sec:gulls:cascade}).
\item \emph{Operating envelope.} Beyond the natural priors of its training set the released model fails in
identifiable regimes: very wide binaries, variables with season-length periods, sub-day single lenses and faint
sources, the last two by producing false anomalies (\S\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{enumerate}
Two results are conditional. The fine-tuned checkpoint's RMDC26 numbers are optimistic, because RMDC26 guided its
choice, and at a realistic prevalence its streaming alerts need a second stage that removes single-lens contaminants
(\S\ref{sec:gulls}). Four are not established: a causal timing benefit of the truncation augmentation, a lead of the
recommended recipe over its alternatives, anomaly information carried by the colour bands across simulators, and an
effect of the survey's 12-minute cadence (\S\ref{sec:limits}--\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Six classes across three bands}
We simulate \Rom{} GBTDS light curves in the six selected classes of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}.
\texttt{Flat} sources show no
detectable event. \texttt{PSPL} events are single-lens, point-source point-lens microlensing
\citep{Paczynski1986}. \texttt{NonPSPL} events---the anomalous, science-critical class---are
binary and planetary lenses, computed with the contour-integration code \textsc{VBBinaryLensing}
\citep{Bozza2010,Bozza2018}, and are distinguished by the caustic perturbations visible in the
third panel of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}. The remaining three classes are the variable-star
populations that dominate the real bulge and are the classifier's most dangerous contaminants:
\texttt{PeriodicVar} (RR~Lyrae, eclipsing binaries, $\delta$~Scuti), \texttt{LongPeriodVar}
(Miras, semiregulars, and OSARGs---small-amplitude red-giant pulsators---whose slow rise most
readily mimics a long-timescale microlensing event), and \texttt{Eruptive} (dwarf novae and Be
outbursts). We adopt an authored truncated log-normal event-timescale distribution whose mean is
anchored to an empirical inner-bulge estimate \citep{Mroz2019}. Binary mass ratio and projected
separation are instead drawn log-uniformly over broad analytic supports; they are not a fitted
population model \citep{Suzuki2016,Zhang2021}. Variable-star periods and amplitudes are motivated
by OGLE bulge catalogues, but the generated curves are phenomenological analytic waveform families
rather than sampled empirical templates \citep{Soszynski2013,Soszynski2014}. Every event is realised
on a hybrid \Rom{}-like schedule: legacy-like $\sim$15-minute wide-band sampling and a single
72-day season, combined with 6-hour visits in each of the current F087 and F213 auxiliary filters.
The curves are degraded by photometric noise, blending, and interstellar extinction. The released
model was trained on this legacy schedule (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

The source population is likewise parametric rather than a Galactic population synthesis. The
observed F146 baseline is uniform from 20 to 25~mag and $A_{K_s}$ is uniform from 0.2 to 1.2,
sampled independently. Baseline colour offsets come from a grey intrinsic source spectrum plus the
adopted extinction law; intrinsic stellar colour and its correlations with luminosity, variability,
and line of sight are not modelled. These choices give a classifier benchmark broad coverage.

The \texttt{NonPSPL} generator contains static binary lenses (2L1S) with finite-source
magnification, but not binary-source (1L2S) events, annual parallax, or lens orbital motion. The
1L2S omission is the most consequential of the three: a binary source produces a bump on an
otherwise single-lens curve that can mimic a binary-lens anomaly, and it is a long-recognised
degeneracy with planetary-mass 2L1S perturbations \citep{Gaudi1998}. It is therefore the dominant
astrophysical false positive that our purity estimate does not contain, and adding it would
lower the reported \texttt{NonPSPL} purity by an amount we have not measured. Annual parallax
and lens orbital motion distort the light-curve shape but do not manufacture a caustic-like
feature, so we expect them to matter less over a single 72-day season. The contaminant set
likewise omits many real irregular and correlated systematics.

\subsection{Labelling by an adopted synthetic detectability policy}\label{sec:detcond}
BinML's policy-conditioned synthetic labels are set by explicit thresholds applied to simulated,
noise-free curves, rather than by generator class alone; we use ``detectability-conditioned'' as
shorthand for this policy. A microlensing event whose in-window amplitude falls below the adopted
floor is labelled \texttt{Flat}. Likewise, a binary whose caustic perturbation falls below the
anomaly thresholds is labelled \texttt{PSPL}. For binaries we measure the departure of the
noiseless curve from a refitted single-lens model and withhold the anomalous label when that
departure is too small under the policy.

The class definitions---and therefore every metric in this paper---depend on these thresholds. An
event must reach $\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{event}} \ge 500$ against a flat baseline to be a
detected event at all; a binary must reach $\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{anom}} \ge 160$ against its
best-fitting single-lens model to keep the anomalous label; and both statistics must exceed a peak
amplitude of $0.02$~mag. Both $\Delta\chi^{2}$ values are computed on noise-free curves, so neither
is a likelihood-ratio statistic: under the null each is identically zero, and each grows in
proportion to the epoch count. At \Rom{}'s sampling that makes both $\chi^{2}$ cuts close to
vestigial for our error model and leaves the $0.02$~mag amplitude floor as the operative criterion. That floor is an
adopted simulation policy rather than a measured instrumental limit. Section~\ref{sec:ablations}
measures what the labelling scheme buys against the alternative of labelling by generator intent.

The scale of the relabelling is the point. Across the stored events of the final test set,
$\bmlRelabelled\%$ receive a policy-conditioned label that differs from the class they were generated as. The effect is
concentrated where it should be. Of the stored events generated as binary or planetary lenses, only
$\bmlNonpsplKept\%$ show a detectable anomaly and keep the anomalous label; $\bmlNonpsplToPspl\%$
have a caustic perturbation below those thresholds and are labelled as single lenses. (Those
single-lens by-products are stored only in part, \S\ref{sec:eval}; over all generated binaries the two
fractions are $\bmlNonpsplKeptW\%$ and $\bmlNonpsplToPsplW\%$.) Similarly,
$\bmlLpvToFlat\%$ of the long-period variables vary too weakly within a single season to be
detected and are labelled flat. Training against generator
intent asks the network to assign an anomalous or variable label on about a third of its examples
even though the corresponding signal falls below the adopted policy threshold.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{outputs/figures/schematic.pdf}
\caption{BinML architecture. Each of the three \Rom{} bands is binned onto a fixed grid whose
bins carry five channels---baseline-relative mean, min, and max magnitude, observed fraction, and
an observed mask. A convolutional stem, whose non-learned min/max lanes preserve caustic extrema
by construction, reduces the bands to $\bmlNtokens$ tokens that a four-layer transformer
classifies into six classes. The whole model has $\bmlNparams$ parameters.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure*}

BinML has two parts (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}). The first is a small convolutional network---a
stack of learned filters that scan along the light curve looking for local shapes, the same kind
of building block used in image recognition. The second is a transformer, a now-standard
architecture that lets every part of the light curve ``attend to'' every other part, so the model
can weigh, for example, a brief brightening against the overall trend around it. Together they
come to just $\bmlNparams$ trainable numbers, which by the standards of modern deep learning is
tiny. The small size is deliberate. We are not short of training data---we simulate millions of events
($\bmlTrainFinalEvents$ million in the final training stage alone)---so the pressure is not to build a larger model but to build one cheap enough to
run on the entire survey, night after night. A compact network that classifies a light curve in a
single pass is far more useful here than a heavy one applied only to a pre-selected handful of
candidates.

The model reads the light curve in a form that keeps the survey's own timing. Each of the three
filters is divided into a fixed set of time bins across the 72-day observing season: the workhorse
F146 filter into $\bmlBinsF$ bins (from $\bmlEpochsF$ individual exposures taken every
$\bmlCadence$~minutes), and the two colour filters into 96 bins each. For every bin the model is
given five simple numbers: the average brightness in that bin, the brightest and faintest points
in it, the fraction of the bin that was actually observed, and a flag saying whether it was
observed at all. All brightnesses are measured relative to a single quiet-state (baseline)
level defined in F146 and subtracted from all three filters. This removes absolute source
brightness while retaining the simulated inter-band offsets, including the effects of extinction
and blending, but not an independently measured intrinsic source colour.

The training and frozen evaluations use the simulator-provided F146 baseline. A deployed pipeline
would estimate that level from a partial, possibly magnified light curve; the released inference
interface includes an estimator, whose error we have not evaluated against the generator value.

Keeping the brightest and faintest point in each bin, rather than only the average, retains narrow
caustic structure during time binning. In the largest measured case, mean pooling
would reduce a nearly three-magnitude excursion to about a third of a magnitude. We therefore
propagate exact extrema through each strided reduction stage before a learned projection combines
the channels; the extrema are not supplied unchanged to the final classifier. When a colour filter
is missing for an event---the
bluer F087 filter yields no usable epoch for $\bmlNoFilterBlue\%$ of detectable-anomaly
events behind heavy dust---the model is simply told to
ignore it rather than being fed a guess, and such anomalies are still recovered at $\bmlSliceNoBlue$ (argmax recall). A final pooling step combines the processed bins into a
single summary, from which a standard classifier reads off the six class probabilities. The model
uses only the binned brightnesses; it does not use the individual error bars.

This compactness makes repeated survey-scale scoring practical. Classification is a single forward
pass with no per-event fitting. On a $\bmlInferCores$-core \bmlInferCpu{} CPU with
$\bmlInferThreads$ threads and otherwise idle, the model scores $\bmlInferEps$ pre-tokenised light curves per second
($\bmlInferMs$~ms per event) in batches of 1024; the rate falls when other work loads the machine.
\code{validation/inference\_benchmark.py} regenerates this together with the processor, thread count, load average
and timing protocol, and replaces the figure of an earlier revision, whose hardware and script were not retained.

\section{Partial-light-curve evaluation}\label{sec:cascade}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]

not the full three-state sequence.}
\label{fig:cascade}
\end{figure*}

A streaming classifier receives a partial light curve. Before its anomaly appears a binary lens is photometrically
a single lens, so assigning the complete-season class to every prefix would teach the network a target the revealed
window does not support. We therefore make the label a function of observation time. For each binary we compute the
day $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ at which its anomaly first crosses the adopted thresholds, from the noise-free light curve
and a single-lens fit seeded at the injected parameters. During training the network sees light curves truncated at
random days: a binary truncated before $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ carries the \texttt{PSPL} label, with the detectability
floor re-evaluated on the truncated data, and earns \texttt{NonPSPL} once the anomaly lies inside the revealed
window. The training labels resolve $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ only to the end of the $7.2$-day interval in which the
anomaly became detectable, a conservative bias that usually delays the label (the evaluation onsets below are
resolved at $0.5$~days). The prefix labels of the other classes are also generator-informed: an eruptive source
changes class at its injected start time, and periodic variability uses an analytic amplitude--period proxy rather
than the realised waveform. Measured against the label rule itself on one training shard, the released training labels differ from it for
$\bmlTruthTruncNonLegacy\%$ of truncated binary presentations, about half of them still \texttt{PSPL} after the
anomaly had become detectable, and for up to $\bmlTruthTruncLpv\%$ of the other classes' presentations (long-period
variables; Table~\ref{tab:labelfid}). The released model is trained with these approximate labels.

Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} shows the cascade on individual events, and Figure~\ref{fig:probconf} extends it to a clear
and a marginal event of every class, each re-observed 30 times with fresh photometric noise: the clear events settle
once their evidence appears, whereas the marginal ones wander as the season is revealed, and for most of them the
noise draw alone moves the end-of-season call by tens of percentage points; the flat source stays near 1.

\paragraph{Protocol.} An \emph{eligible event} is a simulated binary whose anomaly survives
detectability-conditioned labelling and has a finite onset; eligibility never depends on the model. For each of
$\bmlCascN$ eligible binaries we reveal the season in half-day steps, scoring F146 alone, and record the
\emph{alert}, the first cut at which $P(\texttt{NonPSPL})$ reaches the operating threshold; an event with no alert
is right-censored. An alert is \emph{premature} if it precedes the onset, and the \emph{lag} is alert time minus
onset time. The onset is ambiguous, because the anomaly statistic is not monotone in revealed time: as more of the
season arrives the best single-lens fit can absorb an early deviation, which happens for $\bmlCascNonMonotone$ of
the $\bmlCascN$ events. We therefore bracket it, between the \emph{first-detectable} cut and the cut after which
the anomaly stays detectable to the end of the season (\emph{persistently detectable}), and report both; the
generator's own $7.2$-day grid rounds the onset upward by a median of $\bmlCascOnsetShift$~days and counts on-time
alerts as premature.

\paragraph{Results.} BinML alerts on $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of eligible binaries within the season. The alert precedes
the onset for $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ of eligible events (95\% CI $[\bmlCascPrematureLo,\bmlCascPrematureHi]\%$)
under the first-detectable onset and for $\bmlCascPrematurePersistPct\%$ under the persistent one, and the other
alerts come a median of $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$ or $\bmlCascMedianLagPersist$~days after it
(Figure~\ref{fig:cascade_summary}). The timing depends strongly on the alert policy (Table~\ref{tab:policy}).
Coarser update grids, consecutive crossings and all three bands each lower the premature rate further and delay the
alerts, and consecutive crossings also cost detection. Alerting on the argmax class instead of the threshold makes
detection near-complete but premature for $\bmlCascArgmaxPct\%$ of eligible binaries. Whether the truncation

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:eval}

Three choices define what the synthetic benchmark measures.

The first is how we summarise performance. A single accuracy figure is misleading here, because
the anomalous events are so rare that a model could score well simply by never flagging one. What
a follow-up team actually needs to know is a pair of numbers. One is \emph{purity}: of the events
the model flags as anomalous, what fraction really are. The other is \emph{completeness}: of the
anomalous events that exist, what fraction the model catches. These are precisely the quantities
tabulated as precision and recall in Table~\ref{tab:perclass}, and their harmonic mean is the F1
score there (macro-F1 being its unweighted average over the six classes). These trade off against each
other---flag more events and you catch more real ones but also let in more false ones---so we fix
the purity to a chosen value, $\bmlPurityTarget$, and report the completeness reached at that
setting as our headline number. This is exactly the form in which a survey team would state a
requirement: ``keep the flagged anomaly sample at least ninety per cent pure, and report how many
detectable anomalies remain.'' This is not the same as planet completeness because
\texttt{NonPSPL} also contains stellar binaries.

The second is a partial correction for how the training set was built. To train efficiently we
subsample the abundant demoted-binary by-products, and each test event carries a weight that
reverses \emph{that} subsampling, applied to purity (which depends on the mix of false positives)
but not to completeness (a per-class property). The weighting undoes one internal sampling step; it
does \emph{not} reweight the deliberately chosen generator class quotas to a survey event-rate
prior.

The third is a reference to judge the anomaly ranking against. For each event we can compute, from
the noiseless light curve and the true parameters, its injected anomaly \dchisq{}---in effect how
detectable the anomaly would be to an observer who already knew the answer. This is a
\emph{truth-informed reference}, not an oracle upper bound: it uses information the survey never
has, and a learned model exploiting other morphology could in principle rank better on real, noisy
data. Comparing BinML against it measures how much of the injected
anomaly ordering the model reconstructs from the raw measurements alone.

\section{Generalisation and limits}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Stress tests.} Held-out numbers can reflect memorised training examples, so we also score a
$\bmlStressN$-million-event stress set drawn from a disjoint pseudo-random stream: a natural-population subset,
targeted regimes and out-of-range sweeps that push one parameter beyond the natural priors. The set was scored with
the released model's predecessor; for the released model we regenerated and scored a subset (Table~\ref{tab:stress}).
On the
natural population the released model reproduces its held-out performance (macro-F1 $\bmlStressRelMacroF$ against
$\bmlMacroF$). Its training was not blind to the targeted regimes, so for it these numbers measure how far that
coverage carries.

The out-of-range sweeps expose four failures. Anomaly recall falls to $\bmlStressRelWidesep$ for very wide
separations ($s = 5$--$12$), and periodic variables with periods comparable to or longer than the season are
recovered only $\bmlStressRelLongp$ of the time, since within one window they are not observably periodic. Two
failures manufacture spurious anomalies, the mode that matters most for follow-up: $\bmlStressRelShortteSingleNp\%$ of
detectable sub-day single lenses are called anomalies, and among faint microlensing events ($m = 25$--$27.5$) without a
detectable anomaly the false-anomaly rate rises from $\bmlStressRelNatMlFa\%$ to $\bmlStressRelFaintMlFa\%$. Anomaly
precision on the faint events collapses, mostly because of the faint photometry rather than the sweep's class mix.
Each failure has a plausible physical cause (too few epochs on the peak, a signal that does not complete within the
window, noise comparable to the signal); they are the current model's operating envelope, not established limits of
the method.

\paragraph{Cadence.} BinML is a specialist for dense, nearly continuous space-based sampling; archival ground-survey
light curves are outside the evaluated regime. A matched-density test isolates the gap topology of a ground network
such as KMTNet \citep{Kim2016}: at the same visit counts, anomaly recall through an eight-hour nightly window is zero
where uniform sampling still recovers part of it, and a gap-free regular grid keeps working where random uniform
sampling fails (Table~\ref{tab:density}). Empty two-hour bins are therefore what makes the released model fail
outright, although thinning alone also costs recall and the arrangement of the visits matters beyond empty bins. Our
schedule is a legacy \Rom{}-like design; the current survey specifies F146 every 12 minutes, staggered colour visits, 66-second exposures and six high-cadence
plus four low-cadence seasons \citep{STScI2025GBTDS,ROTAC2025}. Retraining the whole pipeline at the 12-minute rate
makes no difference we can resolve with one seed per arm (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). The exposure time is not tested,
and the multi-season structure cannot be represented by a single-season input.

\paragraph{The schedule is what the released model cannot survive.} The survey as simulated in RMDC26
\citep{RGESPIT2026} pauses F146 for $\bmlGullsPauseHoursLo$--$\bmlGullsPauseHoursHi$~h per $\bmlGullsSeasonDays$-day
season, in six or seven pauses whose phases differ between seasons. Essentially none of our training events contains
an empty mid-season F146 bin. Imposing the measured pauses on our own held-out seasons, with nothing else changed,
collapses the released model (Table~\ref{tab:gap}): single-lens recall falls from $\bmlGapShippedPsplClean$ to
$\bmlGapShippedPsplSched$, mostly to \texttt{NonPSPL}, and \texttt{Flat} recall from $\bmlGapShippedFlatClean$ to
$\bmlGapShippedFlatSched$. The remedy is an augmentation that blanks random runs of 1--12~h during a 12-epoch
warm-start fine-tune. It raises held-out average precision under the pauses from $\bmlGapApShipped$ to
$\bmlGapApRandNorelabel$ at a small clean-data cost, and training on RMDC26's measured seasons instead gives
$\bmlGapApSeasons$. On our seasons the two are within the spread of three training seeds ($\bmlSeedHeldApRange$); on
RMDC26 the measured pauses lead in recall (\S\ref{sec:gulls}). The released checkpoint's input contract is therefore
\emph{continuous F146}; for a gapped schedule use the recommended checkpoint of \S\ref{sec:gulls}.

\paragraph{Prevalence.} Purity depends on the prevalence of anomalies in the input stream, which our mixture sets at
$\bmlPriorPiZero\%$ and a real survey stream sets far lower. Under a pure prior shift, completeness at the fixed
threshold stays at $\bmlCompleteness$ while purity falls to $\bmlPurityOnePct$ at 1\% prevalence and
$\bmlPurityTenthPct$ at 0.1\% (Figure~\ref{fig:prior}); re-tuning the threshold to hold the purity costs completeness
instead (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). A deployment should plan against the curve for its own prevalence; this transfer
assumes events that look like ours in different proportions, not a different survey.

\paragraph{Colour calibration and the detectability floor.} An audit against the current Roman tables
\citep{RomanTechInfo} finds our F087 and F213 zeropoints optimistic by $0.10$--$0.14$~mag, the F087 saturation limit
too faint and the thermal backgrounds not reproduced.
Regenerating two held-out shards with the audited colour bands and scoring them with the released model barely moves
the anomaly channel, while the variable classes lose precision, not recall (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Short fine-tunes
show that the flat-versus-periodic boundary follows the calibration a model is trained on, by an amount that depends
on where training stops, while the anomaly channel does not.
The class boundaries also depend on the $0.02$~mag detectability floor, a stand-in for correlated photometric
systematics. Regenerated with the floor at $0.01$ or $0.05$~mag and scored at the frozen threshold, the same shards
leave the anomaly ranking largely intact but move the operating point: a lower floor raises the anomaly prevalence and the purity
and lowers the completeness, and a higher floor does the reverse. The completeness and purity we quote are conditional
on the $0.02$~mag floor; a survey requirement should rest on a model retrained at the floor its photometry supports.

\section{Cross-simulator validation on an independent \Rom{} simulation}\label{sec:gulls}

Every number so far comes from our own simulator. RMDC26 \citep{RGESPIT2026} simulates the survey with GULLS
\citep{Penny2013,Penny2019} and the SynthPop Galactic model \citep{Kluter2025}, with its own schedule, photometry and
population. No RMDC26 event entered training and no deployed threshold was chosen on it, but it was used to diagnose
the residual false alarms, to set the upper end of the single-lens source-size prior and to choose among
$\bmlGullsNcheckpoints$ fine-tuned checkpoints, so the recommended checkpoint's numbers are the best of several on this
set and are optimistic. Table~\ref{tab:rmdcchecks} collects the second-order checks of this section.

\subsection{Data, selection, and input contract}

RMDC26 contains $\bmlGullsNcatSingle$ single lenses (1S1L), $\bmlGullsNcatPlanet$ planetary lenses (1S2L) and
$\bmlGullsNcatPlanetBin$ planetary lenses with a second source (2S2L), the planetary classes pre-selected by GULLS' own
detection statistic. Two differences limit what it can test. It has no pure binary-source class, so the
\citet{Gaudi1998} degeneracy remains untested, and the source is flagged binary in $\bmlGullsBinSrcPct\%$ of scored
2S2L events, so a \texttt{NonPSPL} call on one of them may respond to its second source rather than its planet. And
its anomalies are planetary (median mass ratio $\bmlGullsQmedPlanet$), whereas $\bmlDetStellarPct\%$ of our
detectable anomalies are stellar-mass-ratio binaries, so transfer recall is not comparable to the completeness of
\S\ref{sec:results}.

Selection uses catalogue parameters only and is applied identically to the three classes: a host single-lens peak
amplitude of at least $0.1$~mag and $1 \le t_{\rm E} \le 300$~d, our natural prior's support. Of the
$\bmlGullsEligibleN$ events that pass, the $\bmlGullsN$ that peak inside one of the six high-cadence seasons are
scored ($\bmlGullsNSingle$ 1S1L, $\bmlGullsNPlanet$ 1S2L, $\bmlGullsNPlanetBin$ 2S2L); $\bmlGullsBetweenPct\%$ peak
between seasons (\S\ref{sec:gulls:seasons}) and most of the rest in low-cadence seasons, outside our support. The host
amplitude cut favours resonant and central-caustic planetary geometries. Some inputs lie outside our training priors, notably brighter baselines, lower blend fractions and much
shorter single lenses. The model sees the 72-day window from the season boundary in all three bands, pooled to 15-minute
epochs as in training, with the baseline taken as the median F146 magnitude far from the catalogue peak; a real-time
pipeline would know neither the boundary nor the peak.

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:gulls:results}

Table~\ref{tab:gulls} compares checkpoints on identical events by two measures: the single-lens false-alarm rate at
the frozen threshold, and planetary recall at matched single-lens false-alarm budgets, which a threshold shift cannot
fake. Rates are per simulated event unless weighted by RMDC26's event rate, which matters because the
unweighted sample over-represents short single lenses, whose false-alarm rate is lower. Event-bootstrap intervals do
not cover training variation, so we read a difference between single runs as unresolved when it is smaller than the
spread of three training seeds of the recommended recipe (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}). The checkpoints form a ladder:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The shipped checkpoint flags $\bmlGullsFaShipped\%$ of single lenses, its first choice split between
\texttt{NonPSPL} and \texttt{PeriodicVar}, the signature of the empty mid-season bins (\S\ref{sec:limits}).
\item Gap augmentation brings the rate to $\bmlGullsFaGapaware\%$. Its residual false alarms rise steeply with
$\rho/|u_0|$, the ratio of source radius to impact parameter (Table~\ref{tab:rho}); these are high-magnification
events, and the likely cause is that our generator's single lenses were point sources.
\item Twelve further epochs with finite-source single lenses \citep{Bozza2018} lower the rate to $\bmlGullsFaFspl\%$
and raise planetary recall at the $\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ budget from $\bmlGullsRecGapaware$ to $\bmlGullsRecFspl$. A
control with the same pool, recipe and epochs but point-source single lenses shows that the extra training carries
most of the false-alarm gain and the low-budget recall gain. The physics adds the recall gain at high budgets and
fewer false alarms in the two largest $\rho/|u_0|$ bins, per simulated event only. Even so, false alarms in the
largest-ratio bin stay near $\bmlGullsRhoHiFspl$, so the missing physics explains only part of the residual.
\item On one pool, training on the measured pauses instead of random gaps raises recall at the budget in both
weightings, leading in $\bmlGullsSeasonWins$ of the $\bmlGullsNseasons$ seasons, and lowers false alarms per

\subsection{What ``recall'' means across two label ontologies}\label{sec:gulls:labels}

GULLS assigns classes by generator; BinML's are observational (\S\ref{sec:detcond}). Applied to RMDC26's noise-free
light curves, with GULLS' errors as $\sigma$, our labelling rule finds no anomaly the policy would claim within one
season in $\bmlGullsUndetPlanet\%$ of 1S2L and $\bmlGullsUndetPlanetBin\%$ of 2S2L events. This depends on the floor,
the window and our selection; it is not a statement about what a survey could detect. On the planetary events with a
claimable anomaly the recommended checkpoint's recall at the frozen threshold is $\bmlGullsRecDetPlanet$ /
$\bmlGullsRecDetPlanetBin$, against $\bmlGullsRecGenPlanet$ / $\bmlGullsRecGenPlanetBin$ on generator labels. It also
flags $\bmlGullsFlagFloorVeto\%$ of the planetary events whose anomaly is significant but below the $0.02$~mag floor,
and $\bmlGullsSingleMisfitK$ of the $\bmlGullsSingleMisfitN$ single lenses whose static refit shows a significant
sub-floor misfit, so its response to sub-floor structure is not specific to planets.

\subsection{Events whose peak falls between seasons}\label{sec:gulls:seasons}

The single-season contract leaves out the $\bmlGullsBetweenPct\%$ of eligible events that peak between seasons. For
the first $\bmlMsN$ such events of each class we scored the high-cadence season on each side of the gap and kept the
larger anomaly probability. A claimable anomaly is visible in a wing for $\bmlMsDetPlanet\%$ of the scorable 1S2L
events, against $\bmlGullsDetPlanet\%$ in season, so the unobserved gap costs $\bmlMsDetLossPlanet$ points of claimable
anomalies. On the events with one, recall at the frozen threshold is lower than in season but comparable at a matched
single-lens budget, so scoring seasons around a gap would need its own operating point. No model recovers anomalies that fall in the
unobserved gaps.

\subsection{The partial-season cascade on RMDC26}\label{sec:gulls:cascade}

We repeated the primary protocol of \S\ref{sec:cascade} on RMDC26 with the recommended checkpoint at the frozen
threshold, with the onset taken from our labelling rule on GULLS' noise-free curve (Table~\ref{tab:cgstrata}). Of the $\bmlCgElig$
eligible binaries, $\bmlCgPrem\%$ are flagged before the onset (95\% interval $\bmlCgPremLo$--$\bmlCgPremHi\%$),
$\bmlCgDet\%$ by the end of the season, and the median lag is $\bmlCgLag$~d. Premature alerts are about as rare as in
the in-house scan of \S\ref{sec:cascade}, but detection is lower. $\bmlCgRmLowmassFrac\%$ of RMDC26's eligible
anomalies have $q \le 10^{-4}$, a regime the in-house scan barely samples, and detection there is
$\bmlCgRmLowmassDet\%$. In the two mass-ratio strata both scans sample, detection is also lower and alerts come later
on RMDC26; these differences are at most nominally resolved, and they mix a different checkpoint with a different
simulator.

Alerts on single lenses set the burden. $\bmlCgSingleAlert\%$ of them raise an alert at some point in the season, a
slightly optimistic figure that is several times the rate on our own simulator with the same checkpoint. If 1\% of
events were planetary binaries, only $\bmlCgPurOne\%$ of alerts would come from a claimable anomaly ($\bmlCgPurFive\%$
at 5\%), and RMDC26 contains no variables or flat sources, so a real stream would be less pure. The low premature-alert
rate holds on another simulator, but at realistic prevalence the alerts need a second stage that removes single-lens
contaminants.

\subsection{What this test does and does not establish}

It establishes that the shipped model's failure on a gapped schedule is a property of the schedule, reproducible
without GULLS data and reduced by augmentation, preferably with the measured pauses; that continued training on a
pool rich in high-magnification single lenses accounts for most of the further reduction in false alarms; and that
finite-source single lenses lower false alarms further at large $\rho/|u_0|$ and raise recall at high budgets, per
simulated event. It does not establish a \Rom{} planet yield: RMDC26's population, detection cut and our selection all
differ from our training population, neither simulator contains real systematics, every fine-tune but the recommended
recipe is a single seed, and RMDC26 was used to choose among checkpoints. Nor does it show that the colour bands carry
anomaly information across simulators: removing them lowers planetary recall, but for the recommended checkpoint mostly
on events without a claimable anomaly. Sub-day single lenses, outside our timescale prior, are mostly
classified \texttt{PeriodicVar}; a model for free-floating planets needs sub-day timescales in its prior.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

BinML is intended as a cheap first-pass triage layer before binary-lens modelling: the completeness-at-fixed-purity
framing exposes the trade-off a survey team must set, and the forward pass is fast enough for repeated survey-scale
scoring. The evaluation uses synthetic photometry, a simulator-provided baseline, a truth-informed onset and half-day
updates, so this is a computational benchmark rather than a deployable broker. An alert several days after the onset
arrives once a short caustic crossing has finished, so for brief planetary anomalies BinML is a post-hoc identifier
rather than a trigger for the feature itself. That latency still serves anomalies that are not brief, and everything
that depends on recognising the event rather than the feature: scheduling denser photometry for the rest of an ongoing
event, initiating colour or parallax campaigns, and prioritising which light curves receive expensive modelling.
Triggering on the anomaly itself would require evaluating at the native cadence, which our grid sensitivity
(\S\ref{sec:cascade}) does not cover. BinML's input is one contiguous season, which leaves out the events that peak
between seasons or in low-cadence seasons (\S\ref{sec:gulls:seasons}); a whole-mission model taking the full light
curve is the natural next version.

\subsection{Ablating the two mechanisms}\label{sec:ablations}

\paragraph{Detectability-conditioned labelling.} With the training label the only intervention (both arms use the same
policy-conditioned class weights), the arm trained on generator intent loses $\bmlAbLabelGap$ in macro-F1 against the
policy-conditioned label (95\% CI $[\bmlAbLabelGapLo,\bmlAbLabelGapHi]$, paired bootstrap over events). Against the
generator label the ordering reverses, so each arm wins on the ontology it was trained for. The damage concentrates
where the relabelling acts, in \texttt{PSPL} and \texttt{NonPSPL}, and at the frozen threshold the generator-intent arm
flags anomalies at a purity of only $\bmlAbLabelGenPur$, because it was trained to call anomalous the sub-threshold
binaries that make up $\bmlGenNpDemoted\%$ of the stored events of the final-test split.

\paragraph{The truncation augmentation: unresolved.} The second ablation does not identify a timing benefit. Counted
against the generator's onset grid, removing the augmentation raises the premature rate at the frozen threshold but
lowers it under argmax. Almost all of the augmented arm's premature alerts, however, fall inside the window the grid
cannot resolve, so the argmax reversal is not established, and at the threshold the augmented arm alerts far less
often, so its lower premature rate does not show a benefit either. The augmentation is also expensive, lowering
detection and static macro-F1 at the same threshold, consistent with the augmented model saying \texttt{NonPSPL} less
often. An exploratory risk--coverage comparison on stored traces (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade_matched}), thresholding each
arm to the same detection fraction, gives the augmented arm fewer premature alerts from $0.50$ through $0.90$
detection ($\bmlMatchOnSixty\%$ against $\bmlMatchOffSixty\%$ at $0.60$), reversing at $0.95$; because thresholds and
outcomes use the same events and one seed per arm, it is descriptive, not confirmatory. A causal timing claim needs
thresholds selected on disjoint prefixes, held-out paired evaluation and repeated seeds.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

BinML is a compact multi-band classifier for progressively revealed, synthetic \Rom{}-like light curves, trained on
labels that follow an adopted detectability policy and the revealed part of the season. Its strongest results, ranked
at the end of \S\ref{sec:intro}, are its anomaly recovery on complete seasons, the need for policy-conditioned labels,
the finding that the survey's observing pauses cause most of its failure on RMDC26 and that gap augmentation
removes most of that failure, and the rarity of premature streaming alerts. All are conditional on the simulation, its detectability floor and its
anomaly prevalence, and none is a planet yield. The remaining tests are retraining under another detectability floor,
an estimated-baseline ablation, repeated training seeds for the released model, a streaming threshold calibrated on
disjoint prefixes, a whole-mission model, the binary-source degeneracy, sub-day timescales in the prior, and validation
on real photometry. The released code and weights provide a rebuildable benchmark.

\section{Simulation and training infrastructure}\label{app:infra}

The simulated data were generated on Amazon Web Services in the \texttt{\bmlSimRegion} region, mostly on
transient (Spot) instances of $\bmlSimVcpus$ virtual CPUs; the generator is embarrassingly parallel, one process
per self-contained shard seeded by its index, so a lost shard costs only itself. The same code, with the seed
bases given in Table~\ref{tab:stress}, produced the stress set. The fleets' software environment was not pinned, so a shard
regenerated elsewhere is close to, but not identical with, the original. We did not retain throughput or billing
records for these runs.

The released checkpoint is the last of $\bmlTrainStages$ stages, five of them warm-started from the previous one,
each keeping its epoch with the highest validation \texttt{NonPSPL} F1. Every stage used AdamW (weight decay
$0.01$), a one-cycle schedule (15\% warm-up, cosine decay) and cross-entropy with class-balanced weights (the
selection weights of \S\ref{sec:eval}, rescaled so that every class carries the same total weight). The base model
trained from scratch on $\bmlTrainBaseEvents$ natural-prior events (peak learning rate $4\times10^{-4}$, batch size
512, factor 2 on the anomaly class); stage 2 dropped that factor ($1.2\times10^{-4}$); stage 3 added the truncation
augmentation on half of all examples ($3\times10^{-5}$); stage 4 added enriched pools of 15 targeted regimes,
among them the low-$q$ and faint-source ($m = 23.5$--$25$) regimes ($5\times10^{-5}$, batch size 384 from here on);
stage 5 applied the detectability floor to truncated periodic classes; and stage 6 relabelled truncated binaries
as \texttt{PSPL} until their recorded onset and added pools of separations $s = 3$--$8$, timescales
$t_{\rm E} = 0.3$--$10$~d and further faint sources, $\bmlTrainFinalEvents$ million events in all, for
$\bmlTrainEpochs$ epochs; the released checkpoint is its zero-based epoch 4. Seeds are fixed ($\bmlSeedTrain$).
The fine-tunes of \S\ref{sec:limits} and \S\ref{sec:gulls} warm-start from the released weights or the
gap-augmented checkpoint with batch size 256 and the factor 2 on the anomaly class, at peak learning rates of
$5\times10^{-5}$ (colour calibration) and $10^{-4}$ (RMDC26). Every stage was trained on a personal computer
(\bmlTrainDevice) at $\bmlTrainEpsLo$--$\bmlTrainEpsHi$ training events per second including data loading,
augmentation and the validation pass; the $\bmlTrainStages$ stages took about $\bmlTrainHours$~hours of wall-clock,
about $\bmlTrainHoursTypical$ with a typical epoch in place of one stretched by a system sleep (which the throughput
range excludes). The compactness of the network is what keeps
training and survey-scale inference within reach of a single workstation.
