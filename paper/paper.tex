\begin{abstract}
The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will monitor hundreds of millions of stars in the
Galactic bulge at $\sim$15-minute cadence, and among them it will catch the brief brightenings
of gravitational microlensing---including the rare events in which a planet betrays itself
through a short, sharp anomaly riding on an otherwise smooth light curve. Finding those events in
a data stream far too large to inspect by hand, and flagging each one the moment its evidence
appears rather than after the season has ended, is as much a problem of survey logistics as of
astrophysics. We present \textbf{BinML}, a compact ($\bmlNparams$-parameter) neural network that
sorts \Rom{} light curves into six classes: sources with no detectable event, ordinary
single-lens microlensing, the anomalous binary and planetary lenses that carry the planet signal,
and three kinds of variable star that would otherwise contaminate the sample. Two ideas make
BinML suited to a real survey rather than a fixed benchmark. First, each event is labelled by
what can actually be seen in its photometry, not by the parameters used to simulate it, so the
network is never trained to claim an anomaly that leaves no observable trace. Second, the model
reports each class only once that class's evidence has arrived, so a binary is announced when its
anomaly appears and not during the smooth rise that precedes it; this cuts premature planet
alerts from $\bmlPrematureBefore\%$ to $\bmlPrematureAfter\%$. On an independent test set of
$\bmlNtest$ events, BinML recovers $\bmlCompletenessPct\%$ of the anomalous events while holding
the flagged sample to a purity of $\bmlPurityTarget$, and it reproduces this performance on a
$\bmlStressN$-million-event test drawn from parameters it never saw. The model and its full
simulation-to-inference pipeline are released as open source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gravitational microlensing occupies a niche no other planet-detection method reaches. Because it
responds to a lens's mass rather than its light, it is sensitive to cold, low-mass planets far
from their host stars and to unbound planetary-mass objects drifting through the Galaxy---the very
population that transit and radial-velocity surveys, biased toward hot and close-in planets,
recover least well \citep{Mao1991,GouldLoeb1992,Suzuki2016}. The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will exploit
this sensitivity systematically. Its Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey (GBTDS) will monitor the
crowded bulge fields at high cadence in the near-infrared, and is predicted to detect of order a
thousand bound planets and to pin down the free-floating planet mass function
\citep{Spergel2015,Penny2019,Johnson2020,Sumi2023,Gaudi2022,Wilson2023}. The wide F146 filter samples the light curve every
$\sim\bmlCadence$~minutes; two auxiliary bands, F087 and F213, add colour information on a
$\sim$6-hour cycle.

That sensitivity is inseparable from two operational problems. The first is sheer volume: the
survey will yield hundreds of millions of light curves, within which genuine microlensing events
are a small minority and anomalous---binary or planetary---events rarer still. No team can
inspect them by hand, and the definitive step for any candidate, fitting a full binary-lens
model, is far too expensive to run on the whole stream. The second problem is timing. The
signal that betrays a planet is short: a caustic crossing lasting hours to a day, riding on top
of a smooth magnification that itself unfolds over weeks. A classifier meant to \emph{trigger}
follow-up must therefore be honest about time---it must not announce a binary during the smooth
rise, before the caustic that would justify the alert has actually been observed. A model that
guesses early is worse than useless: it spends a finite follow-up budget on planets that have
not yet revealed themselves, and may never. The need for real-time response is not new to
microlensing: rapid-response anomaly-detection and alert systems have driven planet follow-up for
close to two decades \citep{Dominik2007,Dominik2008,Tsapras2009}. Those systems, however, sit
\emph{downstream} of detection, reacting to deviations once an event is already known; the
scale and cadence of \Rom{} instead demand that the real-time decision be made inside the
classifier that scans the raw stream.

Machine learning has been applied to microlensing classification before, and the trajectory is
instructive. Feature-engineered classifiers such as \textsc{MicroLIA} \citep{Godines2019} and
neural networks trained on lower-cadence ground-based data \citep{Mroz2020} established that the
problem is learnable on OGLE- and ZTF-like photometry. Work aimed specifically at high-cadence,
\Rom{}-like microlensing sampling \citep{Khakpash2021}, together with deep-learning classifiers
built to flag transients in real time more broadly \citep{Muthukrishna2019}, are the closest
antecedents of the present work. Architecturally we build on the now-standard use of
transformers for astronomical time series \citep{Astromer2023,Vaswani2017}. What distinguishes
BinML, however, is not the network but the way the problem is posed: we treat detectability and
observation time as first-class parts of the label definition rather than as afterthoughts of
evaluation.

This paper makes three contributions, developed in the sections that follow. First, we introduce
\emph{detectability-conditioned labelling} (\S\ref{sec:sims}): events are labelled by the
evidence a perfect observer could recover, which removes the label noise that would otherwise
punish the model for failing to see what is physically unobservable. Second, we build a
\emph{real-time cascade} (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) by making each label a function of how much of the
season has been revealed, so the network learns to report classes in the order their evidence
arrives. Third, we evaluate the model honestly at survey scale
(\S\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:limits}): completeness at a fixed purity as the headline metric, a
selection-aware reweighting that corrects for the training distribution, and a
$\bmlStressN$-million-event stress test on parameters the model never saw.

\subsection{Six classes across three bands}
We simulate \Rom{} GBTDS light curves in the six classes of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}, chosen to
span the populations a real triage pipeline must separate. \texttt{Flat} sources show no
detectable event. \texttt{PSPL} events are single-lens, point-source point-lens microlensing
\citep{Paczynski1986}. \texttt{NonPSPL} events---the anomalous, science-critical class---are
binary and planetary lenses, computed with the contour-integration code \textsc{VBBinaryLensing}
\citep{Bozza2010,Bozza2018}, and are distinguished by the caustic perturbations visible in the
third panel of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}. The remaining three classes are the variable-star
populations that dominate the real bulge and are the classifier's most dangerous contaminants:
\texttt{PeriodicVar} (RR~Lyrae, eclipsing binaries, $\delta$~Scuti), \texttt{LongPeriodVar}
(Miras, semiregulars, and OSARGs, whose slow rise most readily mimics a long-timescale
microlensing event), and \texttt{Eruptive} (dwarf novae and Be outbursts). Event-timescale,
mass-ratio, and separation priors follow the measured bulge distributions
\citep{Mroz2019,Suzuki2016}; the variable-star templates follow the OGLE bulge catalogues
\citep{Soszynski2013,Soszynski2017}. Every event is realised on the true \Rom{} cadence in all
three bands and degraded by survey-realistic photometric noise, blending, and interstellar
extinction, so that the training distribution matches what the telescope will actually deliver.

\subsection{Labelling by what is observable}\label{sec:detcond}
The methodological core of BinML is a single principle: \emph{an event's label is set by what a
perfect observer could recover from the photometry, not by the parameters we injected}. The
principle has teeth at both boundaries the classifier cares about. A microlensing event whose
peak falls outside the 72-day season, or whose amplitude is buried beneath the noise, is labelled
\texttt{Flat}, because no algorithm and no human modeller could distinguish it from a constant
source; training the network to call it \texttt{PSPL} would only teach it to guess. Likewise, a
binary whose caustic perturbation lies everywhere below the noise floor is, observationally, a
single lens, and is labelled \texttt{PSPL}. In practice we measure how far each binary's true,
noiseless light curve departs from the best single-lens description of it; if that departure is
too small to rise above the noise, the event is not given the anomalous label, whatever generated
it. This does two useful things at once. It cleans up the boundaries between classes, where most
labelling mistakes would otherwise happen, and it keeps our later purity figures meaningful: an
anomaly the model appears to ``miss'' under this scheme is one that carried no detectable evidence
to begin with. We return to what this means for the numbers in \S\ref{sec:results}.

The scale of this relabelling is not a detail but the point. Across the test set,
$\bmlRelabelled\%$ of events receive an observational label that differs from the class they were
generated as---that is, more than a third of all events would carry a demonstrably misleading
label under the naive scheme of labelling by generator intent. The effect is concentrated exactly
where it should be: of the events generated as binary or planetary lenses, only $\bmlNonpsplKept\%$
show a detectable anomaly and keep the anomalous label, while $\bmlNonpsplToPspl\%$ have a caustic
perturbation buried below the noise and are, correctly, indistinguishable from single lenses;
similarly, $\bmlLpvToFlat\%$ of the long-period variables vary too weakly within a single season
to be detected and are labelled flat. Training a network against generator intent would therefore
ask it to assert, on more than a third of its examples, evidence that is physically absent---the
definition of label noise. Conditioning the labels on detectability removes it at the source. A
full ablation that retrains the network on generator-intent labels and measures the resulting
degradation is the natural next experiment, and we flag it as such; here we establish the size of
the mislabelling the scheme corrects.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{outputs/figures/schematic.pdf}
\caption{BinML architecture. Each of the three \Rom{} bands is binned onto a fixed grid whose
bins carry five channels---baseline-relative mean, min, and max magnitude, observed fraction, and
an observed mask. A convolutional stem, whose non-learned min/max lanes preserve caustic extrema
by construction, reduces the bands to $\bmlNtokens$ tokens that a four-layer transformer
classifies into six classes. The whole model has $\bmlNparams$ parameters.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure*}

BinML has two parts (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}). The first is a small convolutional network---a
stack of learned filters that scan along the light curve looking for local shapes, the same kind
of building block used in image recognition. The second is a transformer, a now-standard
architecture that lets every part of the light curve ``attend to'' every other part, so the model
can weigh, for example, a brief brightening against the overall trend around it. Together they
come to just $\bmlNparams$ trainable numbers, which by the standards of modern deep learning is
tiny. That small size is a deliberate choice rather than a limitation. We are not short of
training data---we can simulate tens of millions of events---so the pressure is not to build a
larger model but to build one cheap enough to run on the entire survey, night after night. A
compact network that classifies a light curve in a single pass is far more useful here than a
heavy one that could only be applied to a pre-selected handful of candidates.

The model reads the light curve in a form that keeps the survey's own timing. Each of the three
filters is divided into a fixed set of time bins across the 72-day observing season: the workhorse
F146 filter into $\bmlBinsF$ bins (from $\bmlEpochsF$ individual exposures taken every
$\bmlCadence$~minutes), and the two colour filters into 96 bins each. For every bin the model is
given five simple numbers: the average brightness in that bin, the brightest and faintest points
in it, the fraction of the bin that was actually observed, and a flag saying whether it was
observed at all. All brightnesses are measured relative to a single quiet-state (baseline)
level, subtracted from all three filters together, which preserves the source's true colour so
the network can use it.

Keeping the brightest and faintest point in each bin, rather than only the average, is the one
design choice we treat as non-negotiable, and it is worth explaining why. The anomaly that reveals
a planet can be a sharp spike of nearly three magnitudes that lasts only a few hours. If the model
simply averaged each bin, that spike would be diluted to about a third of a magnitude---no larger
than the gentle curve of an ordinary single lens---and the very distinction the model exists to
make, planet-bearing binary versus plain single lens, would be washed out before the network ever
saw it. We therefore carry the per-bin maximum and minimum through the whole network untouched, so
a caustic spike always survives to be seen. When a colour filter is missing for an event---the
bluer F087 filter is lost in about 38\% of cases behind heavy dust---the model is simply told to
ignore it rather than being fed a guess. A final pooling step combines the processed bins into a
single summary, from which a standard classifier reads off the six class probabilities. The model
uses only the binned brightnesses; it does not use the individual error bars.

This compactness is what makes survey-scale, real-time use practical. Classification is a single
forward pass with no per-event fitting, and on a single $\bmlInferCores$-core CPU---no GPU
required---BinML classifies roughly $\bmlInferEps$ light curves per second, or $\bmlInferMs$~ms
per event. At that rate the network's own arithmetic keeps
pace with a full season of hundreds of millions of light curves in about a day on one node---in
a real deployment, reading and binning the light curves, rather than the forward pass, would set
the throughput---and the workload parallelises trivially across nodes. Re-classifying the entire
archive each night as new epochs arrive, which is what the cascade of \S\ref{sec:cascade}
requires, is therefore inexpensive rather than prohibitive.

\section{The real-time cascade}\label{sec:cascade}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{outputs/figures/cascade_evolution.pdf}
\caption{The real-time cascade, shown on three simulated events as the season is progressively
revealed (left to right in each panel). \emph{Top:} the light curve in all three bands (F146
grey, F087 blue, F213 red), with the dashed line marking the anomaly-onset day
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ for the binaries. \emph{Bottom:} BinML's class
probability as a function of how much of the season it has seen. For the clean binary (left),
probability flows \texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}, with the
jump to \texttt{NonPSPL} occurring precisely when the caustic enters the observed window---not
during the smooth rise before it. The subtle binary (centre) shows the same ordering with a late,
weak anomaly. Crucially, the single-lens control (right) never acquires appreciable
\texttt{NonPSPL} probability: the model does not invent an anomaly that is not there.}
\label{fig:cascade}
\end{figure*}

A survey classifier is never handed a finished light curve; it watches the season fill in night
by night. This is the setting in which a naive model fails in the most damaging way. A binary
lens is indistinguishable from a single lens throughout its smooth rise, and becomes identifiable
only once its caustic is on screen. A network trained on complete curves learns to
\emph{anticipate} the anomaly from the smooth portion---inflating early \texttt{NonPSPL}
probability and, in a deployed pipeline, firing follow-up triggers on planets that have not yet
revealed themselves.

We remove this failure mode by making the label itself a function of observation time. For each
binary we compute the day $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ at which its anomaly first becomes detectable, using
the same \dchisq{} threshold that defines the \texttt{PSPL}/\texttt{NonPSPL} boundary in
\S\ref{sec:detcond}. During training the network sees light curves truncated at random days: a
binary truncated \emph{before} $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ carries the \texttt{PSPL} label (with the
detectability floor re-evaluated on the truncated data), and earns \texttt{NonPSPL} only once the
anomaly lies inside the revealed window. The model thereby internalises the cascade
\texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}.

Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} shows the cascade at the level of individual events. As the clean binary
is revealed, BinML's probability mass moves from \texttt{Flat} to \texttt{PSPL} during the smooth
magnification and only jumps to \texttt{NonPSPL} when the caustic crosses into view at
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$; the subtle binary follows the same sequence with a weaker, later anomaly; and
the single-lens control never acquires meaningful \texttt{NonPSPL} probability at all. The
aggregate effect (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade_summary}) is large. The rate of \emph{premature} anomaly
flags---calling a binary \texttt{NonPSPL} before its anomaly is observable---falls from
$\bmlPrematureBefore\%$ to $\bmlPrematureAfter\%$, and the mean pre-onset \texttt{NonPSPL}
probability drops from $\bmlPreonsetBefore$ to $\bmlPreonsetAfter$. This costs essentially nothing
on the full-season task for which conventional classifiers are optimised: the missed-planet rate,
a true \texttt{NonPSPL} called \texttt{PSPL} on the complete curve, is statistically unchanged
($\bmlMissedBefore\rightarrow\bmlMissedAfter$).

The complement of avoiding premature flags is catching real anomalies quickly once they do
appear, which is the quantity a live alert stream cares about most.
Figure~\ref{fig:latency} measures this detection latency directly: for a sample of binaries we
step the model through the revealed season and record the first day its anomaly probability
crosses the operating threshold, relative to the true onset day $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$. The
distribution peaks just after onset, with a median lag of $\bmlDetLag$~days---fast enough that a
caustic crossing flagged by BinML is still in progress or freshly past when the alert fires. The
small negative-lag tail is the same population of premature flags quantified above, here recast
as timing false positives; it is a manageable minority rather than the bulk of the distribution.

This same ``report each class only when its evidence arrives'' behaviour is not special to
binaries; it holds for every class the model knows. Figure~\ref{fig:proball} shows, for one
representative event of each class, how the six class probabilities evolve as the season is
revealed. A short-period variable is recognised almost immediately, because its repeating
brightness pattern is visible from the first few days. A single-lens event stays unremarked until
its magnification begins, then rises cleanly to the single-lens class. An outburst is quiet until
it erupts, at which point the model switches decisively to the eruptive class. In every panel the
probability of the correct class rises just as its characteristic signal becomes visible and not
before---which is what makes the model's output safe to act on in real time.

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:eval}

Before presenting the results we explain three choices that make our numbers a fair reflection of
how the model would behave on the real survey, rather than flattering scores on a convenient test.

The first is how we summarise performance. A single accuracy figure is misleading here, because
the anomalous events are so rare that a model could score well simply by never flagging one. What
a follow-up team actually needs to know is a pair of numbers. One is \emph{purity}: of the events
the model flags as anomalous, what fraction really are. The other is \emph{completeness}: of the
anomalous events that exist, what fraction the model catches. These trade off against each
other---flag more events and you catch more real ones but also let in more false ones---so we fix
the purity to a chosen value, $\bmlPurityTarget$, and report the completeness reached at that
setting as our headline number. This is exactly the form in which a survey team would state a
requirement: ``keep the flagged sample at least ninety per cent pure, and tell me how many planets
I still find.''

The second is a correction for how the training set was built. To train efficiently we
deliberately over-represented rare and faint events and under-represented the common ones, so the
mix of classes in our data is not the mix the survey will see. To undo this when reporting
survey-level numbers, we give each test event a weight that reverses its over- or
under-representation, so the weighted test set matches the true survey population. We apply this
correction only where it belongs: to purity, which depends on how often each kind of source turns
up, but not to completeness, which is a property of a single class and does not depend on the
mixture. Every population-level number below carries this correction.

The third is a yardstick to judge the model against. For each event we can compute, from the
perfect noiseless light curve and the true parameters, how strong its anomaly signal is---in
effect, how detectable it would be to an ideal observer who already knew the answer. Ranking
events this way is an ``oracle'': it cannot be done in practice, because it uses information the
survey never has, but it sets the best any blind method could hope to do. Comparing BinML against
it tells us not just whether the model is good in isolation, but how much of the genuinely
recoverable information it manages to extract from the raw data alone.

For a triage layer the two kinds of anomaly error carry very different costs, and it is worth
separating them. A false negative---a real binary called a single lens---costs a planet, and on
full-season curves these occur at a rate of $\bmlMissedRate$, dominated by anomalies that fall
below the detectability floor and so carry no recoverable signal. A false positive---a
non-anomalous source flagged as a binary---costs follow-up time, and these are rarer still;
reassuringly, they are overwhelmingly ordinary single-lens events near the anomaly-detection
boundary rather than variable stars, with the physically dangerous \texttt{LongPeriodVar}%
-as-microlensing confusion occurring at only $\bmlLPVtoPSPL$. Because the operating threshold is a
free parameter (\S\ref{sec:eval}), the balance between the two error types can be set to whatever
a given follow-up programme can afford, moving smoothly along the purity--completeness curve of
Figure~\ref{fig:pr} rather than being stuck with one fixed decision.

Finding the anomalous events is where the model earns its keep, and Figure~\ref{fig:pr} measures
this against the ideal-observer yardstick of \S\ref{sec:eval}. A convenient way to score the whole
purity--completeness curve with one number is its average precision, which runs from~0 to a perfect~1;
BinML scores $\bmlAPnet$, against the oracle ceiling's $\bmlAPoracle$. The gap is small. The
network recovers very nearly all of the anomaly information that is present even in principle---but
it does so blind, reading the raw brightness measurements in a single pass, without fitting a
binary-lens model to a single candidate. Its success also rises steadily with how strong the
anomaly is, from $\bmlSliceSuzuki$ for the borderline planetary signals near the mass-ratio limit
where planets become common to $0.972$ for the strongest anomalies, just as one would expect if
the model is limited by the information in the data rather than by any shortcoming of its own.

\section{Generalisation and limits}\label{sec:limits}

A model can reproduce held-out numbers by memorising the distribution it was trained on, so we test
generalisation directly. We generated a $\bmlStressN$-million-event stress set from a disjoint
pseudo-random stream---seed base $\bmlSeedStress$ against $\bmlSeedTrain$ for the
train/validation/test data---which guarantees parameter tuples the model provably never encountered.
On the natural-population subset BinML reproduces its held-out performance, with a macro-F1 of
$\bmlStressMacroF$.

The degradations that do appear are confined to physically extreme corners, and each has a physical
cause rather than a modelling one: sources fainter than $m\approx25$, where noise begins to
manufacture spurious anomalies; very wide caustics with $s>5$, which are rarely crossed within a
single season; and sub-day timescales, which place too few epochs on the peak to constrain it. These
are near-fundamental limits of the photometry, not gaps a larger training set could close. Robustness
to missing data is likewise encouraging: events observed without the blue band are still recovered at
$\bmlSliceNoBlue$, confirming that the model degrades gracefully rather than failing when colour
information is unavailable.

One assumption underlying every number in this paper deserves to be stated plainly, because it is
the most consequential modelling choice we make. The boundaries between our classes---flat versus
event, and single lens versus anomalous---are set not only by a goodness-of-fit threshold but also
by a minimum detectable amplitude, which we fix at twenty times an assumed one-millimagnitude
systematic floor. This floor stands in for the correlated, night-to-week photometric systematics
that do not average down the way independent per-epoch noise does, and which no real pipeline can
follow below some level; we adopt a fixed amplitude cut rather than modelling a full red-noise
process. It is, however, a modelling choice and not a measured property of \Rom{}, and because it
sits on both class boundaries at once, the absolute completeness and purity we quote will shift if
the true systematic floor differs from our assumption. The \emph{relative} results---the size of
the label surgery, the direction and magnitude of the cascade effect, the shape of the parameter
dependence---are far less sensitive to it, but a quantitative recalibration of this floor against a
real \Rom{} red-noise estimate is a prerequisite before the absolute numbers are used to set a
survey requirement.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

BinML is built to be a triage layer for the \Rom{} GBTDS: a cheap first pass that flags candidate
microlensing and anomalous events for the expensive, definitive step of full light-curve modelling.
Two properties make it suited to that role rather than merely accurate on a static benchmark. The
completeness-at-fixed-purity framing lets a survey team set the operating point directly against
their follow-up budget, trading completeness for purity along a curve they control rather than
accepting whatever an argmax delivers. And the real-time cascade means the flag can be trusted as a
\emph{trigger}: a \texttt{NonPSPL} call certifies that the anomaly has been seen, not merely
anticipated, which is the precondition for autonomous follow-up. That BinML attains near-oracle
anomaly ranking blind, in a single forward pass, is what makes running it across the full survey
stream feasible where per-event binary-lens fitting never could be.

The natural next step is validation on real events. OGLE-IV light curves, and ultimately early
\Rom{} data, are what turn ``works on simulations'' into ``works,'' and they are the trigger for any
targeted retraining. The one weak spot with real follow-up implications---faint-source false
anomalies---is the obvious target for such retraining, should real data show it to matter in
practice; the remaining limits sit close enough to the physics that we expect little return from
chasing them.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented BinML, a compact multi-band transformer classifier for \Rom{} GBTDS light curves,
built around two ideas---detectability-conditioned labelling and a real-time anomaly-onset
cascade---that make it deployable rather than merely accurate. It reaches a completeness of
$\bmlCompleteness$ at a purity of $\bmlPurity$ (average precision $\bmlAP$), recovers near-oracle
anomaly ranking blind, cuts premature binary alerts from $\bmlPrematureBefore\%$ to
$\bmlPrematureAfter\%$, and generalises to a $\bmlStressN$-million-event unseen-parameter stress
test. The model, its weights, and the full simulation-to-inference pipeline are released as open
source.

\appendix

\section{Simulation and training infrastructure}\label{app:infra}

Because reproducibility and the real computational cost of a method are as much a part of a survey
tool as its accuracy, we describe here exactly how the data were generated and the model trained,
and what it cost.

\paragraph{Simulation (AWS).} The training set of roughly $\bmlSimEvents$~million events was
generated on Amazon Web Services in the \texttt{\bmlSimRegion} region, on a small fleet of
$\bmlSimInstances$ transient (Spot) instances of $\bmlSimVcpus$ virtual CPUs each. The generator is
embarrassingly parallel: one process builds one self-contained shard of a few thousand light
curves, seeded purely by its index, so a shard that is lost to a Spot interruption costs at most
that shard and the run resumes by skipping shards already written to cloud storage. At a measured
throughput of about $\bmlSimEps$ events per second across the fleet, the full set was produced in
roughly $\bmlSimHours$~hours. Because the instances are free-tier-eligible Spot capacity, the
compute cost of a full generation is at the level of cents; the only non-trivial cost is storage of
the compact binned shards, a few dollars per month.

\paragraph{Training (local, with a cloud comparison).} The network is small enough that it was
trained end to end on a personal computer, on the \bmlTrainDevice, at a batch size of
$\bmlTrainBatch$. A benchmark of the training step (forward and backward pass on a full batch)
sustains about $\bmlTrainEps$ events per second, which puts a full training run of a few passes
over the $\bmlSimEvents$-million-event set at roughly $\bmlTrainHours$~hours of wall-clock, and the
production model was trained in this range. On local hardware the marginal cost is essentially only
electricity---of order ten cents' worth---since no compute is rented. For comparison, renting a
modest cloud GPU (for example on AWS or Modal) would train the same model faster but for a cost of
order a few dollars, given its small size. Either way, the end-to-end cost of building BinML from
scratch is dominated by neither training nor inference and is negligible. This is a direct consequence of the design choice to keep the network
compact (\S\ref{sec:model}): the entire pipeline, from simulation through training to survey-scale
inference, runs at hobbyist cost rather than requiring a dedicated computing allocation.
