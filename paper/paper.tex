\begin{abstract}
The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will monitor hundreds of millions of stars in the
Galactic bulge at $\sim$15-minute cadence, and among them it will catch the brief brightenings
of gravitational microlensing---including the rare events in which a planet betrays itself
through a short, sharp anomaly riding on an otherwise smooth light curve. Finding those events in
a data stream far too large to inspect by hand, and flagging each one the moment its evidence
appears rather than after the season has ended, is as much a problem of survey logistics as of
astrophysics. We present \textbf{BinML}, a compact ($\bmlNparams$-parameter) neural network that
sorts \Rom{} light curves into six classes: sources with no detectable event, ordinary
single-lens microlensing, the anomalous binary and planetary lenses that carry the planet signal,
and three kinds of variable star that would otherwise contaminate the sample. Two ideas make
BinML suited to a real survey rather than a fixed benchmark. First, each event is labelled by
what can actually be seen in its photometry, not by the parameters used to simulate it, so the
network is never trained to claim an anomaly that leaves no observable trace. Second, the model
reports each class only once that class's evidence has arrived, so a binary is announced when its
anomaly appears and not during the smooth rise that precedes it. Measured event by event, the
model's first alert precedes the anomaly in $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ of eligible binaries, and
follows it by a median of $\bmlCascMedianLag$~days when it does not. On a held-out test set of
$\bmlNtest$ events and under the adopted synthetic class mixture, BinML recovers
$\bmlCompletenessPct\%$ of the anomalous events while holding the flagged sample to a purity of
$\bmlPurityTarget$, and it reproduces this on a $\bmlStressN$-million-event set drawn from
parameters it never saw. These results use a legacy Cycle-7-inspired \Rom{}-like cadence and are a
reproducible synthetic benchmark rather than a forecast of survey yields, which would require
event-rate priors and the current survey design. The model and its full simulation-to-inference
pipeline are released as open source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gravitational microlensing occupies a niche no other planet-detection method reaches. Because it
responds to a lens's mass rather than its light, it is sensitive to cold, low-mass planets far
from their host stars and to unbound planetary-mass objects drifting through the Galaxy---the very
population that transit and radial-velocity surveys, biased toward hot and close-in planets,
recover least well \citep{Mao1991,GouldLoeb1992,Suzuki2016}. The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will exploit
this sensitivity systematically. Its Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey (GBTDS) will monitor the
crowded bulge fields at high cadence in the near-infrared, and is predicted to detect of order a
thousand bound planets and to pin down the free-floating planet mass function
\citep{Spergel2015,Penny2019,Johnson2020,Sumi2023,Gaudi2012}; the same data will also yield
transiting planets \citep{Wilson2023}. In its design
\citep{Penny2019}, the wide F146 filter samples the light curve every
$\sim\bmlCadence$~minutes; two auxiliary bands, F087 and F213, add colour information on a
$\sim$6-hour cycle.

That sensitivity is inseparable from two operational problems. The first is sheer volume: the
survey will yield hundreds of millions of light curves, within which genuine microlensing events
are a small minority and anomalous---binary or planetary---events rarer still. No team can
inspect them by hand, and the definitive step for any candidate, fitting a full binary-lens
model, is far too expensive to run on the whole stream. The second problem is timing. The
signal that betrays a planet is short: a caustic crossing lasting hours to a day, riding on top
of a smooth magnification that itself unfolds over weeks. A classifier meant to \emph{trigger}
follow-up must therefore be honest about time---it must not announce a binary during the smooth
rise, before the caustic that would justify the alert has actually been observed. A model that
guesses early is actively harmful: it spends a finite follow-up budget on planets that have
not yet revealed themselves, and may never. The need for real-time response is not new to
microlensing: rapid-response anomaly-detection and alert systems have driven planet follow-up for
close to two decades \citep{Udalski2003,Dominik2007,Dominik2008,Tsapras2009}. Those systems, however, sit
\emph{downstream} of detection, reacting to deviations once an event is already known; the
scale and cadence of \Rom{} instead demand that the real-time decision be made inside the
classifier that scans the raw stream.

Machine learning has been applied to microlensing classification before, and the trajectory is
instructive. Feature-engineered classifiers such as \textsc{MicroLIA} \citep{Godines2019} and
neural networks trained on lower-cadence ground-based data \citep{Mroz2020} established that the
problem is learnable on OGLE- and ZTF-like photometry. Work aimed specifically at high-cadence,
\Rom{}-like microlensing sampling \citep{Khakpash2021}, together with deep-learning classifiers
built to flag transients in real time more broadly \citep{Muthukrishna2019}, are the closest
antecedents of the present work. Architecturally we build on the now-standard use of
transformers for astronomical time series \citep{Astromer2023,Vaswani2017}. What distinguishes
BinML, however, is not the network but the way the problem is posed: we treat detectability and
observation time as first-class parts of the label definition rather than as afterthoughts of
evaluation.

This paper makes three contributions, developed in the sections that follow. First, we introduce
\emph{detectability-conditioned labelling} (\S\ref{sec:sims}): events are labelled by the
evidence a perfect observer could recover, which removes the label noise that would otherwise
punish the model for failing to see what is physically unobservable. Second, we build a
\emph{real-time cascade} (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) by making each label a function of how much of the
season has been revealed, so the network learns to report classes in the order their evidence
arrives. Third, we evaluate the model honestly at survey scale
(\S\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:limits}): completeness at a fixed purity as the headline metric, a
selection-aware reweighting that corrects for the training distribution, and a
$\bmlStressN$-million-event stress test on parameters the model never saw.

\subsection{Six classes across three bands}
We simulate \Rom{} GBTDS light curves in the six classes of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}, chosen to
span the populations a real triage pipeline must separate. \texttt{Flat} sources show no
detectable event. \texttt{PSPL} events are single-lens, point-source point-lens microlensing
\citep{Paczynski1986}. \texttt{NonPSPL} events---the anomalous, science-critical class---are
binary and planetary lenses, computed with the contour-integration code \textsc{VBBinaryLensing}
\citep{Bozza2010,Bozza2018}, and are distinguished by the caustic perturbations visible in the
third panel of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}. The remaining three classes are the variable-star
populations that dominate the real bulge and are the classifier's most dangerous contaminants:
\texttt{PeriodicVar} (RR~Lyrae, eclipsing binaries, $\delta$~Scuti), \texttt{LongPeriodVar}
(Miras, semiregulars, and OSARGs---small-amplitude red-giant pulsators---whose slow rise most
readily mimics a long-timescale microlensing event), and \texttt{Eruptive} (dwarf novae and Be
outbursts). Event-timescale, mass-ratio, and separation priors follow the measured bulge
distributions \citep{Mroz2019,Suzuki2016}; the variable-star templates follow the OGLE bulge
catalogues \citep{Soszynski2013,Soszynski2014}. Every event is realised on a legacy
Cycle-7-inspired \Rom{}-like cadence---$\sim$15-minute F146 sampling with 6-hour colour visits over
a single 72-day season---in all three bands and degraded by photometric noise, blending, and
interstellar extinction. This cadence is not the current GBTDS design (\S\ref{sec:limits}); we use
it because the model and its training predate the updated survey definition.

\subsection{Labelling by what is observable}\label{sec:detcond}
The methodological core of BinML is a single principle: \emph{an event's label is set by what a
perfect observer could recover from the photometry, not by the parameters we injected}. The
principle operates at both boundaries the classifier cares about. A microlensing event whose
peak falls outside the 72-day season, or whose amplitude is buried beneath the noise, is labelled
\texttt{Flat}, because its in-window signal falls below the adopted simulation-based detectability
threshold; training the network to call it \texttt{PSPL} would only teach it to guess. Likewise, a
binary whose caustic perturbation lies everywhere below the noise floor is, observationally, a
single lens, and is labelled \texttt{PSPL}. In practice we measure how far each binary's true,
noiseless light curve departs from the best single-lens description of it; if that departure is
too small to rise above the noise, the event is not given the anomalous label, whatever generated
it. This does two useful things at once. It cleans up the boundaries between classes, where most
labelling mistakes would otherwise happen, and it keeps our later purity figures meaningful: an
anomaly the model appears to ``miss'' under this scheme is one that carried no detectable evidence
to begin with. We return to what this means for the numbers in \S\ref{sec:results}.

The scale of this relabelling is not a detail but the point. Across the test set,
$\bmlRelabelled\%$ of events receive an observational label that differs from the class they were
generated as---that is, more than a third of all events would carry a demonstrably misleading
label under the naive scheme of labelling by generator intent. The effect is concentrated exactly
where it should be: of the events generated as binary or planetary lenses, only $\bmlNonpsplKept\%$
show a detectable anomaly and keep the anomalous label, while $\bmlNonpsplToPspl\%$ have a caustic
perturbation buried below the noise and are, correctly, indistinguishable from single lenses;
similarly, $\bmlLpvToFlat\%$ of the long-period variables vary too weakly within a single season
to be detected and are labelled flat. Training a network against generator intent would therefore
ask it to assert, on more than a third of its examples, evidence that is physically absent---the
definition of label noise. Conditioning the labels on detectability removes it at the source. A
full ablation that retrains the network on generator-intent labels and measures the resulting
degradation is the natural next experiment, and we flag it as such; here we establish the size of
the mislabelling the scheme corrects.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{outputs/figures/schematic.pdf}
\caption{BinML architecture. Each of the three \Rom{} bands is binned onto a fixed grid whose
bins carry five channels---baseline-relative mean, min, and max magnitude, observed fraction, and
an observed mask. A convolutional stem, whose non-learned min/max lanes preserve caustic extrema
by construction, reduces the bands to $\bmlNtokens$ tokens that a four-layer transformer
classifies into six classes. The whole model has $\bmlNparams$ parameters.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure*}

BinML has two parts (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}). The first is a small convolutional network---a
stack of learned filters that scan along the light curve looking for local shapes, the same kind
of building block used in image recognition. The second is a transformer, a now-standard
architecture that lets every part of the light curve ``attend to'' every other part, so the model
can weigh, for example, a brief brightening against the overall trend around it. Together they
come to just $\bmlNparams$ trainable numbers, which by the standards of modern deep learning is
tiny. That small size is a deliberate choice rather than a limitation. We are not short of
training data---we simulate of order ten million events---so the pressure is not to build a
larger model but to build one cheap enough to run on the entire survey, night after night. A
compact network that classifies a light curve in a single pass is far more useful here than a
heavy one that could only be applied to a pre-selected handful of candidates.

The model reads the light curve in a form that keeps the survey's own timing. Each of the three
filters is divided into a fixed set of time bins across the 72-day observing season: the workhorse
F146 filter into $\bmlBinsF$ bins (from $\bmlEpochsF$ individual exposures taken every
$\bmlCadence$~minutes), and the two colour filters into 96 bins each. For every bin the model is
given five simple numbers: the average brightness in that bin, the brightest and faintest points
in it, the fraction of the bin that was actually observed, and a flag saying whether it was
observed at all. All brightnesses are measured relative to a single quiet-state (baseline)
level, subtracted from all three filters together, which preserves the source's true colour so
the network can use it.

Keeping the brightest and faintest point in each bin, rather than only the average, is the one
design choice we treat as non-negotiable, and it is worth explaining why. The anomaly that reveals
a planet can be a sharp spike of nearly three magnitudes that lasts only a few hours. If the model
simply averaged each bin, that spike would be diluted to about a third of a magnitude---no larger
than the gentle curve of an ordinary single lens---and the very distinction the model exists to
make, planet-bearing binary versus plain single lens, would be washed out before the network ever
saw it. We therefore carry the per-bin maximum and minimum through the whole network untouched, so
a caustic spike always survives to be seen. When a colour filter is missing for an event---the
bluer F087 filter is lost in about 38\% of cases behind heavy dust---the model is simply told to
ignore it rather than being fed a guess. A final pooling step combines the processed bins into a
single summary, from which a standard classifier reads off the six class probabilities. The model
uses only the binned brightnesses; it does not use the individual error bars.

This compactness is what makes survey-scale, real-time use practical. Classification is a single
forward pass with no per-event fitting, and on a single $\bmlInferCores$-core CPU---no GPU
required---BinML classifies roughly $\bmlInferEps$ light curves per second, or $\bmlInferMs$~ms
per event. At that rate one node classifies of order a hundred million light curves per day, so a
handful of nodes cover a full season of hundreds of millions of curves in a day or two---and in a
real deployment it is reading and binning the light curves, not the forward pass, that would set
the throughput. The workload parallelises trivially across nodes. Re-classifying the entire
archive each night as new epochs arrive, which is what the cascade of \S\ref{sec:cascade}
requires, is therefore inexpensive rather than prohibitive.

\section{The real-time cascade}\label{sec:cascade}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{outputs/figures/cascade_evolution.pdf}
\caption{The real-time cascade, shown on three simulated events as the season is progressively
revealed (left to right in each panel). \emph{Top:} the light curve in all three bands (F146
grey, F087 blue, F213 red), with the dashed line marking the anomaly-onset day
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ for the binaries. \emph{Bottom:} BinML's class
probability as a function of how much of the season it has seen. For the clean binary (left),
probability flows \texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}, with the
jump to \texttt{NonPSPL} occurring near the coarsely estimated detectability onset---not
during the smooth rise before it. The subtle binary (centre) shows the same ordering with a late,
weak anomaly. Crucially, the single-lens control (right) never acquires appreciable
\texttt{NonPSPL} probability: the model does not invent an anomaly that is not there.}
\label{fig:cascade}
\end{figure*}

A survey classifier is never handed a finished light curve; it watches the season fill in night
by night. This is the setting in which a naive model fails in the most damaging way. A binary
lens is indistinguishable from a single lens throughout its smooth rise, and becomes identifiable
only once its caustic is on screen. A network trained on complete curves learns to
\emph{anticipate} the anomaly from the smooth portion---inflating early \texttt{NonPSPL}
probability and, in a deployed pipeline, firing follow-up triggers on planets that have not yet
revealed themselves.

We remove this failure mode by making the label itself a function of observation time. For each
binary we compute the day $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ at which its anomaly first becomes detectable, using
the same \dchisq{} threshold that defines the \texttt{PSPL}/\texttt{NonPSPL} boundary in
\S\ref{sec:detcond}. During training the network sees light curves truncated at random days: a
binary truncated \emph{before} $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ carries the \texttt{PSPL} label (with the
detectability floor re-evaluated on the truncated data), and earns \texttt{NonPSPL} only once the
anomaly lies inside the revealed window. The model thereby internalises the cascade
\texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}.

Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} shows the cascade at the level of individual events. As the clean binary
is revealed, BinML's probability mass moves from \texttt{Flat} to \texttt{PSPL} during the smooth
magnification and only jumps to \texttt{NonPSPL} when the caustic crosses into view at
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$; the subtle binary follows the same sequence with a weaker, later anomaly; and
the single-lens control never acquires meaningful \texttt{NonPSPL} probability at all. The
aggregate behaviour is measured event by event rather than on isolated snapshots, because a live
stream re-evaluates every light curve as each new epoch arrives. For each of $\bmlCascN$ eligible
detectable binaries we sweep the revealed season and record the \emph{first} day the anomaly
probability crosses the operating threshold; an alert is premature if that first crossing precedes
the onset $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$. The model detects $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of these binaries within the
season (the rest are right-censored), with a median lag of $\bmlCascMedianLag$~days after onset.
Premature first alerts occur for $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ of eligible binaries (95\% CI
$[\bmlCascPrematureLo,\bmlCascPrematureHi]$), or $\bmlCascPrematureOfDet\%$ of those detected.

Two caveats govern how that number should be read, and both cut against a flattering
interpretation. First, it is \emph{grid-dependent}: the more often the stream is re-evaluated, the
more opportunities exist for an early excursion to cross the threshold, and halving our reveal
step from one day to half a day roughly doubles the measured premature rate. At the survey's true
$\sim$15-minute cadence it would be higher still, so our figure is a lower bound on what a
continuously-evaluated pipeline would see. Second, an earlier version of this work quoted a much
smaller rate obtained from a single randomly-chosen pre-onset snapshot per event; that is a
different quantity---the probability that an arbitrary pre-onset moment is over threshold---and it
is not the alert rate. We report the event-level figure because it is the one an alert broker
experiences.

We therefore do not claim the cascade eliminates premature alerts. What the training scheme
supplies is an ordering---probability mass moves \texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}%
$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL} as evidence accumulates (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade})---and a
median alert that lands shortly after onset rather than during the smooth rise. Establishing that
the truncation augmentation \emph{causes} this, rather than merely accompanying it, requires a
matched-recipe ablation against a model trained without it; an earlier untracked comparison
reporting a large reduction could not be reproduced and has been withdrawn.

The complement of avoiding premature flags is catching real anomalies quickly once they do
appear, which is the quantity a live alert stream cares about most.
Figure~\ref{fig:latency} measures this detection latency: we step the model through the revealed
season on a half-day grid and record the first day its anomaly probability crosses the operating
threshold, relative to the onset day $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$. Two properties of this measurement matter.
First, we retain \emph{every} eligible detectable binary ($N=\bmlCascN$) rather than only those the
model eventually flags: $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ are detected within the season and the remaining
$\bmlCascCensored$ are right-censored, so the quoted latency is not inflated by discarding
failures---the median below is conditional on detection, and should be read together with that
detection fraction rather than as an unconditional expectation. Second, among detected events the
median lag is $\bmlCascMedianLag$~days. A caveat we state explicitly: $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ is itself
estimated on a coarse grid, so this should be read as ``within a few days of onset'' rather than as
a precise interval. The negative-lag fraction ($\bmlCascPrematureOfDet\%$ of detections) is the
premature-alert population quantified above; these are the same events, from the same sweep.

This same ``report each class only when its evidence arrives'' behaviour is not special to
binaries; it holds for every class the model knows. Figure~\ref{fig:proball} shows, for one
representative event of each class, how the six class probabilities evolve as the season is
revealed. A short-period variable is recognised almost immediately, because its repeating
brightness pattern is visible from the first few days. A single-lens event stays unremarked until
its magnification begins, then rises cleanly to the single-lens class. An outburst is quiet until
it erupts, at which point the model switches decisively to the eruptive class. In every panel the
probability of the correct class rises just as its characteristic signal becomes visible and not
before---which is what lets the model's output be used as a real-time trigger under the evaluated
conditions.

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:eval}

Before presenting the results we explain three choices that make our numbers a fair reflection of
how the model would behave on the real survey, rather than flattering scores on a convenient test.

The first is how we summarise performance. A single accuracy figure is misleading here, because
the anomalous events are so rare that a model could score well simply by never flagging one. What
a follow-up team actually needs to know is a pair of numbers. One is \emph{purity}: of the events
the model flags as anomalous, what fraction really are. The other is \emph{completeness}: of the
anomalous events that exist, what fraction the model catches. These are precisely the quantities
tabulated as precision and recall in Table~\ref{tab:perclass}, and their harmonic mean is the F1
score there (macro-F1 being its unweighted average over the six classes). These trade off against each
other---flag more events and you catch more real ones but also let in more false ones---so we fix
the purity to a chosen value, $\bmlPurityTarget$, and report the completeness reached at that
setting as our headline number. This is exactly the form in which a survey team would state a
requirement: ``keep the flagged sample at least ninety per cent pure, and tell me how many planets
I still find.''

The second is a partial correction for how the training set was built. To train efficiently we
subsample the abundant demoted-binary by-products, and each test event carries a weight that
reverses \emph{that} subsampling, applied to purity (which depends on the mix of false positives)
but not to completeness (a per-class property). This is important to state precisely: the weighting
undoes one internal sampling step, but it does \emph{not} reweight the deliberately chosen
generator class quotas to a survey event-rate prior. The class mixture here is therefore an adopted
synthetic mixture, not an inferred \Rom{} population, and the purity and completeness we quote are
conditional on it; \S\ref{sec:limits} discusses the prior-sensitivity this implies.

The third is a reference to judge the anomaly ranking against. For each event we can compute, from
the noiseless light curve and the true parameters, its injected anomaly \dchisq{}---in effect how
detectable the anomaly would be to an observer who already knew the answer. This is a
\emph{truth-informed reference}, not an oracle upper bound: it uses information the survey never has
and is not fieldable, and a learned model exploiting other morphology could in principle rank
better than it on real, noisy data. Comparing BinML against it measures how much of the injected
anomaly ordering the model reconstructs from the raw measurements alone.

event-rate priors would be needed (\S\ref{sec:limits}). Per-class performance is collected in

For a triage layer the two kinds of anomaly error carry very different costs, and it is worth
separating them. A false negative---a detectable binary called a single lens---costs a planet, and
on full-season curves these occur at a rate of $\bmlMissedRate$, concentrated just above the
detectability floor where the anomaly signal is weakest (Figure~\ref{fig:params}). A false positive---a
non-anomalous source flagged as a binary---costs follow-up time, and these are rarer still;
reassuringly, they are overwhelmingly ordinary single-lens events near the anomaly-detection
boundary rather than variable stars, with the \texttt{LongPeriodVar}-as-microlensing confusion
(an observational false positive under the adopted labelling) occurring at only $\bmlLPVtoPSPL$. Because the operating threshold is a
free parameter (\S\ref{sec:eval}), the balance between the two error types can be set to whatever
a given follow-up programme can afford, moving smoothly along the purity--completeness curve of
Figure~\ref{fig:pr} rather than being stuck with one fixed decision.

Finding the anomalous events is the model's primary task, and Figure~\ref{fig:pr} measures
this against the truth-informed reference of \S\ref{sec:eval}. A convenient way to score the whole
purity--completeness curve with one number is its average precision, which runs from~0 to a perfect~1;
BinML scores $\bmlAPnet$, against the reference's $\bmlAPoracle$. The gap is small---though it
should be read with a caveat: the reference ranks by the same anomaly \dchisq{} that, on the noiseless
curve, defines the \texttt{NonPSPL} label itself, so this is not an independent information bound
but a measure of how faithfully BinML reconstructs the label-defining quantity. What it establishes
is nonetheless the operative point: the network recovers that ordering without access to the
generator truth, from the raw noisy brightness measurements in a single pass, without ever computing
the \dchisq{} or fitting a binary-lens model to a candidate. Its success also rises steadily with how strong the
anomaly is, from $\bmlSliceSuzuki$ for the borderline planetary signals near the mass-ratio limit
where planets become common to $\bmlSliceStrongAnom$ for the strongest anomalies, just as one would expect if
the model is limited by the information in the data rather than by any shortcoming of its own.

\section{Generalisation and limits}\label{sec:limits}

A model can reproduce held-out numbers by memorising individual training examples rather than
learning the underlying mapping, so we test generalisation directly---both to unseen parameter
draws from the same priors and, through the out-of-range regimes below, to parts of parameter space
the training distribution barely populates. We generated a $\bmlStressN$-million-event stress set from a disjoint
pseudo-random stream---seed base $\bmlSeedStress$ against $\bmlSeedTrain$ for the
train/validation/test data---which guarantees parameter tuples the model provably never encountered;
it comprises a $\bmlStressNatN$-million-event natural-population subset and a suite of
deliberately out-of-range regimes. On the natural-population subset BinML reproduces its held-out
performance, with a macro-F1 of $\bmlStressMacroF$.

The out-of-range regimes, by contrast, expose real failures, and we report them rather than
characterising them as merely ``extreme''. Anomaly recall stays near its in-distribution value of
$\bmlStressNatNp$ across most regimes, but collapses to $\bmlStressWidesep$ for very wide
separations, where the caustic is rarely crossed within a season. Two contaminant regimes fail
outright: long-period variables whose period exceeds the season are recovered as
\texttt{PeriodicVar} only $\bmlStressLongp$ of the time (a nearly complete failure, since within one
window they are not observably periodic), and sub-day-timescale single lenses are recovered at
$\bmlStressShortte$. For faint sources, \texttt{PSPL} recall falls to $\bmlStressFaintPspl$ and
anomaly \emph{precision} to $\bmlStressFaintPrec$---noise manufacturing spurious anomalies is the
single most consequential failure mode for a follow-up programme. In the deep-planetary regime,
recall holds at $\bmlStressPlanetNp$ but precision falls to $\bmlStressPlanetPrec$.

We deliberately do not claim these are irreducible. Each has a plausible physical explanation---too
few epochs on the peak, a signal that does not complete within the window, photometric noise
comparable to the signal---but we have run no ablation showing that broader training coverage could
not close them, and the deep-planetary deficit in particular is a regime the training distribution
barely populates. They are better read as the current model's documented operating envelope than as
limits of the method. Robustness to missing data is more encouraging: events observed without the
blue band are still recovered at $\bmlSliceNoBlue$, so the model degrades gracefully when colour is
unavailable.

One boundary is deliberate and worth stating as such: BinML is a specialist for \Rom{}'s dense
cadence. Its discriminating power---the non-learned min/max lanes that preserve a caustic spike, the
colour bands, the fine time grid---presumes the $\sim$15-minute F146 sampling the survey will
deliver. On the far sparser cadence of an archival ground survey the model is out of its regime, and
should not be applied there: this is a matter of scope, not of accuracy, since \Rom{} will supply
exactly the sampling the model is built for. The obstacle is more fundamental than raw cadence.
Even the densest existing survey, the three-site KMTNet \citep{Kim2016}, does not validate the model: applied to
real KMTNet events---including several sampled at $60$--$80$ points per day, above the density at
which \emph{uniform} subsampling of our simulations still works---the model misclassifies them as
periodic variables. The cause is the sampling \emph{pattern}, not its mean rate: ground data is
clustered into nightly visits separated by daytime gaps, and that near-diurnal periodicity is read
as stellar variability. A matched-density test isolates this from the trivial explanation that
ground data simply has fewer points. Comparing uniform sampling against an eight-hour nightly
observing window \emph{at identical total visit counts}---so that only the gap topology differs---%
anomaly recall over $\bmlGapN$ events is $\bmlGapUniformA$ (uniform; 95\% CI
$[\bmlGapUniformALo,\bmlGapUniformAHi]$) versus $\bmlGapNightlyA$ (gapped; upper bound
$\bmlGapNightlyAHi$) at $\bmlGapVisitsA$~visits per day, and $\bmlGapUniformB$ versus
$\bmlGapNightlyA$ at $\bmlGapVisitsB$~per day; the intervals do not overlap. At
$\bmlGapVisitsC$~visits per day both schedules fail ($\bmlGapUniformC$ uniform), so below that
density sparsity alone dominates and the comparison is uninformative. In the regime where uniform
sampling still works, the gapped schedule does not: the daily gap, not the visit count, is what
defeats the model. We note this establishes gap \emph{sensitivity} in simulation; it is consistent
with, but does not by itself prove, that gaps caused the KMTNet failures above. Roman's uninterrupted,
space-based cadence has no such gaps, and no ground network can reproduce it. Genuine validation of
the model in its own regime therefore awaits \Rom{}. Sparse single-band archival data is meanwhile
the province of simpler feature- or single-band models---and, as a sanity check that the overall
approach transfers to real photometry at all, an earlier single-band variant of this model
correctly flagged the caustic-crossing binary lenses OGLE-2014-BLG-0289 and OGLE-2013-BLG-0578 in
real OGLE-IV data \citep{Udalski2015}.

A further scope limitation concerns the survey design itself. The cadence we simulate---%
$\sim$15-minute F146 sampling with 6-hour colour visits over a single 72-day season---follows the
older Cycle-7-inspired \Rom{} design \citep{Khakpash2021}, not the current Galactic Bulge
Time-Domain Survey definition, which specifies a faster ($\sim$12-minute) high-cadence F146,
more frequent colour visits, and six high-cadence plus four low-cadence seasons with the
attendant seasonal gaps \citep{ROTAC2025}. Our results are therefore a benchmark under a
legacy \Rom{}-like schedule, not a forecast under the survey as it will actually run.

We can, however, bound how much the \emph{sampling rate} alone matters. We repeated the entire
pipeline---simulation, training from scratch, and evaluation---at the current $\sim$12-minute F146
rate and at our legacy 15-minute rate, holding everything else fixed (matched shard count,
$\bmlCadN$ events, $\bmlCadEpochs$ epochs, identical seed). The two arms are indistinguishable:
anomaly average precision $\bmlCadFifteenAp$ (15\,min) versus $\bmlCadTwelveAp$ (12\,min), and
completeness $\bmlCadFifteenCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlCadFifteenPurity$ versus
$\bmlCadTwelveCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlCadTwelvePurity$, with per-class F1 differing by less
than $0.02$ everywhere. The two headline differences even disagree in sign, which is the signature
of run-to-run variation rather than a systematic effect. This is physically unsurprising: both rates
sample an hours-to-day caustic densely, so a $25\%$ change in epoch count adds little information.
The rate change is thus not the risk; what remains untested is the current design's
\emph{structure}---six high-cadence plus four low-cadence seasons with seasonal gaps---which our
single-season, fixed-window input cannot represent without an architectural change. That, rather
than re-tuning the cadence, is the most important extension of this work.

The single most important caveat on the headline numbers concerns the class mixture. The purity we
quote is not a property of the classifier alone but of the classifier \emph{and} the assumed
prevalence of anomalous events in the input stream. Our synthetic evaluation mixture contains
detectable anomalies at a prevalence of $\bmlPriorPiZero\%$; the prevalence in a real survey stream
is set by microlensing event rates and any upstream candidate selection, and is typically far
lower. Figure~\ref{fig:prior} makes the dependence explicit. Completeness is prevalence-independent
and stays at $\bmlCompleteness$; but at the fixed operating threshold the purity falls from the
$\bmlPurity$ we report at the synthetic prevalence to $\bmlPurityOnePct$ at a $1\%$ anomaly
prevalence and $\bmlPurityTenthPct$ at $0.1\%$. The $\bmlPurity$ figure should therefore be read as
conditional on the adopted mixture, not as a survey purity; establishing the latter requires cited
event-rate priors for the specific input population, which we do not attempt here. This is exactly
why we frame completeness at fixed purity, rather than a single accuracy, as the reportable
quantity---and why the completeness, not the purity, is the number that transfers.

It is fair to ask what a survey team could actually achieve at $0.90$ purity if they re-tuned the
threshold for their own prevalence, rather than what our fixed threshold delivers. Re-optimising on
the frozen artifact: at the synthetic mixture's prevalence the answer is $\bmlPrevCompSynth$,
essentially our headline; at a $1\%$ anomaly prevalence it falls to $\bmlPrevCompOne$; and at
$0.1\%$ to $\bmlPrevCompTenth$. The degradation is intrinsic---holding purity fixed as the
contaminant pool grows requires a stricter threshold, which costs completeness---and any deployment
should plan against the figure matching its own input prevalence, not against $\bmlCompleteness$.

A related caveat concerns the photometric calibration of the colour bands. Our simulated
zeropoints were anchored to the published Cycle-7 values for the wide band and scaled for F087 and
F213, and an audit against the current Roman zeropoint tables \citep{ROTAC2025} shows the wide-band
value is accurate to $0.03$~mag while our F087 and F213 zeropoints are optimistic by roughly $0.10$
and $0.14$~mag respectively. Two further colour-band assumptions are less defensible: our F087
saturation limit was carried over from a much longer Cycle-7 exposure and is almost certainly
wrong in the wrong direction (at equal exposure F087 should saturate at a \emph{fainter} magnitude
than F146, not a brighter one), and our sky-background ratios do not reproduce the published
thermal backgrounds, which make F213 substantially worse and F087 substantially better relative to
F146 than we assume. These errors sit in the colour bands, which the model uses chiefly to reject variable-star
contaminants. We deliberately do not characterise the resulting bias as small: we have not
quantified it, and doing so requires regenerating the training set and retraining, or at minimum a
colour/noise ablation. The affected quantity is most plausibly contaminant rejection rather than
the anomaly channel, but that expectation is untested. The corrected values are recorded alongside the
originals in the released code so that any future retraining starts from the audited numbers.

One assumption underlying every number in this paper deserves to be stated plainly, because it is
the most consequential modelling choice we make. The boundaries between our classes---flat versus
event, and single lens versus anomalous---are set not only by a goodness-of-fit threshold but also
by a minimum detectable amplitude, which we fix at twenty times an assumed one-millimagnitude
systematic floor. This floor stands in for the correlated, night-to-week photometric systematics
that do not average down the way independent per-epoch noise does, and which no real pipeline can
follow below some level; we adopt a fixed amplitude cut rather than modelling a full red-noise
process. It is, however, a modelling choice and not a measured property of \Rom{}, and because it
sits on both class boundaries at once, the absolute completeness and purity we quote will shift if
the true systematic floor differs from our assumption. The \emph{relative} results---the size of
the label surgery, the direction and magnitude of the cascade effect, the shape of the parameter
dependence---are far less sensitive to it, but a quantitative recalibration of this floor against a
real \Rom{} red-noise estimate is a prerequisite before the absolute numbers are used to set a
survey requirement.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

BinML is built to be a triage layer for the \Rom{} GBTDS: a cheap first pass that flags candidate
microlensing and anomalous events for the expensive, definitive step of full light-curve modelling.
Two properties make it suited to that role rather than merely accurate on a static benchmark. The
completeness-at-fixed-purity framing lets a survey team set the operating point directly against
their follow-up budget, trading completeness for purity along a curve they control rather than
accepting whatever an argmax delivers. And the real-time cascade means the flag can be trusted as a
\emph{trigger}: a \texttt{NonPSPL} call is raised, in the large majority of cases, only after the anomaly is
observable rather than in anticipation of it, which is the precondition for autonomous follow-up. That BinML reconstructs the
truth-informed anomaly ranking from raw photometry in a single forward pass, without per-event
fitting, is what makes running it across the full survey stream computationally feasible.

\subsection{Targeted fine-tuning recovers the low-mass-ratio deficit}\label{sec:finetune}

The mass-ratio breakdown of \S\ref{sec:results} identifies where the model is weakest, and it is
natural to ask whether that deficit is intrinsic or simply a matter of training exposure. We tested
this directly: starting from the shipped checkpoint, we fine-tuned for six epochs at a reduced
learning rate on data drawn from the planetary regime ($10^{-6}<q<10^{-3}$), then scored both the
shipped and the fine-tuned model on the \emph{same} $\bmlFtEvalN$-event natural-mixture set
generated from a seed base neither had seen.

The deficit is largely recoverable. Completeness at the fixed operating threshold rises from
$\bmlFtNeptuneBefore$ to $\bmlFtNeptuneAfter$ in the Neptune-mass decade ($N=\bmlFtNeptuneN$) and
from $\bmlFtGiantBefore$ to $\bmlFtGiantAfter$ for giant planets ($N=\bmlFtGiantN$), both
significant at $\gtrsim3\sigma$; for the lowest mass ratios it rises from $\bmlFtLowmassBefore$ to
$\bmlFtLowmassAfter$, though with only $N=\bmlFtLowmassN$ such events in this evaluation set that
particular figure is suggestive ($\sim$$1.7\sigma$) rather than established. Crucially, nothing
degrades: stellar-binary completeness is unchanged within noise
($\bmlFtStellarBefore\rightarrow\bmlFtStellarAfter$), and no per-class F1 falls by more than
$\bmlFtWorstRegress$, with the contaminant classes improving slightly.

We draw a deliberately limited conclusion. This does not show the model is now good at low-mass
planets in absolute terms, and the fine-tuning set itself is thin---in the deep-planetary regime
only about one simulated binary in seven produces a detectable anomaly at all, so targeted
generation yields far fewer trainable examples than its raw event count suggests. What it does show
is that the mass-ratio deficit reported above is substantially a matter of training exposure rather
than an intrinsic limit of the architecture or the photometry.

\subsection{Which failures targeted training \emph{cannot} fix}\label{sec:hardregime}

The natural follow-up is whether the same treatment repairs the other weak regimes of
\S\ref{sec:limits}. We repeated the experiment at larger scale, fine-tuning on a mixture of eleven
hard regimes chosen to cover every documented failure---wide and resonant separations, short and
long timescales, faint and flat-rich fields, and the difficult contaminant populations---and scored
both models per class on out-of-range evaluation sets built where each failure actually occurs.

The outcome is informative precisely because it is not uniform. Two failures barely move.
\texttt{PeriodicVar} recall for variables whose period exceeds the observing season goes from
$\bmlHrLongpPerBefore$ to $\bmlHrLongpPerAfter$, and \texttt{NonPSPL} recall for very wide
separations from $\bmlHrWidesepNpBefore$ to $\bmlHrWidesepNpAfter$---no change at all, despite
training data drawn specifically from those regimes. Both have an obvious physical reading: a
variable whose period is longer than the window is not observably periodic within it, and a wide
caustic that is never crossed leaves no anomaly in the photometry. We are careful about the
strength of this claim: it rests on a single fine-tuning run without repeated seeds, and the
wide-separation cell contains only $\bmlHrWidesepN$ detectable anomalies, so the evidence is that
targeted training \emph{did not} help under this protocol---consistent with an information limit,
but not a demonstration of one. Two other failures do respond, modestly: \texttt{PSPL}
recall improves from $\bmlHrShortteBefore$ to $\bmlHrShortteAfter$ on sub-day timescales and from
$\bmlHrFaintPsplBefore$ to $\bmlHrFaintPsplAfter$ for faint sources.

The fine-tune also rebalances precision against recall rather than improving both. The clearest
case is \texttt{LongPeriodVar} in faint fields, where precision rises sharply from
$\bmlHrFaintLpvPrecBefore$ to $\bmlHrFaintLpvPrecAfter$ while recall falls from
$\bmlHrFaintLpvRecBefore$ to $\bmlHrFaintLpvRecAfter$; anomaly precision on the natural mixture
drifts down slightly ($\bmlHrNatNpPrecBefore\rightarrow\bmlHrNatNpPrecAfter$). Aggressive multi-regime
fine-tuning is therefore not a free improvement, unlike the narrowly targeted planetary case.

Taken together the two experiments motivate a working distinction, offered as a hypothesis these
results support rather than as a proven dichotomy: some shortfalls are deficits of training
exposure and respond strongly to targeted data (the mass-ratio deficit, recovered at no measurable
cost), while others resisted the same treatment entirely and coincide with regimes where the
observable signal is plausibly absent within a single season. Establishing the latter as a genuine
information limit would need repeated seeds, larger samples in the affected cells, and ideally an
explicit estimate of the recoverable information; we have not done that here. What we can say is
which limitations moved under targeted training and which did not, which is the practically useful
guidance for anyone extending this model.

The natural next step is validation on real events in the model's own regime. As shown in
\S\ref{sec:limits}, this cannot be done from the ground---even KMTNet's dense but nightly-gapped
sampling defeats the model---so it genuinely awaits \Rom{} itself, whose continuous cadence is what
the model is built for. We stress that this is not a request to make BinML cadence-agnostic: a
model tuned to \Rom{}'s sampling should stay tuned to it, and sparse archival data is better served
by simpler single-band classifiers. The one weak
spot with real follow-up implications---faint-source false anomalies---is the obvious thing to watch
in that validation, should it matter in practice; the remaining limits sit close enough to the
physics that we expect little return from chasing them.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented BinML, a compact multi-band transformer classifier for \Rom{}-like GBTDS light
curves, built around two ideas---detectability-conditioned labelling and a real-time anomaly-onset
cascade---that make it a candidate for prospective survey use rather than only accurate on a static
benchmark. On the adopted synthetic class mixture it reaches a completeness of $\bmlCompleteness$ at
a purity of $\bmlPurity$ (average precision $\bmlAP$), reconstructs the truth-informed anomaly
ranking from raw photometry, and issues its first anomaly alert a median of
$\bmlCascMedianLag$~days after the caustic becomes detectable. These results establish a reproducible synthetic benchmark; validation
against survey event-rate priors and the current \Rom{} survey design remains future work. The
model, its weights, and the full simulation-to-inference pipeline are released as open source.

\appendix

\section{Simulation and training infrastructure}\label{app:infra}

Because reproducibility and the real computational cost of a method are as much a part of a survey
tool as its accuracy, we describe here exactly how the data were generated and the model trained,
and what it cost.

\paragraph{Simulation (AWS).} The training set of roughly $\bmlSimEvents$~million events was
generated on Amazon Web Services in the \texttt{\bmlSimRegion} region, on a small fleet of
$\bmlSimInstances$ transient (Spot) instances of $\bmlSimVcpus$ virtual CPUs each. The generator is
embarrassingly parallel: one process builds one self-contained shard of a few thousand light
curves, seeded purely by its index, so a shard that is lost to a Spot interruption costs at most
that shard and the run resumes by skipping shards already written to cloud storage. At a measured
throughput of about $\bmlSimEps$ events per second across the fleet, the full set was produced in
roughly $\bmlSimHours$~hours. Because the instances are free-tier-eligible Spot capacity, the
compute cost of a full generation is at the level of cents; the only non-trivial cost is storage of
the compact binned shards, a few dollars per month.

\paragraph{Training recipe.} The production model was trained with AdamW (learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $0.01$), batch size $\bmlTrainBatch$, cross-entropy loss with the
selection weights of \S\ref{sec:eval} and an additional factor $2$ on the anomaly class, and the
truncation augmentation applied to half of all training examples. Seeds are fixed
($\bmlSeedTrain$) and the data loader is seeded per worker. Fine-tuning runs use a reduced learning
rate of $5\times10^{-5}$.

\paragraph{Training (local, with a cloud comparison).} The network is small enough that it was
trained end to end on a personal computer, on the \bmlTrainDevice, at a batch size of
$\bmlTrainBatch$. A benchmark of the training step (forward and backward pass on a full batch)
sustains about $\bmlTrainEps$ events per second, which puts a full training run of a few passes
over the $\bmlSimEvents$-million-event set at roughly $\bmlTrainHours$~hours of wall-clock, and the
production model was trained in this range. On local hardware no compute is rented, so the marginal
training cost is only electricity; a modest cloud GPU (for example on AWS or Modal) would train the
same model faster for a few dollars. The compactness of the network (\S\ref{sec:model}) is what
keeps both training and survey-scale inference within reach of a single workstation rather than
requiring a dedicated computing allocation.
