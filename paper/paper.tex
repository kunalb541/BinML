\begin{abstract}
The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will produce more microlensing light curves than can be
inspected manually. We present \textbf{BinML}, a compact ($\bmlNparams$-parameter) neural network
for triaging synthetic \Rom{}-like light curves into six classes: no detectable event, single-lens
microlensing, detectable binary-lens anomalies (\texttt{NonPSPL}), and three broad variable-star
classes. Labels are conditioned on explicit synthetic detectability thresholds and on the portion
of the season revealed to the model. BinML recovers $\bmlCompletenessPct\%$ of detectable
\texttt{NonPSPL} events at a target purity of $\bmlPurityTarget$ (achieved $\bmlPurity$) on
complete 72-day light curves in a held-out $\bmlNtest$-event test set whose detectable-anomaly
prevalence is $\bmlPriorPiZero\%$; at a $1\%$ prevalence the same threshold yields purity
$\bmlPurityOnePct$, or completeness $\bmlPrevCompOne$ at fixed purity. A disjoint
$\bmlStressNatN$-million-event set reproduces the complete-season macro-F1; out-of-distribution tests
expose failures, worst on faint sources, where anomaly precision falls to $\bmlStressFaintPrec$.
Revealed in half-day steps, $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of $\bmlCascN$ eligible binaries alert within the
season, $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ (95\% CI $[\bmlCascPrematureLo,\bmlCascPrematureHi]\%$) before a
truth-informed, noise-free onset and the other alerts a median $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days after
it; scanning every class with all three bands gives $\bmlRefStreamAlertsDay$ alerts per 1,000 events per day,
$\bmlRefStreamPurity\%$ of them detectable anomalies at the simulated prevalence ($\bmlRefStreamPurOne\%$ at
1\%; $\bmlRefStreamFPurity\%$ and $\bmlRefStreamFPurOne\%$ with F146 alone). The latency suits
post-hoc modelling priority rather than triggering on hours-long perturbations. These are benchmark
values for a continuous 15-minute schedule and an adopted $0.02$~mag detectability floor, not \Rom{}
yield forecasts. On RMDC26, an independent GULLS simulation of the survey made for the Roman
Microlensing Data Challenge 2026 ($\bmlGullsN$ selected events, none used for training), the shipped
checkpoint flags $\bmlGullsFaShipped\%$ of single lenses per simulated event as anomalous, mostly because our training
seasons lack the survey's F146 observing pauses. Fine-tuning on our own simulations with gap
augmentation, finite-source single lenses and the measured pauses raises planetary recall at a
$\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ single-lens false-alarm budget from $\bmlGullsRecShipped$ to
$\bmlSeedRecLo$--$\bmlSeedRecHi$ per simulated event across three training seeds
($\bmlGullsRecWShipped$ to $\bmlSeedRecWLo$--$\bmlSeedRecWHi$ weighted by event rate); RMDC26 also
guided the diagnosis and the choice of checkpoint, so these values are optimistic, and
$\bmlGullsUndetPlanet$--$\bmlGullsUndetPlanetBin\%$ of the planetary events we checked carry no anomaly our
labelling policy would claim within one season. Code, weights, and reproducibility artifacts are released openly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gravitational microlensing complements other planet-detection methods because it responds to a
lens's mass rather than its light. It is sensitive to cold, low-mass planets far from their host
stars and to unbound planetary-mass objects drifting through the Galaxy---populations that transit
and radial-velocity surveys, biased toward hot and close-in planets, recover less readily
\citep{Mao1991,GouldLoeb1992,Suzuki2016}. The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will exploit
this sensitivity systematically. Its Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey (GBTDS) will monitor the
crowded bulge fields at high cadence in the near-infrared. It is predicted to detect of order a
thousand bound planets and to constrain the free-floating planet mass function
\citep{Spergel2015,Penny2019,Johnson2020,Sumi2023,Gaudi2012}, and the same data will yield
transiting planets \citep{Wilson2023}. In its design
\citep{ROTAC2025,STScI2025GBTDS}, the wide F146 filter samples the light curve every
$\sim12$~minutes; two auxiliary bands, F087 and F213, add colour information on staggered
$\sim$6-hour cycles.

That sensitivity brings an operational problem of scale. Genuine microlensing events are a small
minority of the survey light curves, detectable binary-lens anomalies are rarer still, and fitting
a full binary-lens model to every source is impractical. A cheap classifier can therefore rank
which partial light curves deserve inspection or detailed modelling. Timing matters to that task:
before a caustic perturbation appears, a binary lens can resemble an ordinary single lens. A model
evaluated only on complete seasons can assign an anomalous label before the evidence that defines
it has entered the observed window. Existing rapid-response systems already show the value of
low-latency anomaly detection in microlensing \citep{Udalski2003,Dominik2007,Dominik2008,Tsapras2009}.
Here we ask a narrower question: as an eligible synthetic binary is progressively revealed, when
does the classifier first cross an anomaly threshold relative to a truth-informed onset?

Machine learning has already been used at the scale relevant to microlensing surveys.
\citet{Wyrzykowski2015} searched 150 million OGLE-III sources and classified 3,718 candidates, while
\citet{Husseiniova2021} applied a machine-learning search to roughly 700 million VVV light curves;
both were completed-data searches. The random-forest microlensing classifier of \citet{Godines2019} and neural networks on
ground-based photometry \citep{Mroz2020} address related classification tasks. KMTNet's completed-event finder \citep{Kim2018EventFinder} and the subsequent
\textsc{AnomalyFinder} analyses \citep{Zang2022}, which search residuals from fitted single-lens
models, demonstrate systematic event and anomaly discovery in real survey data. For simulated high-cadence \Rom{} light curves,
\citet{Khakpash2021} developed morphology and model-fit features intended for rapid classification.
In the alert-broker setting, Fink's LIA module
performs real-time microlensing classification but does not include a binary-anomaly class
\citep{Moller2021}. For \Rom{}, \citet{Zhang2021} demonstrated real-time posterior inference for a
known binary-lens event; that is downstream parameter inference, not discovery in the full stellar
stream. Most closely, \citet{CrispimRomao2025} evaluated an isolation-forest alarm on drip-fed
synthetic LSST light curves containing microlensing, binary lenses, and variables. Their online
stage separates signal from constant sources and their physical classification is offline; our
contribution is a supervised six-class \Rom{}-like model with time-dependent labels. Related
time-domain work also studies early classification of explosive transients
\citep{Muthukrishna2019}. Architecturally we use standard time-series transformer components
\citep{Astromer2023,Vaswani2017}, and our evaluation shares the prior-shift concern highlighted by
PLAsTiCC \citep{Hlozek2020}.

Across this work we find no trained classifier that combines all of the elements BinML needs:
multi-band \Rom{}-cadence input, a binary and planetary anomaly class evaluated alongside the
variable-star populations that contaminate it, operation on partial rather than completed light
curves, and released weights. The searches above are single-band, completed-data, or
binary-blind; the \Rom{}-specific work stops at feature definition or at downstream inference
for an already-identified event. To our knowledge BinML is the first released model to occupy
that combination, which is what the rest of this paper evaluates.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we introduce \emph{detectability-conditioned
labelling} (\S\ref{sec:sims}): events are labelled by whether they cross adopted synthetic
thresholds, aligning the training target with that synthetic policy.
Second, we build a \emph{partial-light-curve cascade} (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) by making each label a
function of how much of the season has been revealed. Third, we evaluate the model at synthetic
survey scale (\S\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:limits}):
completeness at a fixed purity as the headline metric, a selection-aware reweighting that corrects
for the training distribution, and a $\bmlStressN$-million-event stress collection spanning an
independent same-prior sample and targeted out-of-range regimes. Fourth, we test transfer to RMDC26,
an independent simulation of the survey made with a different simulator (\S\ref{sec:gulls}): it
exposes a failure of the shipped model on the survey's observing schedule, which a fine-tune with gap
augmentation reduces.

\subsection{Six classes across three bands}
We simulate \Rom{} GBTDS light curves in the six selected classes of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}.
\texttt{Flat} sources show no
detectable event. \texttt{PSPL} events are single-lens, point-source point-lens microlensing
\citep{Paczynski1986}. \texttt{NonPSPL} events---the anomalous, science-critical class---are
binary and planetary lenses, computed with the contour-integration code \textsc{VBBinaryLensing}
\citep{Bozza2010,Bozza2018}, and are distinguished by the caustic perturbations visible in the
third panel of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}. The remaining three classes are the variable-star
populations that dominate the real bulge and are the classifier's most dangerous contaminants:
\texttt{PeriodicVar} (RR~Lyrae, eclipsing binaries, $\delta$~Scuti), \texttt{LongPeriodVar}
(Miras, semiregulars, and OSARGs---small-amplitude red-giant pulsators---whose slow rise most
readily mimics a long-timescale microlensing event), and \texttt{Eruptive} (dwarf novae and Be
outbursts). We adopt an authored truncated log-normal event-timescale distribution whose mean is
anchored to an empirical inner-bulge estimate \citep{Mroz2019}. Binary mass ratio and projected
separation are instead drawn log-uniformly over broad analytic supports; they are not a fitted
population model \citep{Suzuki2016,Zhang2021}. Variable-star periods and amplitudes are motivated
by OGLE bulge catalogues, but the generated curves are phenomenological analytic waveform families
rather than sampled empirical templates \citep{Soszynski2013,Soszynski2014}. Every event is realised
on a hybrid \Rom{}-like schedule: legacy-like $\sim$15-minute wide-band sampling and a single
72-day season, combined with 6-hour visits in each of the current F087 and F213 auxiliary filters.
The curves are degraded by photometric noise, blending, and interstellar extinction. The released
model was trained on this legacy schedule (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

The source population is likewise parametric rather than a Galactic population synthesis. The
observed F146 baseline is uniform from 20 to 25~mag and $A_{K_s}$ is uniform from 0.2 to 1.2,
sampled independently. Baseline colour offsets come from a grey intrinsic source spectrum plus the
adopted extinction law; intrinsic stellar colour and its correlations with luminosity, variability,
and line of sight are not modelled. These choices give a classifier benchmark broad coverage.

The \texttt{NonPSPL} generator contains static binary lenses (2L1S) with finite-source
magnification, but not binary-source (1L2S) events, annual parallax, or lens orbital motion. The
1L2S omission is the most consequential of the three: a binary source produces a bump on an
otherwise single-lens curve that can mimic a binary-lens anomaly, and it is a long-recognised
degeneracy with planetary-mass 2L1S perturbations \citep{Gaudi1998}. It is therefore the dominant
astrophysical false positive that our purity estimate does not contain, and adding it would
lower the reported \texttt{NonPSPL} purity by an amount we have not measured. Annual parallax
and lens orbital motion distort the light-curve shape but do not manufacture a caustic-like
feature, so we expect them to matter less over a single 72-day season. The contaminant set
likewise omits many real irregular and correlated systematics.

\subsection{Labelling by an adopted synthetic detectability policy}\label{sec:detcond}
BinML's policy-conditioned synthetic labels are set by explicit thresholds applied to simulated,
noise-free curves, rather than by generator class alone; we use ``detectability-conditioned'' as
shorthand for this policy. A microlensing event whose in-window amplitude falls below the adopted
floor is labelled \texttt{Flat}. Likewise, a binary whose caustic perturbation falls below the
anomaly thresholds is labelled \texttt{PSPL}. For binaries we measure the departure of the
noiseless curve from a refitted single-lens model and withhold the anomalous label when that
departure is too small under the policy.

The class definitions---and therefore every metric in this paper---depend on these thresholds. An
event must reach $\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{event}} \ge 500$ against a flat baseline to be a
detected event at all; a binary must reach $\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{anom}} \ge 160$ against its
best-fitting single-lens model to keep the anomalous label; and both statistics must exceed a peak
amplitude of $0.02$~mag. Both $\Delta\chi^{2}$ values are computed on noise-free curves, so neither
is a likelihood-ratio statistic: under the null each is identically zero, and each grows in
proportion to the epoch count. At \Rom{}'s sampling that makes both $\chi^{2}$ cuts close to
vestigial for our error model and leaves the $0.02$~mag amplitude floor as the operative criterion. That floor is an
adopted simulation policy rather than a measured instrumental limit. Section~\ref{sec:ablations}
measures what the labelling scheme buys against the alternative of labelling by generator intent.

The scale of the relabelling is the point. Across the test set, $\bmlRelabelled\%$ of events
receive a policy-conditioned label that differs from the class they were generated as. The effect is
concentrated where it should be. Of the events generated as binary or planetary lenses, only
$\bmlNonpsplKept\%$ show a detectable anomaly and keep the anomalous label; $\bmlNonpsplToPspl\%$
have a caustic perturbation below those thresholds and are labelled as single lenses. Similarly,
$\bmlLpvToFlat\%$ of the long-period variables vary too weakly within a single season to be
detected and are labelled flat. Training against generator
intent asks the network to assign an anomalous or variable label on about a third of its examples
even though the corresponding signal falls below the adopted policy threshold.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{outputs/figures/schematic.pdf}
\caption{BinML architecture. Each of the three \Rom{} bands is binned onto a fixed grid whose
bins carry five channels---baseline-relative mean, min, and max magnitude, observed fraction, and
an observed mask. A convolutional stem, whose non-learned min/max lanes preserve caustic extrema
by construction, reduces the bands to $\bmlNtokens$ tokens that a four-layer transformer
classifies into six classes. The whole model has $\bmlNparams$ parameters.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure*}

BinML has two parts (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}). The first is a small convolutional network---a
stack of learned filters that scan along the light curve looking for local shapes, the same kind
of building block used in image recognition. The second is a transformer, a now-standard
architecture that lets every part of the light curve ``attend to'' every other part, so the model
can weigh, for example, a brief brightening against the overall trend around it. Together they
come to just $\bmlNparams$ trainable numbers, which by the standards of modern deep learning is
tiny. The small size is deliberate. We are not short of training data---we simulate of order ten
million events---so the pressure is not to build a larger model but to build one cheap enough to
run on the entire survey, night after night. A compact network that classifies a light curve in a
single pass is far more useful here than a heavy one applied only to a pre-selected handful of
candidates.

The model reads the light curve in a form that keeps the survey's own timing. Each of the three
filters is divided into a fixed set of time bins across the 72-day observing season: the workhorse
F146 filter into $\bmlBinsF$ bins (from $\bmlEpochsF$ individual exposures taken every
$\bmlCadence$~minutes), and the two colour filters into 96 bins each. For every bin the model is
given five simple numbers: the average brightness in that bin, the brightest and faintest points
in it, the fraction of the bin that was actually observed, and a flag saying whether it was
observed at all. All brightnesses are measured relative to a single quiet-state (baseline)
level defined in F146 and subtracted from all three filters. This removes absolute source
brightness while retaining the simulated inter-band offsets, including the effects of extinction
and blending, but not an independently measured intrinsic source colour.

The training and frozen evaluations use the simulator-provided F146 baseline. A deployed pipeline
would estimate that level from a partial, possibly magnified light curve; the released inference
interface includes an estimator, whose error we have not evaluated against the generator value.

Keeping the brightest and faintest point in each bin, rather than only the average, retains narrow
caustic structure during time binning. In the largest measured case, mean pooling
would reduce a nearly three-magnitude excursion to about a third of a magnitude. We therefore
propagate exact extrema through each strided reduction stage before a learned projection combines
the channels; the extrema are not supplied unchanged to the final classifier. When a colour filter
is missing for an event---the
bluer F087 filter yields no usable epoch for $\bmlNoFilterBlue\%$ of detectable-anomaly
events behind heavy dust---the model is simply told to
ignore it rather than being fed a guess. A final pooling step combines the processed bins into a
single summary, from which a standard classifier reads off the six class probabilities. The model
uses only the binned brightnesses; it does not use the individual error bars.

This compactness makes repeated survey-scale scoring practical. Classification is a single forward
pass with no per-event fitting. On a $\bmlInferCores$-core \bmlInferCpu{} CPU with
$\bmlInferThreads$ threads, the model scores $\bmlInferEps$ pre-tokenised light curves per second
($\bmlInferMs$~ms per event) in batches of 1024. \code{validation/inference\_benchmark.py}
regenerates this together with the processor, thread count and timing protocol; an earlier
revision quoted a figure roughly three times larger whose hardware and script were not retained
and which we could not reproduce, so it is withdrawn.

\section{Partial-light-curve evaluation}\label{sec:cascade}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{outputs/figures/cascade_evolution.pdf}

revealed (left to right in each panel). \emph{Top:} the light curve in the bands with surviving epochs
(F146 grey, F087 blue, F213 red; F087 is undetected for the clean binary). The dashed vertical line in both rows marks the anomaly-onset day
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ for the binaries. \emph{Bottom:} BinML's class
probability as a function of how much of the season it has seen. For the clean binary (left),
probability flows \texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}, with the
jump to \texttt{NonPSPL} occurring near the truth-informed simulated onset---not
during the smooth rise before it. The subtle binary (centre) shows the same ordering with a late,
weak anomaly, and the single-lens control (right) acquires no appreciable \texttt{NonPSPL}
probability. \emph{These three events were selected to illustrate the intended behaviour and are
not evidence for it}; the aggregate evaluation in \S\ref{sec:cascade} measures the first
\texttt{NonPSPL} threshold crossing on eligible binaries and, separately, the alert rate over every
class, not the full three-state sequence.}
\label{fig:cascade}
\end{figure*}

A streaming classifier receives a partial rather than a finished light curve. Before an anomalous
feature appears, a binary lens may be photometrically consistent with a single lens, so assigning
the complete-season class to every prefix would teach the network a target unsupported by the
revealed window.

We instead make the label a function of observation time. For each binary we compute the day
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ at which its anomaly first crosses the adopted thresholds. This reference is
truth-informed: it is derived from the simulated, noise-free light curve and a single-lens fit
seeded with injected parameters, rather than from an observable broker statistic. During training
the network sees light curves truncated at random days: a
binary truncated \emph{before} $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ carries the \texttt{PSPL} label (with the
detectability floor re-evaluated on the truncated data), and earns \texttt{NonPSPL} only once the
anomaly lies inside the revealed window. In the training data $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ is resolved
only to the end of the $7.2$-day interval in which the anomaly first became detectable, so the
training label switches to \texttt{NonPSPL} up to $7.2$ days after the true onset---a
conservative bias that almost always delays the label (it can advance it where detectability is not
monotone in the revealed time; see below); the evaluation onsets in
Section~\ref{sec:cascade} are resolved at $0.5$~days and do not inherit it. The resulting training target follows the cascade
\texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}.

The prefix labels for other classes are also generator-informed. Single-lens amplitude is
evaluated from injected parameters, an eruptive source changes class at its injected start time,
and periodic or long-period variability uses an analytic amplitude--period proxy. That proxy does
not evaluate the realised waveform, phase, noisy measurements, or irregular sampling. We later measured
these approximations against the label rule itself on one natural-prior training shard ($\bmlTruthN$
events, $\bmlTruthReps$ truncations each), regenerated with the noise-free signal stored per 2-hour bin
and with the onset also recorded on a half-day grid. For a binary the reference refits the single-lens
model to the noise-free curve's revealed epochs and applies the anomaly thresholds, as the generator does.
The released training labels differ from it for $\bmlTruthTruncNonLegacy\%$ of truncated binary
presentations, about half of them ($\bmlTruthTruncNonLate\%$) still labelled \texttt{PSPL} after the anomaly
had become detectable, and from the rule's amplitude and event thresholds for $\bmlTruthTruncLpv\%$,
$\bmlTruthTruncPer\%$, $\bmlTruthTruncPspl\%$ and $\bmlTruthTruncErupt\%$ of truncated long-period,
periodic, single-lens and eruptive presentations. The released model is trained with these labels. With the
stored per-bin signal for the thresholds and the half-day onset for the anomaly, the disagreement for
binaries falls to $\bmlTruthTruncNonFixed\%$, the cases where detectability is not monotone in the revealed
time (a further $\bmlTruthUnstable\%$ are refits too unstable to call).

Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} shows the cascade at the level of individual events. As the clean binary
is revealed, BinML's probability mass moves from \texttt{Flat} to \texttt{PSPL} during the smooth
magnification and only jumps to \texttt{NonPSPL} when the caustic crosses into view at
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$; the subtle binary follows the same sequence with a weaker, later anomaly; and
the single-lens control never acquires meaningful \texttt{NonPSPL} probability.

\paragraph{Definitions.} We fix four terms and keep the same denominators throughout. An
\emph{eligible event} is a simulated binary whose anomaly survives detectability-conditioned
labelling and has a finite onset time; eligibility never depends on the model's output. A
\emph{threshold crossing} is a revealed window at which $P(\texttt{NonPSPL})$ reaches the operating
threshold. The \emph{alert} is the first such crossing; an event with no crossing before the season
ends is \emph{right-censored}. An alert is \emph{premature} if it precedes the onset, and the
\emph{lag} is the alert time minus the onset time, negative for premature alerts.

\paragraph{Protocol.} We measure event by event rather than on isolated snapshots, because a live
stream re-evaluates every light curve as each new epoch arrives. For each of $\bmlCascN$ eligible
binaries we reveal the season in half-day steps and record the alert. The primary scan reveals
\emph{only the F146 band}; revealing all three bands is reported separately below. All
sensitivities are the same $\bmlCascN$ events reduced under different rules, from one stored scan.
The scan holds prefix traces only for eligible binaries---none for demoted binaries, single lenses,
flat sources, or variables---and its operating threshold was selected on complete-season validation
light curves. It therefore measures detection and timing conditional on binary eligibility; the
sequential false-alert rate, purity and alert burden come from a second scan over every class, at the end
of this section.

\paragraph{When does the anomaly begin?} The generator records the truth-informed onset on a coarse
$7.2$-day grid, which rounds it \emph{upward}: an alert arriving within that window is scored
premature even though the anomaly was already detectable. The median inflation is
$\bmlCascOnsetShift$~days. Worse, the anomaly statistic is not monotone in revealed time---as more
of the season arrives, the best-fitting single-lens model can absorb an early deviation and push
the residual back below threshold, which happens for $\bmlCascNonMonotone$ of our $\bmlCascN$
events. Part of this is numerical rather than physical: the single-lens refit is a local optimisation
seeded at the injected parameters, and for rare borderline events its answer changes under
perturbations of the seed at the $10^{-7}$ level. ``The onset'' is therefore ambiguous, and we bracket it
rather than choose:
\emph{first-detectable} is the first half-day cut at which the anomaly is detectable at all, and
\emph{persistently-detectable} is the cut after which it stays detectable to the end of the season.
The first is permissive, the second strict; we report both.

the remaining $\bmlCascCensored$ are right-censored. The alert precedes the anomaly onset for
$\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ of eligible events (95\% CI
$[\bmlCascPrematureLo,\bmlCascPrematureHi]\%$) under the first-detectable onset, rising to
$\bmlCascPrematurePersistPct\%$ ($[\bmlCascPrematurePersistLo,\bmlCascPrematurePersistHi]\%$) under

$\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days (first-detectable) or $\bmlCascMedianLagPersist$~days (persistent).
Because premature alerts are a small fraction, the median over \emph{all} $\bmlCascNdet$ alerts is
also $\bmlCascMedianLagAll$~days.

\paragraph{How the alert rule changes the answer.} Because the whole scan is stored, we can vary
the decision rule on the same events. Coarsening the evaluation grid from half-daily to daily
leaves the premature rate at $\bmlCascGridOnePct\%$ but costs detection
($\bmlCascGridOneDet\%$); at two-day steps it falls to $\bmlCascGridTwoPct\%$, again with fewer
detections. Requiring two consecutive crossings before alerting---one plausible debouncing
rule---gives $\bmlCascPersistTwoPct\%$ premature at $\bmlCascPersistTwoDet\%$ detection.
Revealing all three bands rather than F146 alone reduces the premature fraction to

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:eval}

Three choices define what the synthetic benchmark measures.

The first is how we summarise performance. A single accuracy figure is misleading here, because
the anomalous events are so rare that a model could score well simply by never flagging one. What
a follow-up team actually needs to know is a pair of numbers. One is \emph{purity}: of the events
the model flags as anomalous, what fraction really are. The other is \emph{completeness}: of the
anomalous events that exist, what fraction the model catches. These are precisely the quantities
tabulated as precision and recall in Table~\ref{tab:perclass}, and their harmonic mean is the F1
score there (macro-F1 being its unweighted average over the six classes). These trade off against each
other---flag more events and you catch more real ones but also let in more false ones---so we fix
the purity to a chosen value, $\bmlPurityTarget$, and report the completeness reached at that
setting as our headline number. This is exactly the form in which a survey team would state a
requirement: ``keep the flagged anomaly sample at least ninety per cent pure, and report how many
detectable anomalies remain.'' This is not the same as planet completeness because
\texttt{NonPSPL} also contains stellar binaries.

The second is a partial correction for how the training set was built. To train efficiently we
subsample the abundant demoted-binary by-products, and each test event carries a weight that
reverses \emph{that} subsampling, applied to purity (which depends on the mix of false positives)
but not to completeness (a per-class property). The weighting undoes one internal sampling step; it
does \emph{not} reweight the deliberately chosen generator class quotas to a survey event-rate
prior.

The third is a reference to judge the anomaly ranking against. For each event we can compute, from
the noiseless light curve and the true parameters, its injected anomaly \dchisq{}---in effect how
detectable the anomaly would be to an observer who already knew the answer. This is a
\emph{truth-informed reference}, not an oracle upper bound: it uses information the survey never
has, and a learned model exploiting other morphology could in principle rank better on real, noisy
data. Comparing BinML against it measures how much of the injected
anomaly ordering the model reconstructs from the raw measurements alone.

\section{Generalisation and limits}\label{sec:limits}

Held-out numbers can in principle reflect memorised training examples, so we test generalisation
directly: both to unseen parameter draws from the same priors and, through the out-of-range regimes
below, to parts of parameter space the training distribution barely populates. The
$\bmlStressN$-million-event stress set is drawn from a disjoint pseudo-random stream---seed base
$\bmlSeedStress$ against $\bmlSeedTrain$ for the train/validation/test data---so its parameter
draws are independent of the training draws;
it comprises a $\bmlStressNatN$-million-event natural-population subset and a suite of deliberately
out-of-range regimes. On the natural-population subset BinML reproduces its held-out performance,
with a macro-F1 of $\bmlStressMacroF$.

The out-of-range regimes expose failures. Anomaly recall stays near its in-distribution value of
$\bmlStressNatNp$ across most regimes, but collapses to $\bmlStressWidesep$ for very wide
separations ($\bmlStressWidesepN$ retained wide-separation binaries; the out-of-range shards
carry far fewer swept-class events than their totals suggest, and we quote the swept-class
support for each number). This recall is conditional on an anomaly already passing the detectability policy, so
the collapse is a classifier failure on the retained wide-separation subset, not relabelling of
uncrossed caustics. Two contaminant regimes fail
outright: \texttt{PeriodicVar} examples whose period exceeds the season are recovered as
\texttt{PeriodicVar} only $\bmlStressLongp$ of the time ($\bmlStressLongpN$ events; a nearly
complete failure, since within one window they are not observably periodic), and sub-day-timescale
single lenses are recovered at $\bmlStressShortte$ ($\bmlStressShortteN$ events). For faint
sources, \texttt{PSPL} recall falls to $\bmlStressFaintPspl$ ($\bmlStressFaintPsplN$ events) and
anomaly \emph{precision} to $\bmlStressFaintPrec$ ($\bmlStressFaintNpN$ detectable
anomalies)---noise manufacturing spurious anomalies is the
single most consequential failure mode for a follow-up programme. In the low-$q$ regime,
recall holds at $\bmlStressPlanetNp$ but precision falls to $\bmlStressPlanetPrec$.

Each failure has a plausible physical explanation---too few epochs on the peak, a signal that does
not complete within the window, photometric noise comparable to the signal---and the low-$q$ deficit
in particular is a regime the training distribution barely populates. These are the current model's
operating envelope, not established limits of the method. Robustness to missing data is more
encouraging: events observed without the
blue band are still recovered at $\bmlSliceNoBlue$, so the model degrades gracefully when colour is
unavailable.

One boundary is deliberate: BinML is a specialist for dense, nearly continuous space-based
sampling. The non-learned min/max lanes, colour bands, and fine time grid were developed on the
hybrid schedule described above. Archival ground-survey light curves lie outside this evaluated
regime. Earlier ad-hoc applications to selected KMTNet and OGLE events are not treated as results
because their event selection and predictions were not retained in frozen, hashed artifacts.

We instead use simulation to isolate one relevant cadence effect. Existing ground networks such as
the three-site KMTNet \citep{Kim2016} observe through nightly windows separated by daytime gaps. A
matched-density test compares uniform sampling with an eight-hour nightly observing window
\emph{at identical total visit counts}, so that only the gap topology differs. Anomaly recall over
$\bmlGapN$ events is $\bmlGapUniformA$ (uniform; 95\% CI
$[\bmlGapUniformALo,\bmlGapUniformAHi]$) versus $\bmlGapNightlyA$ (gapped; upper bound
$\bmlGapNightlyAHi$) at $\bmlGapVisitsA$~visits per day, and $\bmlGapUniformB$ versus
$\bmlGapNightlyA$ at $\bmlGapVisitsB$~per day; the intervals do not overlap. At
$\bmlGapVisitsC$~visits per day both schedules fail ($\bmlGapUniformC$ uniform), so below that
density sparsity alone dominates and the comparison is uninformative. In the regime where uniform
sampling still works, the gapped schedule does not. This establishes gap sensitivity in the
simulation, not the cause of any behaviour on real data.

Our hybrid schedule---$\sim$15-minute F146 sampling with 6-hour visits in each colour filter over
a single 72-day season---is not an exact historical survey design. The current Galactic Bulge
Time-Domain Survey definition specifies F146 approximately every 12 minutes, F087 and F213 each on
a 6-hour cycle staggered so that one colour or the other is obtained every 3 hours, 66-second
exposures, and six high-cadence plus four low-cadence seasons of about 72 days each
\citep{STScI2025GBTDS,
ROTAC2025}. Our per-band colour cadence therefore already matches the current design; what differs
is the F146 rate, the exposure time and the photometric calibration that follows from it, the
absence of the staggered colour interleaving, the within-season F146 observing pauses (which, as shown
below, the shipped model cannot survive), and the multi-season structure. Our results are a benchmark under a legacy \Rom{}-like schedule, not a forecast under
the survey as it will actually run.


We can, however, bound how much the \emph{sampling rate} alone matters. We repeated the entire
pipeline---simulation, training from scratch, and evaluation---at the current $\sim$12-minute F146
rate and at our legacy 15-minute rate, holding everything else fixed (same shards, seed and
$\bmlCadEpochs$ epochs; $\bmlCadNfifteen$ and $\bmlCadNtwelve$ events survived the
detectability cuts in the two training pools). Each arm is scored on held-out events simulated
from disjoint seeds that never entered training ($\bmlCadEvalNfifteen$ and $\bmlCadEvalNtwelve$
survived the cuts in the two arms). The two arms score alike:
anomaly average precision $\bmlCadFifteenAp$ (15\,min) versus $\bmlCadTwelveAp$ (12\,min), with
no per-class F1 differing by more than $\bmlCadMaxFOneDelta$. Completeness at the purity target
is $\bmlCadFifteenCompleteness$ at an achieved purity of $\bmlCadFifteenPurity$ versus
$\bmlCadTwelveCompleteness$ at $\bmlCadTwelvePurity$; because the threshold search lands the
two arms at different achieved purities, average precision is the like-for-like comparison. With
one seed per arm this bounds the rate change as unimportant at the resolution we can measure
rather than establishing equivalence. (An earlier version of this experiment scored each arm on
a pool that overlapped its training data; that inflated the 15-minute AP to
$\bmlCadFifteenApTrainpool$ and the 12-minute AP to $\bmlCadTwelveApTrainpool$ and did not
change the contrast.) Both rates sample an hours-to-day
caustic densely, so a $25\%$ change in epoch count adds little information. Two further differences
are outside this experiment. The current design's 66-second exposure and its colour-band calibration
differ from ours; the colour calibration is tested below at our exposure time, and the exposure time
itself is not tested. More consequentially, the current design's \emph{structure} differs: observing pauses within each season, and six high-cadence plus four
low-cadence seasons separated by gaps. The pauses turn out to matter far more than the sampling rate
and can be handled by augmentation; the multi-season structure cannot be represented by our
single-season, fixed-window input without an architectural change.

\paragraph{The schedule, not the sampling rate, is what the shipped model cannot survive.}
Our training grid is continuous within a season. The survey as simulated in the Roman Microlensing Data Challenge 2026 (RMDC26) release of
the Roman Galactic Exoplanet Survey Project Infrastructure Team \citep{RGESPIT2026} is not: following the
survey design \citep{STScI2025GBTDS,ROTAC2025}, F146 pauses for $\bmlGullsPauseHoursLo$--$\bmlGullsPauseHoursHi$~h
per $\bmlGullsSeasonDays$-day season, in six or seven pauses whose phases differ between its six
high-cadence seasons (only those near days $\bmlGullsCommonPauseA$, $\bmlGullsCommonPauseB$ and
$\bmlGullsCommonPauseC$ recur), and the season is $\bmlGullsSeasonShort$ days shorter than our 72-day
window. Essentially none of our training events contains an empty mid-season F146 bin, so the model's
only prior for an empty token is the unrevealed future of a truncated season. Imposing the measured
pauses on our own held-out seasons, with nothing else changed, collapses the shipped model
(Table~\ref{tab:gap}): single-lens recall falls from $\bmlGapShippedPsplClean$ to
$\bmlGapShippedPsplSched$, mostly to \texttt{NonPSPL}, and \texttt{Flat} recall from
$\bmlGapShippedFlatClean$ to $\bmlGapShippedFlatSched$, mostly to \texttt{PeriodicVar}; the eruptive
and long-period variables and \texttt{NonPSPL} itself also degrade, and \texttt{NonPSPL} precision
falls from $\bmlGapShippedPrecClean$ to $\bmlGapShippedPrecSched$. A single gap of half an hour costs
nothing, but one gap of 1--2~h placed at mid-season already lowers single-lens recall from $\bmlGapSensNone$ to
$\bmlGapSensTwo$ ($\bmlGapSensN$ events per class; the cost depends on where the gap falls), the lost events
going to \texttt{NonPSPL} and \texttt{PeriodicVar}. The remedy is an augmentation, not a new model:
blanking one to eight contiguous runs of 1--12~h in every band during a 12-epoch warm-start fine-tune of
the shipped weights raises held-out average precision under the measured pauses from
$\bmlGapApShipped$ to $\bmlGapApRand$, while clean-data average precision falls only from
$\bmlGapApCleanShipped$ to $\bmlGapApCleanRand$. Training on the pauses themselves does at least as well,
provided they are the right pauses: drawing one of RMDC26's six measured seasons per training example gives
$\bmlGapApSeasons$ under the measured pauses against $\bmlGapApRandNorelabel$ for random gaps, with clean
average precision $\bmlGapApCleanSeasons$ against $\bmlGapApCleanRandNorelabel$, whereas training on a single season's
pauses scores only $\bmlGapApFirstSeason$ across the six seasons, its own included. The first two
differences are smaller than the spread of held-out average precision across three training seeds of one
recipe ($\bmlSeedHeldApRange$, \S\ref{sec:gulls:results}), so on our own seasons the measured and random pauses
are not separated; on RMDC26 the measured pauses lead by more than that seed range. Both schedule arms
train with the caustic-in-gap relabel off. That rule relabels a binary as \texttt{PSPL} when the bins
around its recorded onset are blanked, but the onset records when the anomaly first becomes detectable,
not where the caustic is; with a fixed mask and the legacy 7.2-day onset grid it made
$\bmlGapRelabelPct\%$ of the \texttt{NonPSPL}-labelled training events eligible for relabelling, while
with random gaps it makes no measurable difference ($\bmlGapApRand$ with it, $\bmlGapApRandNorelabel$
without). We later applied the label rule itself to the epochs that survive the pauses, refitting the
single-lens model to each binary's noise-free curve (one natural-prior training shard, $\bmlTruthN$ events,
$\bmlTruthReps$ presentations each): under the measured pauses the caustic-in-gap rule gives
$\bmlTruthSeasonsOn\%$ of binary presentations a single-lens label although their surviving anomaly would
still be claimed, while with the rule off $\bmlTruthSeasonsOff\%$ keep a binary label that the surviving
epochs do not support. The shipped checkpoint's input contract is therefore \emph{continuous F146}; for a gapped
schedule, use the recommended checkpoint of \S\ref{sec:gulls} at its own threshold.

The purity we quote is a property of the classifier \emph{and} the assumed prevalence of anomalous
events in the input stream, not of the classifier alone. Our synthetic evaluation mixture contains
detectable anomalies at a prevalence of $\bmlPriorPiZero\%$; the prevalence in a real survey stream
is set by microlensing event rates and any upstream candidate selection, and is typically far
lower. Figure~\ref{fig:prior} makes the dependence explicit. Under a pure prior shift that leaves
the class-conditional score distributions fixed, completeness at the fixed threshold is
prevalence-independent and stays at $\bmlCompleteness$. Purity is not: it falls from the
$\bmlPurity$ we report at the synthetic prevalence to $\bmlPurityOnePct$ at a $1\%$ anomaly
prevalence, and to $\bmlPurityTenthPct$ at $0.1\%$. Read $\bmlPurity$ as conditional on the adopted
mixture; a survey purity would need event-rate priors for the specific input population. Under
prior shift, fixed-threshold completeness transfers and purity does not, which is why we frame
completeness at fixed purity as the reportable quantity.

A survey team could instead re-tune the threshold to its own prevalence and ask what completeness
remains at $\bmlPrevPurTarget$ purity. For each assumed prevalence we select the threshold on the
reserved validation rows and apply it unchanged to the frozen test rows, so the completeness below
is held out rather than the best point of the test set's own precision-recall curve. At the
synthetic mixture's prevalence the answer is
$\bmlPrevCompSynth$, essentially our headline; at a $1\%$ anomaly prevalence it falls to
$\bmlPrevCompOne$; and at $0.1\%$ to $\bmlPrevCompTenth$. The degradation is arithmetic: holding
purity fixed as the contaminant pool grows requires a stricter threshold, which costs completeness.
Any deployment should plan against the figure matching its own input prevalence, not against
$\bmlCompleteness$.

This transfer is prior shift and nothing more: we rescale the class mixture and hold the
class-conditional score distributions fixed. It is the right model for a survey whose events look
like ours but arrive in different proportions, and the wrong one for a survey whose events look
different---different cadence, blending, or variables our simulator does not contain.

The colour bands carry a calibration error. Our simulated zeropoints were anchored to the published
Cycle-7 values for the wide band and scaled for F087 and F213, and an audit against the current
Roman zeropoint tables \citep{RomanTechInfo} shows the wide-band value is accurate to $0.03$~mag while
our F087 and F213 zeropoints are optimistic by roughly $0.10$ and $0.14$~mag respectively. Two
further colour-band assumptions are less defensible. Our F087
saturation limit ($13.9$~AB) was carried over from a much longer Cycle-7 exposure and is too
faint for the design exposure: a shorter exposure fills the detector well only for a brighter
source, and at equal exposure and well depth F087, which collects roughly a third of F146's
electrons at the same AB magnitude, saturates about $1.2$~mag \emph{brighter} than F146 rather
than fainter. Our sky-background ratios also fail to reproduce the published thermal backgrounds,
which make F213 substantially worse and F087 substantially better relative to F146 than we assume.
Both errors sit in the colour bands, which the model uses chiefly to reject variable-star
contaminants. To measure their effect we regenerated two shards of the held-out pool ($\bmlRefN$ events,
disjoint from training; $\bmlRefValPct\%$ of them are among the rows the frozen threshold was chosen on) with
the audited colour-band zeropoints, backgrounds and saturation limits (F146, the exposure time and the
cadence unchanged; the events are identical and $\bmlRefColLabChanged$ labels change) and scored both
versions with the shipped model at its frozen threshold. Every quantity below is on every event of the
shards. The anomaly channel barely moves: completeness is $\bmlRefColCompAudited$ against
$\bmlRefColCompTrained$, population-weighted purity $\bmlRefColPurAudited$ against $\bmlRefColPurTrained$,
and average precision $\bmlRefColApAudited$ against $\bmlRefColApTrained$. The variable classes lose
precision, not recall: with the audited photometry the model calls more flat sources periodic and more
single lenses eruptive (periodic-variable precision $\bmlRefColPrecPerTrained$ to $\bmlRefColPrecPerAudited$,
eruptive $\bmlRefColPrecEruptTrained$ to $\bmlRefColPrecEruptAudited$; F1 $\bmlRefColFonePerTrained$ to
$\bmlRefColFonePerAudited$ and $\bmlRefColFoneEruptTrained$ to $\bmlRefColFoneEruptAudited$), while their
recall is unchanged, no more variables are mistaken for microlensing, and long-period variables are
essentially unchanged (F1 $\bmlRefColFoneLpvAudited$ against $\bmlRefColFoneLpvTrained$). Short fine-tunes of
the shipped weights on two training shards generated with each calibration (up to three epochs, one seed
each, keeping the epoch with the best validation \texttt{NonPSPL} F1) keep average precision between
$\bmlRefColFtApLo$ and $\bmlRefColFtApHi$ whichever calibration the fine-tune and the test use, at the kept and
at the last epoch. The fine-tune on the audited calibration falls to periodic-variable F1
$\bmlRefColFtFonePerAT$ on the old photometry, calling many flat sources periodic, against
$\bmlRefColFtFonePerTT$ for the fine-tune on the old calibration ($\bmlRefColFtFonePerATFinal$ against
$\bmlRefColFtFonePerTTFinal$ at the last epoch). On the audited photometry the two fine-tunes differ at the kept
epoch ($\bmlRefColFtFonePerAA$ and $\bmlRefColFtFonePerTA$) but not at the last ($\bmlRefColFtFonePerAAFinal$ and
$\bmlRefColFtFonePerTAFinal$). The flat-versus-periodic boundary is therefore tied to the colour calibration a
model is trained on, by an amount that depends on where training stops; the anomaly channel is not.
The corrected values are recorded alongside the originals in the released code so that any future
retraining starts from the audited numbers.

The boundaries between our classes---flat versus event, and single lens versus anomalous---are set
not only by a goodness-of-fit threshold but also by a minimum detectable amplitude, which we fix at
twenty times an assumed one-millimagnitude systematic floor. This floor stands in for the
correlated, night-to-week photometric systematics
that do not average down the way independent per-epoch noise does, and which no real pipeline can
follow below some level; we adopt a fixed amplitude cut rather than modelling a full red-noise
process. Because that choice sits on both class boundaries at once, the absolute completeness and
purity we quote shift if the true systematic floor differs from our assumption. To measure the label
side of this dependence we regenerated the same two shards with the floor at $0.01$, $0.02$ (adopted) and
$0.05$~mag and scored them with the shipped model, trained at $0.02$~mag, at its frozen threshold.
The floor changes which generated events are kept, so the arms are partly overlapping draws from the same
population ($\bmlRefFloorNLo$, $\bmlRefFloorNMid$ and $\bmlRefFloorNHi$ events; $\bmlRefFloorShareLo\%$ of the
adopted-floor events recur in the $0.01$-mag arm and $\bmlRefFloorShareHi\%$ in the $0.05$-mag arm). Halving
the floor raises the population-weighted anomaly prevalence from $\bmlRefFloorPrevMid\%$ to
$\bmlRefFloorPrevLo\%$ and lowers completeness from $\bmlRefFloorCompMid$ (95\% Wilson interval
$\bmlRefFloorCompCiLMid$--$\bmlRefFloorCompCiUMid$) to $\bmlRefFloorCompLo$
($\bmlRefFloorCompCiLLo$--$\bmlRefFloorCompCiULo$), while purity rises from $\bmlRefFloorPurMid$ to
$\bmlRefFloorPurLo$: binaries whose anomaly peaks between $0.01$ and $0.02$~mag were labelled single-lens
in training, and the lost completeness is consistent with the model still treating them so. Raising the
floor to $0.05$~mag moves prevalence, completeness and purity the other way ($\bmlRefFloorPrevHi\%$,
$\bmlRefFloorCompHi$ and $\bmlRefFloorPurHi$). Both changes lower the six-class macro-F1 (to
$\bmlRefFloorFoneLo$ and $\bmlRefFloorFoneHi$ from $\bmlRefFloorFoneMid$), because the same floor
also sets the flat/variable boundaries, while average precision stays between $\bmlRefFloorApMin$ and
$\bmlRefFloorApMax$. A different floor therefore leaves the anomaly ranking largely intact but moves
the operating point. We have not retrained under the other floors, so the completeness and purity we
quote are conditional on the $0.02$~mag floor, and a survey requirement should rest on a model
retrained at the floor the real photometry supports.

\section{Cross-simulator validation on an independent \Rom{} simulation}\label{sec:gulls}

Every number so far comes from our own simulator. RMDC26 \citep{RGESPIT2026} is a simulation of the
survey made by a different group with GULLS \citep{Penny2013,Penny2019} and the SynthPop Galactic model
\citep{Kluter2025}, with its own implementation of the observing schedule, photometry and Galactic
population, released openly for testing and development. We use it to test transfer: no RMDC26 event entered
training and no deployed operating threshold was chosen on it (the matched-budget reporting thresholds below
are set on its single lenses by construction). It was, however, used to diagnose the residual
false alarms, to set the upper end of the single-lens source-size prior, and to choose among the
$\bmlGullsNcheckpoints$ fine-tuned checkpoints scored on it (one seed each; two further seeds of the chosen
recipe were trained afterwards), and the recommendation below
was made after seeing both its per-event and its rate-weighted results, so the numbers for the recommended
checkpoint are the best of several on this set and are optimistic.

\subsection{Data, selection, and input contract}

RMDC26 contains $\bmlGullsNcat$ events: $\bmlGullsNcatSingle$ single lenses (1S1L),
$\bmlGullsNcatPlanet$ planetary lenses (1S2L) and $\bmlGullsNcatPlanetBin$ planetary lenses with a
second source (2S2L). Every 1S2L and 2S2L event carries a planetary companion (median mass ratio
$\bmlGullsQmedPlanet$ and $\bmlGullsQmedPlanetBin$ on the scored events; similar but not identical
distributions, KS $p \sim 10^{\bmlGullsQksExp}$), the source is flagged binary in $\bmlGullsBinSrcPct\%$
of scored 2S2L events, and both planetary classes are pre-selected by GULLS' own detection statistic
($\bmlGullsDetCut$ or more for every such event). RMDC26 ships no pure binary-source class, so the
\citet{Gaudi1998} degeneracy of \S\ref{sec:sims} remains untested, and a \texttt{NonPSPL} call on a 2S2L
event may respond to its second source rather than its planet. RMDC26's anomalies are all planetary,
whereas $\bmlOursNonpsplStellarPct\%$ of our \texttt{NonPSPL} evaluation events are
stellar-mass-ratio binaries (median $q = \bmlOursNonpsplQmed$), so transfer recall is not comparable to
the in-distribution completeness of \S\ref{sec:results}.

Selection uses catalogue parameters, never the model's output, and is applied identically to the
three classes. We keep events whose host single-lens peak amplitude, computed from the catalogue impact
parameter and blend fraction, is at least $0.1$~mag (the median single-lens event is
$\bmlGullsAmpMedSingle$~mag and $\bmlGullsAmpFracSingle\%$ pass) and whose Einstein timescale lies in
our prior's support, $1 \le t_{\rm E} \le 300$~d ($\bmlGullsSubdaySingle\%$ of RMDC26 single lenses are
sub-day, against $\bmlGullsSubdayPlanet\%$ and $\bmlGullsSubdayPlanetBin\%$ of the planetary classes).
That leaves $\bmlGullsEligibleN$ events. Of these, $\bmlGullsScoredPct\%$ ($\bmlGullsN$:
$\bmlGullsNSingle$ 1S1L, $\bmlGullsNPlanet$ 1S2L, $\bmlGullsNPlanetBin$ 2S2L) peak inside one of the
six high-cadence seasons and are scored; $\bmlGullsBetweenPct\%$ peak in one of the
$\bmlGullsGapLo$--$\bmlGullsGapHi$-day gaps between seasons (\S\ref{sec:gulls:seasons}),
$\bmlGullsOutsidePct\%$ before or after the survey, $\bmlGullsLowcadPct\%$ in one of the four
low-cadence seasons (outside our support), and $\bmlGullsNoBaseN$ lack a usable baseline. The
amplitude cut is on the \emph{host} lens: it removes most wide-orbit planets, whose host is barely
magnified ($\bmlGullsWidePass\%$ of 1S2L events with $s > 2$ pass, against $\bmlGullsResonantPass\%$ at
$0.5 \le s < 2$), so the scored planetary events are dominated by resonant and central-caustic
geometries. Some scored inputs lie outside our training priors: the baseline is brighter than our
20~mag limit for $\bmlGullsBrightSingle\%$ of single lenses and $\bmlGullsBrightPlanetLo$--$\bmlGullsBrightPlanetHi\%$
of planetary events, the blend fraction is below our prior's minimum for
$\bmlGullsFsLowLo$--$\bmlGullsFsLowHi\%$ of events, and the scored single lenses are much shorter than our
prior's: $\bmlGullsShortSingle\%$ have $t_{\rm E} < 3$~d ($\bmlGullsShortSingleW\%$ weighted by event rate),
against $\bmlPriorShortPct\%$ of our prior's mass (median $t_{\rm E}$ $\bmlGullsTeMedSingle$~d, against
$\bmlGullsTeMedPlanet$~d for 1S2L).

The model is given the 72-day window starting at the season boundary in all three bands. The
baseline is the median F146 magnitude more than $5\,t_{\rm E}$ from the peak over the whole survey,
using the catalogue $t_0$ and $t_{\rm E}$; a real-time pipeline would have neither. Observations are pooled
to one value per 15-minute epoch before binning, as the training cache is built; binning by raw
observation instead moves the shipped checkpoint's single-lens false-alarm rate from $\bmlGullsFaShipped\%$
to $\bmlGullsRawpoolFa\%$, so the operating point is sensitive to this choice. Two sampling differences survive preprocessing:
the pauses, and partial bin occupancy, since RMDC26's colour visits displace one of eight F146 epochs
in about $\bmlGullsPartialBinsPct\%$ of the 2-h bins, an occupancy our training data never contain.
Setting that occupancy to full on $\bmlOccN$ real RMDC26 inputs lowers the recommended checkpoint's
single-lens false alarms from $\bmlOccFaObs\%$ to $\bmlOccFaOne\%$ and its planetary recall from
$\bmlOccRecObs$ to $\bmlOccRecOne$: the model reads partial occupancy as mild evidence of an anomaly.
(The pattern cannot be imposed faithfully on our binned simulations, because a bin's features would
still come from all its epochs; teaching it would need augmentation before binning.)

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:gulls:results}

Table~\ref{tab:gulls} gives the single-lens false-alarm rate at the frozen operating threshold and
planetary recall at matched single-lens false-alarm budgets, on the identical $\bmlGullsN$ events for
every checkpoint; the matched-budget comparison is the one a threshold shift cannot fake. Rates are per
simulated event. RMDC26 also gives each event an event-rate weight, which the release recommends for
representative statistics, and because the unweighted sample
over-represents short single lenses while false alarms rise with timescale (for the recommended checkpoint
from $\bmlGullsFaTeLoRec\%$ of single lenses at $1 \le t_{\rm E} < 3$~d to $\bmlGullsFaTeHiRec\%$ at
30--300~d), the weighted rates differ; the table gives both, and we say where a conclusion depends on
the weighting. The intervals quoted below come from a bootstrap over events, with the classes resampled
separately; they cover the evaluation sample, not training variation.

The shipped checkpoint flags $\bmlGullsFaShipped\%$ of single lenses. Every flagged event is, by
construction, called \texttt{NonPSPL}; across all single lenses the model's first choice splits between
\texttt{NonPSPL} and \texttt{PeriodicVar} with almost none called \texttt{PSPL}, the signature of the
empty mid-season tokens. Gap augmentation brings the false-alarm rate to $\bmlGullsFaGapaware\%$. Its
residual false alarms rise monotonically with $\rho/|u_0|$, the ratio of source radius to impact
parameter, from $\bmlGullsRhoLoGapaware$ for point-like sources to $\bmlGullsRhoHiGapaware$ above 3.
The high-ratio bins are high-magnification events with very small $|u_0|$, not large sources: only
$\bmlGullsNrhoGtOne$ scored single lenses have $\rho > 1$ (99th percentile $\bmlGullsRhoPnn$). The
likely cause is in our generator: its single lenses were point sources while its binaries sampled a
finite source, so a finite-source single lens, whose rounded peak resembles no training \texttt{PSPL},
was read as anomalous. Adding finite-source single lenses (a uniform, achromatic disc computed with
\textsc{VBBinaryLensing}'s \texttt{ESPLMag2} \citep{Bozza2018}; $\rho$ log-uniform up to 5, binaries
unchanged) and fine-tuning for a further 12 epochs from the gap-augmented checkpoint lowers the rate to
$\bmlGullsFaFspl\%$ and the largest-ratio bin to $\bmlGullsRhoHiFspl$, while planetary recall at the
$\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ budget rises from $\bmlGullsRecGapaware$ to $\bmlGullsRecFspl$.

That comparison mixes two changes, the physics and 12 further epochs on a larger pool rich in
high-magnification single lenses. A control with the same pool shape, recipe and epochs but point-source
single lenses separates them (its events are a different random draw from the same priors): the extra
training alone lowers false alarms to $\bmlGullsFaCtrl\%$ and raises recall at the budget to
$\bmlGullsRecCtrl$, and the finite-source single lenses then take them to $\bmlGullsFaFspl\%$ and
$\bmlGullsRecFspl$ (mean recall over false-alarm rates up to 30\%: $\bmlGullsMeanRecGapaware$ for gap
augmentation, $\bmlGullsMeanRecCtrl$ and $\bmlGullsMeanRecFspl$). In false alarms and at low budgets most of
the gain over gap augmentation is therefore the extra training; at higher budgets and in mean recall it is
the physics. Per simulated event the physics lowers false alarms most in the two largest-ratio bins (from
$\bmlGullsRhoMidCtrl$ and $\bmlGullsRhoHiCtrl$ to $\bmlGullsRhoMidFspl$ and $\bmlGullsRhoHiFspl$); in the smaller
bins, where finite size cannot matter, its reductions are within the training noise measured below. On our
own finite-source held-out only the physics restores single-lens recall for sources larger than the
impact parameter ($\bmlHeldPsplMidCtrl$ and $\bmlHeldPsplHiCtrl$ for the control, $\bmlHeldPsplMidFspl$
and $\bmlHeldPsplHiFspl$ with finite sources, in the 1--3 and $> 3$ bins). Weighted by event rate its
false-alarm reduction holds ($\bmlDiffPhysFaW$ points, interval $\bmlDiffPhysFaWLo$ to
$\bmlDiffPhysFaWHi$) but its recall gain at the budget is not resolved ($\bmlDiffPhysRecW$, interval
$\bmlDiffPhysRecWLo$ to $\bmlDiffPhysRecWHi$); both weighted differences are smaller than the training-seed
range measured below. Widening the \emph{binary} source-size prior at the same
time was worse: false alarms $\bmlGullsFaFsplFive\%$ instead of $\bmlGullsFaFspl\%$, recall
$\bmlGullsRecFsplFive$ instead of $\bmlGullsRecFspl$. The largest-ratio bins remain near
$\bmlGullsRhoHiFspl$, so the missing physics explains only part of the residual. Among the scored single
lenses, false alarms are $\bmlGullsFaBright\%$ for baselines brighter than our 20~mag limit against
$\bmlGullsFaInside\%$ inside it, which makes out-of-support brightness a candidate for part of the rest.

Training on the measured per-season pauses instead of random gaps also transfers, under both weightings.
On one pool, with everything else fixed, it lowers single-lens false alarms from
$\bmlGullsFaRandNorelabel\%$ to $\bmlGullsFaSeasons\%$ and raises recall at the $\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$
budget from $\bmlGullsRecRandNorelabel$ to $\bmlGullsRecSeasons$ (weighted by event rate, from
$\bmlGullsRecWRandNorelabel$ to $\bmlGullsRecWSeasons$; interval of the gain $\bmlDiffSchedRecWLo$ to
$\bmlDiffSchedRecWHi$), and it is ahead in $\bmlGullsSeasonWins$ of the $\bmlGullsNseasons$ seasons
scored separately. That arm starts from the shipped weights on a natural-prior pool.

Combining the two gives the recommended checkpoint, the row marked (rec.) in Table~\ref{tab:gulls}: the
finite-source recipe trained on the measured pauses with the caustic-in-gap relabel off. It also uses a
smooth finite-source magnification (\textsc{VBBinaryLensing}'s \texttt{ESPLMag} in place of
\texttt{ESPLMag2}, which leaves steps of a few millimagnitudes where it hands over to the point-source
formula), and its truncation augmentation evaluates finite-source single lenses with that magnification.
Those two changes together do nothing measurable: the finite-source recipe rerun with them flags
$\bmlGullsFaEspl\%$ of single lenses ($\bmlGullsFaFspl\%$ for the original), its planetary recall is
within $\bmlGullsEsplDiffMax$ of the original's at every budget, and its false-alarm rate in every
$\rho/|u_0|$ bin within $\bmlGullsEsplBinDiffMax$. Per simulated event the recommended checkpoint's
planetary recall is within $\bmlGullsCombDiffMax$ of the finite-source checkpoint's at every budget, and
at the frozen threshold it flags more single lenses ($\bmlGullsFaCombined\%$ against $\bmlGullsFaFspl\%$, a
difference smaller than the spread of its own seeds, $\bmlSeedFaLo$--$\bmlSeedFaHi\%$), in every $\rho/|u_0|$
bin. Weighted by event rate it is ahead at every budget, by
$\bmlDiffCombRecWMin$--$\bmlDiffCombRecWMax$ for 1S2L (every interval above zero), and at the
$\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ budget by more for 2S2L ($\bmlGullsRecBinWCombined$ against $\bmlGullsRecBinWFspl$), and on our own held-out events with
the measured pauses imposed it is also better (average precision $\bmlSidecarHeldAp$ against
$\bmlFsplHeldAp$; clean $\bmlSidecarHeldApClean$ against $\bmlFsplHeldApClean$). This training run
therefore matches or beats the finite-source checkpoint at every matched budget under both weightings,
and we chose it on that basis. Two further seeds of the same recipe, trained after the choice (the last
two rows of Table~\ref{tab:gulls}), do not: per simulated event they trail the finite-source checkpoint
by $\bmlSeedTrailLo$--$\bmlSeedTrailHi$ at the $\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ budget, and weighted by event rate
they tie it. On our own held-out events with the measured pauses, which involve no RMDC26 selection, the
three seeds tie or exceed the finite-source checkpoint (average precision
$\bmlSeedHeldApLo$--$\bmlSeedHeldApHi$ against $\bmlFsplHeldAp$); on the clean held-out they are at or just
below it ($\bmlSeedHeldApCleanLo$--$\bmlSeedHeldApCleanHi$ against $\bmlFsplHeldApClean$). RMDC26 therefore
does not separate the two recipes. We keep the first seed as the recommended checkpoint, trained on the
survey's measured pauses, and \S\S\ref{sec:gulls:labels}--\ref{sec:gulls:cascade} use it; its planetary
recall at every budget of Table~\ref{tab:gulls}, and at its own threshold, is the best of the three seeds in
both weightings, although another seed flags fewer single lenses at the frozen threshold.

Other changes did not help. Matching GULLS' noise, whose F146 errors equal ours for
sources brighter than 20th magnitude and reach $\bmlGullsNoiseFaint$ times ours at 24--25~mag (a
$\bmlGullsNoiseBkg$-fold background variance reproduces this; a global factor does not), gave a
checkpoint that was worse at every matched budget and in false alarms ($\bmlGullsFaNoisy\%$; recall
$\bmlGullsRecNoisy$), although its recall at the frozen threshold was higher; with a single seed and a
single noise level we do not attribute a mechanism. Recording the anomaly onset on a finer grid (an
attempt made before the off-by-one error in its first implementation was found) moved recall at the
$\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ budget from $\bmlGullsRecFspl$ to $\bmlGullsRecOnset$.

The three seeds of the recommended recipe also give a scale for the training noise behind every
comparison in this section (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}). At the $\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ budget they reach
planetary recall $\bmlSeedRecLo$--$\bmlSeedRecHi$ per simulated event and $\bmlSeedRecWLo$--$\bmlSeedRecWHi$
weighted by event rate, and they flag $\bmlSeedFaLo$--$\bmlSeedFaHi\%$ of single lenses at the frozen
threshold. The paired intervals quoted above cover only the sampling of events: between seeds of one
recipe they can exclude zero (seed 2 against seed 1: $\bmlDiffSeedRec$ per event, interval
$\bmlDiffSeedRecLo$ to $\bmlDiffSeedRecHi$; seed 3 against seed 2: $\bmlDiffSeedRecThreeTwo$, interval
$\bmlDiffSeedRecThreeTwoLo$ to $\bmlDiffSeedRecThreeTwoHi$). Three runs give only a rough scale: it includes
the random training split and the choice of epoch but not the variation between pools, and two runs of one
recipe would differ by more than a three-run range about a third of the time. We therefore read a
difference between single runs as unresolved when it is smaller than the seed range at the same budget and
in the same weighting, and as indicative rather than established when it is larger. On that reading the
measured pauses lead random gaps in recall at every budget and in mean recall, in both weightings; the extra
training accounts for most of the gain over gap augmentation in false alarms and at the low budgets, and the
finite-source physics for the gain at the $\bmlGullsBudgetHi\%$ budget and in mean recall and for the false
alarms of the two largest $\rho/|u_0|$ bins, all per simulated event; weighted by event rate the physics
resolves nothing (those two bins hold about $\bmlGullsNeffRhoMid$ and $\bmlGullsNeffRhoHi$ effective single
lenses). The recommended recipe's lead over the finite-source recipe is not established: at the
$\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ budget its three seeds differ from the finite-source run by $\bmlSeedLeadLo$ to
$\bmlSeedLeadHi$ per simulated event and by $\bmlSeedLeadWLo$ to $\bmlSeedLeadWHi$ weighted, and only the
released seed's weighted lead exceeds the seed range. Two fine-tunes of one recipe on different pools
with the same seed differ by $\bmlSeedSpreadLo$--$\bmlSeedSpreadHi$ per event across the three budgets,

\paragraph{Operating point.} The frozen threshold was chosen on gap-free, point-source data. We chose
the recommended checkpoint's threshold at the paper's 90\% purity target on our own simulated seasons
with the measured RMDC26 pauses imposed, without reading RMDC26, on a finite-source held-out pool whose
population-weighted \texttt{NonPSPL} prevalence ($\bmlSidecarPoolPrev\%$) is close to our test set's
($\bmlPriorPiZero\%$). The paper's procedure takes the threshold on one 20\% validation slice; here that
slice gives $\bmlSidecarSliceThr$, while 200 random slices scatter between $\bmlSidecarThrLo$ and
$\bmlSidecarThrHi$ (68\% of them), which moves RMDC26's single-lens false-alarm rate between
$\bmlSidecarFaLo$ and $\bmlSidecarFaHi\%$; we therefore use the whole pool, which gives $\bmlSidecarThr$.
At $\bmlSidecarThr$ the checkpoint flags $\bmlSidecarFa\%$ of RMDC26 single lenses ($\bmlSidecarFaW\%$
weighted by event rate) with planetary recall $\bmlSidecarRecPlanet$ /
$\bmlSidecarRecPlanetBin$ (1S2L / 2S2L; $\bmlSidecarRecPlanetW$ weighted, 1S2L); the single slice's
threshold would give $\bmlSidecarSliceFa\%$ and $\bmlSidecarSliceRec$. Calibrated the same way, the three
seeds of the recipe together span $\bmlSeedOwnFaLo$--$\bmlSeedOwnFaHi\%$ false alarms and recall
$\bmlSeedOwnRecLo$--$\bmlSeedOwnRecHi$. The finite-source checkpoint,
calibrated the same way, sits at a different point of the same trade-off ($\bmlFsplCalThr$:
$\bmlFsplCalFa\%$ and $\bmlFsplCalRec$). The gap-augmented checkpoint has no usable operating point on a
finite-source population: it reaches 90\% purity only on its few most confident events.

\subsection{What ``recall'' means across two label ontologies}\label{sec:gulls:labels}

GULLS assigns classes by generator, subject to its own detection cut; BinML's classes are observational
(\S\ref{sec:detcond}). Because RMDC26 ships each event's noise-free light curve and per-epoch error, the
two can be reconciled: we applied our labelling rule, the $\Delta\chi^2$ thresholds and the $0.02$~mag
floor against the best-fitting static point-source single-lens model on the noise-free F146 curve, with
GULLS' errors as $\sigma$ and single lenses labelled exactly as in training, to the first
$\bmlGullsTruthNSingle$ scored single lenses and $\bmlGullsTruthNPlanet$ scored events of each planetary
class. Under that rule $\bmlGullsUndetPlanet\%$ of 1S2L and
$\bmlGullsUndetPlanetBin\%$ of 2S2L events carry no anomaly the policy would claim within one season:
$\bmlGullsFailChi\%$ of the planetary events fail the $\Delta\chi^2$ cut and $\bmlGullsFloorVeto\%$ pass
it but fall under the floor (with GULLS' errors, larger than ours for faint sources, the $\Delta\chi^2$ cut
is not the formality it is on our simulator, \S\ref{sec:sims}). Rescaling the $\Delta\chi^2$ cuts for RMDC26's $\bmlGullsEpochExcess\%$ more F146 epochs per
season moves these fractions by under a point. The figure depends on the floor, the single-season window
and our selection; it is not a statement about what a survey could detect. Recall against generator
labels mixes detectable and undetectable planets and is not bounded by the detectable fraction: the
recommended checkpoint flags $\bmlGullsFlagUndet\%$ of the planetary events without a detectable
anomaly. On those with one, its recall at the frozen threshold is $\bmlGullsRecDetPlanet$ /
$\bmlGullsRecDetPlanetBin$ (1S2L / 2S2L) against $\bmlGullsRecGenPlanet$ / $\bmlGullsRecGenPlanetBin$ on
generator labels, and $\bmlSidecarRecDetPlanet$ / $\bmlSidecarRecDetPlanetBin$ at its own operating
point. Recall tracks significance more closely than amplitude: it rises from $\bmlGullsRecChiLo$ for
anomalies with $160 \le \Delta\chi^2 < 500$ to $\bmlGullsRecChiHi$ above $10^5$.

The rule also bears on the floor. The checkpoint flags $\bmlGullsFlagFloorVeto\%$ of the planetary events
whose anomaly is significant but below $0.02$~mag, at a similar rate however far below
($\bmlGullsFloorFlagThreeLo$--$\bmlGullsFloorFlagThreeHi\%$ across sub-floor amplitude bins); with F146
alone the rate falls to $\bmlGullsFloorFlagOneLo$--$\bmlGullsFloorFlagOneHi\%$, so much of it follows the
colour behaviour above. It also flags $\bmlGullsSingleMisfitK$ of the $\bmlGullsSingleMisfitN$ single
lenses whose static refit shows a significant sub-floor misfit (95\% interval
$\bmlGullsSingleMisfitLo$--$\bmlGullsSingleMisfitHi\%$; such misfits come from effects our static
point-source refit does not model, such as parallax): the response to sub-floor misfit is not specific
to planets. Lowering the floor from $0.02$ to
$0.005$~mag raises the fraction of 1S2L events with a claimable anomaly from $\bmlGullsDetPlanet\%$ to
$\bmlGullsFloorDetLo\%$; raising it to $0.05$~mag lowers it to $\bmlGullsFloorDetHi\%$. This is the
label side of the floor sensitivity; retraining under another floor is not covered here.

\subsection{Events whose peak falls between seasons}\label{sec:gulls:seasons}

The single-season input contract leaves out the $\bmlGullsBetweenPct\%$ of eligible events that peak in
one of the gaps between seasons, where nothing is observed. For the first $\bmlMsN$ such events of each
class we scored the high-cadence season on each side of the gap and kept the larger anomaly probability,
and applied the labelling rule of \S\ref{sec:gulls:labels} to each season, starting the single-lens refit
also at the in-window maximum because the host's peak lies outside the window. A high-cadence season
borders the gap for $\bmlMsScorableSingle$, $\bmlMsScorablePlanet$ and $\bmlMsScorablePlanetBin\%$ of the
between-season 1S1L, 1S2L and 2S2L events; the rest border only low-cadence seasons. In those seasons a
claimable anomaly is visible for $\bmlMsDetPlanet\%$ of the scorable 1S2L and $\bmlMsDetPlanetBin\%$ of the
2S2L events, against $\bmlGullsDetPlanet\%$ and $\bmlGullsDetPlanetBin\%$ in-season, so the unobserved gap
costs $\bmlMsDetLossPlanet$ and $\bmlMsDetLossPlanetBin$ points of claimable anomalies. At the frozen threshold
the recommended checkpoint flags $\bmlMsFa\%$ of these single lenses (at most $\bmlMsFaHi\%$ at 95\%
confidence) and $\bmlMsRecGenPlanet\%$ / $\bmlMsRecGenPlanetBin\%$ of their planetary events, and recalls
$\bmlMsRecDetPlanet$ / $\bmlMsRecDetPlanetBin$ of those with a claimable anomaly in a wing, against
$\bmlGullsRecDetPlanet$ / $\bmlGullsRecDetPlanetBin$ in-season: the model is conservative when a season
holds only a wing of the event (we did not test why). Lowering the threshold until $\bmlGullsBudgetMid\%$ of this population's single lenses
are flagged ($\bmlMsBudgetThr$, set on only $\bmlMsBudgetExceed$ exceedances) raises that recall to
$\bmlMsRecBudgetPlanet$ / $\bmlMsRecBudgetPlanetBin$, comparable to the in-season $\bmlGullsRecDetBudgetPlanet$ /
$\bmlGullsRecDetBudgetPlanetBin$ at the same budget, so scoring seasons around a gap would need its own
operating point. No model can recover anomalies that fall in the unobserved gaps; what a whole-mission
model would add is events whose light curves span seasons and the four low-cadence seasons
($\bmlGullsLowcadPct\%$ of eligible events), which are outside BinML's support.

\subsection{The partial-season cascade on RMDC26}\label{sec:gulls:cascade}
Section~\ref{sec:cascade} measures how early the model flags an anomaly, on our own simulator and for
eligible binaries only. We repeated its primary protocol on RMDC26 with the recommended checkpoint at the
frozen threshold. The high-cadence season of $\bmlCgNsingle$ single lenses and $\bmlCgNbinary$ binaries was
revealed in half-day steps, every cut was scored from F146 alone, and the alert is the first cut above the
threshold. The onset is the first cut at which our labelling rule (\S\ref{sec:gulls:labels}) is met on
GULLS' noise-free light curve with GULLS' errors, so it comes from a simulator we did not write. The
$\bmlCgElig$ eligible events are the binaries whose anomaly is claimable over the whole season; nothing is
selected on the model's output.

Premature alerts stay rare: $\bmlCgPrem\%$ of the eligible events are flagged before the onset (95\%
interval $\bmlCgPremLo$--$\bmlCgPremHi\%$), against $\bmlCgInGiantPrem\%$ and $\bmlCgInNeptunePrem\%$ for the
giant-planet and Neptune strata of the in-house scan ($\bmlCgInGiantN$ and $\bmlCgInNeptuneN$ events; shipped
model, our simulator), the only strata comparable with RMDC26's planetary anomalies. Alerts come later: the
median lag of the others is $\bmlCgLag$~d, against $\bmlCgInGiantLag$~d in-house. Detection is lower:
$\bmlCgDet\%$ ($\bmlCgDetLo$--$\bmlCgDetHi\%$) of the eligible events are flagged by the end of the
season, against $\bmlCgInGiantDet\%$ and $\bmlCgInNeptuneDet\%$ in-house (a different checkpoint on a different
simulator, so the difference mixes the two), in line with the full-season recall on
claimable anomalies ($\bmlGullsRecDetPlanet$ and $\bmlGullsRecDetPlanetBin$, \S\ref{sec:gulls:labels}).
Revealing all three bands lowers premature alerts to $\bmlCgPremThree\%$, lengthens the lag to
$\bmlCgLagThree$~d and lowers detection to $\bmlCgDetThree\%$. The in-house scan measured three bands
only for its whole population, mostly stellar binaries (premature alerts $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ to
$\bmlCascMultibandPct\%$, lag $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$ to $\bmlCascMultibandMedianLag$~d, detection
$\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ to $\bmlCascMultibandDet\%$), so its planetary strata cannot be compared on this point.

Scanning single lenses gives the alert burden on a second simulator. $\bmlCgSingleAlert\%$ of single
lenses per simulated event ($\bmlCgSingleAlertW\%$ weighted by event rate) raise an alert at some point in the
season ($\bmlCgSingleAlertDay$ alerts per 1,000 single lenses per day). If 1\% of events were planetary
binaries, $\bmlCgPurOne\%$ of alerts would come from a claimable anomaly, and $\bmlCgPurFive\%$ at 5\% (per
simulated event). At RMDC26's own rate-weighted planetary fraction, which over-represents planets, the figure is
$\bmlCgPurRmdc\%$. These count single-lens contamination only; RMDC26 contains no variable stars or flat
sources (our synthetic ones rarely alert, \S\ref{sec:cascade}, but real variables are untested), so a real
stream would be less pure. On our own simulator, with continuous seasons and the same checkpoint, band and
threshold, $\bmlRefRecSingleK$ of $\bmlRefRecSingleN$ generated single lenses alert ($\bmlRefRecSinglePct\%$);
RMDC26's single lenses alert several times as often. At the recalibrated
threshold single-lens alerts fall to $\bmlCgSingleAlertCal\%$ and purity at 1\% rises to
$\bmlCgPurOneCal\%$, but detection falls to $\bmlCgDetCal\%$, premature alerts to $\bmlCgPremCal\%$, and the
lag grows to $\bmlCgLagCal$~d. The cascade's low premature-alert rate therefore holds on another simulator,
but its alerts are not a follow-up list by themselves: at realistic prevalence they need a second stage that
removes single-lens contaminants.

\subsection{What this test does and does not establish}

It establishes that the shipped model's failure on a gapped schedule is a property of the schedule,
reproducible without any GULLS data and reduced by augmentation, preferably with the measured pauses;
that continued training on a pool rich in high-magnification single lenses accounts for most of the further
reduction in single-lens false alarms after gap augmentation ($\bmlGullsFaGapaware\%$ to $\bmlGullsFaCtrl\%$ per
simulated event, $\bmlGullsFaWGapaware\%$ to $\bmlGullsFaWCtrl\%$ weighted); and that finite-source single
lenses, a physics our generator lacked, lower false alarms further in the two largest $\rho/|u_0|$ bins and raise
recall at high budgets, per simulated event, by more than the training noise we measured. It does
not establish a \Rom{} planet yield: RMDC26's planetary population, detection cut and our amplitude
selection all differ from our training population; neither simulator contains real systematics; our
detectability policy uses a static point-source refit and a uniform source without limb darkening; the
fine-tunes' gap relabelling is approximate (\S\ref{sec:limits}); every fine-tune but the recommended recipe is a
single seed, and that recipe's three seeds give only a rough scale of the training noise, which exceeds several
of the comparisons between recipes; some
conclusions depend on whether events are weighted by rate; and RMDC26 was used to choose among
checkpoints. It does not test the binary-source degeneracy, for which
RMDC26 has no class. Sub-day single lenses, outside our timescale prior, are flagged as anomalies by the
recommended checkpoint less often than in-support single lenses ($\bmlGullsSubdayFa\%$ of
$\bmlGullsSubdayN$ unweighted and $\bmlGullsSubdayFaW\%$ weighted by event rate, against
$\bmlGullsFaCombined\%$ and $\bmlGullsFaWCombined\%$), and their most probable class is mostly
\texttt{PeriodicVar} ($\bmlGullsSubdayPerLo$--$\bmlGullsSubdayPerHi\%$ across checkpoints), with
$\bmlGullsSubdayMl\%$ read as microlensing of either kind; a model for free-floating planets needs sub-day
timescales in its prior, not a threshold change.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

BinML is intended as a cheap first-pass triage layer before full binary-lens modelling. The
completeness-at-fixed-purity framing exposes the tradeoff a survey team would need to set, and the
network's forward pass is fast enough for repeated large-scale scoring. Those properties establish
a computational benchmark rather than a deployable broker: the evaluation uses synthetic
photometry, a simulator-provided baseline, a truth-informed onset, and half-day rather than
native-cadence updates.

Which follow-up modes does this latency serve? A median alert $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days after
onset arrives once a short caustic crossing has finished, so for the brief planetary anomalies that
motivate the survey BinML is a \emph{post-hoc} identifier rather than a trigger for the feature
itself.

A few-day latency could still serve two other tasks. The first is anomalies that are not brief:
extended or repeating caustic structure, second crossings, and long-timescale binaries whose
perturbations persist for days. The second is everything that depends on
recognising the \emph{event} rather than the feature---scheduling denser photometry for the rest
of an ongoing event, initiating colour or parallax campaigns, and prioritising which light curves
receive expensive binary-lens modelling.
Triggering on the anomaly itself would require evaluating at the survey's native cadence: our grid
sensitivity (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) covers only grids coarser than the half-day one, where the
premature rate does not rise, and does not extrapolate to a $15$-minute grid with far more
evaluation opportunities.

Latency does not vary strongly with mass ratio: the median post-onset lag is within a day of the
population value above $q=10^{-4}$, though the detection fraction falls for $10^{-4}<q<10^{-3}$.
We did not stratify by anomaly duration or caustic topology, so the follow-up modes above are a
reading of what a multi-day latency can serve rather than a measurement of those subpopulations.

BinML's input is one contiguous season. Of RMDC26's eligible events, that contract leaves out those
peaking between seasons, $\bmlGullsBetweenSingle\%$ of single lenses but $\bmlGullsBetweenPlanetLo$--$\bmlGullsBetweenPlanetHi\%$
of planetary events, and those peaking in low-cadence seasons, $\bmlGullsLowcadSingle\%$ and
$\bmlGullsLowcadPlanetLo$--$\bmlGullsLowcadPlanetHi\%$ (the release concentrates events in the high-cadence
seasons: $\bmlGullsTzeroDenseLo$--$\bmlGullsTzeroDenseHi\%$ of its catalogue peaks fall in them, although
they span $\bmlGullsDenseSpanPct\%$ of the mission). Scoring the high-cadence seasons around a gap makes
most of the former scorable at a false-alarm cost below one percent, but at the frozen threshold flags only
$\bmlMsRecGenPlanet\%$ of their planetary events; a claimable anomaly survives in a wing for fewer planetary
events than in-season, and the operating point would have to be set separately (\S\ref{sec:gulls:seasons}).
Low-cadence seasons are outside the model's support. A whole-mission model, taking the full light curve, would address both and let the
partial-season cascade of \S\ref{sec:cascade} run across season boundaries. It needs a mission-length
simulation, labels over the full mission and a hierarchical input, and is the natural next version.

\subsection{Ablating the two mechanisms}\label{sec:ablations}

Both of this paper's methodological claims are testable by removing the mechanism and retraining.
Each ablation trains arms from scratch on identical shards with identical hyper-parameters and
seed, differing only in the stated variable, and evaluates them on a common held-out set from an
unseen seed base. These are small-scale runs (16 shards, $\bmlAbLabelEpochs$ epochs) rather than
production trainings, so absolute values sit below the headline numbers and the comparison between
arms is the result. The two ablations come from separate runs whose evaluation sets differ by 18
events out of $\sim$45{,}000, so arms should be compared within a run, not across them.

\paragraph{Detectability-conditioned labelling.} The intervention is the training label alone.
Both principal arms use weights derived from the policy-conditioned class balance, so they differ in
the target label but not in the weighting objective. An additional arm using label-derived weights
is retained as a sensitivity check.

Against the policy-conditioned label, macro-F1 falls from $\bmlAbLabelObsMacroFOne$ to
$\bmlAbLabelGenMacroFOne$ when the network is trained on generator intent---a gap of
$\bmlAbLabelGap$ (95\% CI $[\bmlAbLabelGapLo,\bmlAbLabelGapHi]$, paired bootstrap over the
$\bmlAbLabelNeval$ evaluation events).
Every arm is scored against \emph{both} label sets, and against the generator label the ordering
reverses, $\bmlAbLabelObsMacroFOneGen$ versus $\bmlAbLabelGenMacroFOneGen$: each arm wins on the
ontology it was trained for. The experiment shows optimization alignment, not that the
policy-conditioned ontology is objectively correct.

The damage concentrates where the relabelling acts: \texttt{PSPL} F1 moves from
$\bmlAbLabelObsPsplFOne$ to $\bmlAbLabelGenPsplFOne$ and \texttt{NonPSPL} from
$\bmlAbLabelObsNonpsplFOne$ to $\bmlAbLabelGenNonpsplFOne$. The generator-intent arm is not merely
worse but differently behaved: at the frozen threshold it flags almost every event, reaching
completeness $\bmlAbLabelGenComp$ at a purity of $\bmlAbLabelGenPur$, because it was trained to call
sub-threshold binaries anomalous.

The label-derived-weight sensitivity arm scores $\bmlAbLabelGenOwnWMacroFOne$ against the
policy-conditioned label, compared with $\bmlAbLabelGenMacroFOne$ when the weights are pinned. Weighting
does affect the operating threshold: the sensitivity arm never crosses the frozen threshold.

The mechanism is concrete. Across the final test split, $\bmlRelabelled\%$ of events carry a
policy-conditioned label different from their generated class; a fifth of all events ($\bmlGenNpDemoted\%$,
half of all disagreements) are generated binaries whose anomaly falls below the detection
thresholds. A network trained on generator intent is asked to assign those binaries the anomalous
label despite their perturbations falling below the adopted policy threshold.

Both arms are single training seeds, so the interval above covers evaluation noise, not
initialisation noise; a seed sweep is the next step.

\paragraph{The truncation augmentation: unresolved.} At a single threshold the second ablation
does not identify a timing benefit. Removing the truncation augmentation changes
the premature-alert rate in opposite directions depending on the decision rule: at the frozen
operating threshold the augmented model is better ($\bmlAbCascOnPremThr$ versus
$\bmlAbCascOffPremThr$), but under six-class argmax it is worse ($\bmlAbCascOnPremArgmax$ versus
$\bmlAbCascOffPremArgmax$), on $N=\bmlAbCascN$ binaries. It is also expensive: within-season
detection at the same threshold falls from $\bmlAbCascOffDet$ to $\bmlAbCascOnDet$, and static
macro-F1 from $\bmlAbCascOffMacroFOne$ to $\bmlAbCascOnMacroFOne$.

These changes are consistent with the augmented model saying \texttt{NonPSPL} less often, lowering
premature alerts and genuine detections together. A threshold-only comparison cannot separate that
conservatism from better temporal ordering.

\paragraph{Exploratory risk--coverage comparison.} Using stored probability traces for both arms
on the same $\bmlMatchN$ eligible binaries, we select a separate threshold for each arm to attain
each of six common within-season detection fractions (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade_matched}). The augmented arm has fewer premature alerts
from $0.50$ through $0.90$ detection: at $0.60$ the rates are $\bmlMatchOnSixty\%$ and
$\bmlMatchOffSixty\%$, and at $0.80$ they are $\bmlMatchOnEighty\%$ and
$\bmlMatchOffEighty\%$. The ordering reverses at $0.95$ detection.
The separation at the lower rates is one-sided at the level of individual events: at $0.60$
detection the two arms disagree on $\bmlMatchDiscordSixty$ events, of which
$\bmlMatchOffOnlySixty$ are premature for the unaugmented arm only and $\bmlMatchOnOnlySixty$ for
the augmented arm only, so the augmented arm's premature set is a strict subset of the
unaugmented arm's on this sample.

The thresholds are selected and the premature-alert rates evaluated on the same $\bmlMatchN$ events, and
both training arms use one seed, so this curve is descriptive rather than confirmatory. A causal
timing claim requires thresholds selected on a disjoint calibration sample, paired evaluation on
held-out events, and repeated training seeds.

Two exploratory fine-tuning runs remain as archived JSON summaries rather than results here: each
used a single training seed, and the retained outputs lack the provenance and per-event pairs
needed for matched-purity comparisons.

The natural next step is validation on real events in the model's own regime. Faint-source false
anomalies, the baseline estimate, and colour-band calibration are the specific priorities for that
validation; sparse archival data may be better served by models trained for that sampling than by
making BinML cadence-agnostic.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

BinML is a compact multi-band classifier for six classes of synthetic, progressively revealed
\Rom{}-like light curves. On the adopted class mixture it reaches completeness
$\bmlCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlPurity$ for detectable \texttt{NonPSPL} events. These values are
conditional on the simulation and its class priors; they are not planet yields or survey purity.

At half-day resolution the model alerts on $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of eligible simulated binaries.
Relative to a truth-informed, noise-free onset, $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ alert early and the
non-premature alerts have a median delay of $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days. Scanned in the same half-day
steps with all three bands, a mixture of every class raises $\bmlRefStreamAlertsDay$ alerts per 1,000 events
per day, $\bmlRefStreamPurity\%$ of them from detectable anomalies at the simulated prevalence and
$\bmlRefStreamPurOne\%$ at a 1\% prevalence; no synthetic variable alerts, and nearly all false alerts are
binaries below the detectability policy. With F146 alone the share of detectable anomalies falls to
$\bmlRefStreamFPurity\%$. The matched-risk comparison between models trained with and without
truncation is exploratory---thresholds and outcomes use the same $\bmlMatchN$ events---and does not
establish a causal timing benefit.

Of the tests the submitted version listed as open, two are now reported: a scan of every class that
measures the alert burden (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) and the label side of the $0.02$~mag floor
(\S\ref{sec:limits}). So are a colour-calibration ablation (\S\ref{sec:limits}) and transfer to an independent
simulation of the survey, on which the shipped model fails because of the survey's F146 observing pauses and a
fine-tune trained on those pauses, though not on the multi-season structure, reduces the failure
(\S\ref{sec:gulls}). The principal remaining tests are retraining under another detectability floor, an
estimated-baseline ablation, repeated training seeds for the shipped model (its training set is not
re-created here; seed spread is measured only for the gap-aware recipe), a streaming threshold calibrated on
disjoint prefixes, a model that takes the whole multi-season light curve, and validation on real
photometry. The released code and weights provide a rebuildable static benchmark, with per-artifact
provenance recorded below.

\section*{Data availability}
BinML, its trained weights, and the full simulation-to-inference pipeline are openly available at
\url{https://github.com/kunalb541/BinML}. Reproducibility comes at three distinct levels, and we
distinguish them because they are not equally cheap.

\emph{Manuscript rebuild.} Every number, table and figure in this paper is generated by the build
script from the artifacts distributed with a release, with no access to any local output directory.
This runs in minutes on a laptop and is exercised in continuous integration from a clean
\code{git archive} of the repository.

\emph{Analysis regeneration.} The frozen evaluation artifact---per-event logits, labels, generator
classes, selection weights, event parameters and the final-test index, with a manifest recording
each array's shape, dtype and hash---supports the static results in
\S\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:limits}. The eligible-binary streaming results come from a separate archived scan
(\code{validation/cascade\_trace.npz}, the full probability trace for the frozen $\bmlCascN$-event
sample); the floor, colour-calibration and every-class streaming results of \S\ref{sec:cascade} and
\S\ref{sec:limits} from \code{validation/referee\_round.json}, whose per-event inputs (probability traces,
scores, labels, weights and the two colour fine-tunes) are archived with their hashes in
\code{validation/referee\_round\_archive/}; the stored-truth label measurements from
\code{validation/truth\_relabel\_impact.json}; and the other ablations and auxiliary experiments from their own
archived JSON artifacts, each with the script that produced it. Re-deriving any reported statistic from these requires only the
reduction scripts; regenerating the artifacts themselves requires the model and the simulator, and
takes minutes to hours. Provenance limitations remain in the archived runs. The RMDC26 fine-tunes of
\S\ref{sec:gulls} were run from uncommitted working trees, whose \code{git describe} strings are recorded
without the patches (\code{validation/gulls/PROVENANCE.md} lists what is known for each). The primary trace
records the checkpoint hash but was generated from a dirty tree without recording its patch; the
matched-cascade trace records neither source nor checkpoint hashes. The labelling ablation's source
hash was repaired after the run because the in-run hash was empty. The earlier ablation run that
supplies the truncation-augmentation comparison predates this harness entirely and records no
commit, data or checkpoint hashes at all; its artifact carries a provenance block saying so. Its
labelling arms are superseded by the fixed-weight rerun, and the cascade comparison it still
supplies is the one we report as \emph{not} supporting our claim. These disclosures are retained
in the artifacts; future executions fail on an empty or mismatched source hash, but the published
experiments have not been rerun solely to replace those metadata fields.

\emph{Cross-simulator validation.} The RMDC26 release \citep{RGESPIT2026} is public (CC0) on the Hugging Face
Hub at \code{RGES-PIT/MachineLearning}; we pin revision \code{a338d5bab441b5caf551d2fea9469aadfdc81ec1}.
\code{validation/gulls/rmdc26\_scores.csv.gz} holds every scored event's anomaly probability for every
checkpoint in \S\ref{sec:gulls}, with its class, season, event-rate weight and lens parameters, and the RMDC26
tables of \S\ref{sec:gulls} regenerate from it alone; the recommended checkpoint of \S\ref{sec:gulls} is released as
\code{binml/weights/binml-gapaware.pt}.

\emph{Training reproduction.} The multi-million-event training set is too large to host and is
available on request; it can also be regenerated from the released simulation code, since each
shard is seeded deterministically by its index. Reproducing the released checkpoint, the ablation
arms or the fine-tuning runs means rerunning that generation and the training, which is the
cloud-scale cost described in Appendix~\ref{app:infra}. The build script does not do this, and no
claim in this paper should be read as implying that it does.

\appendix

\section{Simulation and training infrastructure}\label{app:infra}

For reproducibility we describe the compute configuration used to generate the data and train the
model. We did not retain billing records and therefore do not report a currency cost.

\paragraph{Simulation (AWS).} The training set of roughly $\bmlSimEvents$~million events was
generated on Amazon Web Services in the \texttt{\bmlSimRegion} region, on a small fleet of
$\bmlSimInstances$ transient (Spot) instances of $\bmlSimVcpus$ virtual CPUs each. The generator is
embarrassingly parallel: one process builds one self-contained shard of a few thousand light
curves, seeded purely by its index, so a shard that is lost to a Spot interruption costs at most
that shard and the run resumes by skipping shards already written to cloud storage. At a measured
throughput of about $\bmlSimEps$ events per second across the fleet, the full set was produced in
roughly $\bmlSimHours$~hours. Storage use and cloud price vary with provider, region, and date and
are not reconstructed here.

\paragraph{Training recipe.} The released checkpoint is the last of $\bmlTrainStages$ warm-started
stages, each keeping its epoch with the highest validation \texttt{NonPSPL} F1. A base model was trained
from scratch on $\bmlTrainBaseEvents$ natural-prior events with AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$,
weight decay $0.01$, batch size 512) and cross-entropy loss with the selection weights of \S\ref{sec:eval}
and an additional factor $2$ on the anomaly class. Stage 2 restarted from its fourth epoch without that
factor (learning rate $1.2\times10^{-4}$); stage 3 added the truncation augmentation, applied to half of all
training examples (learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$); stage 4 added targeted hard-regime pools (learning rate
$5\times10^{-5}$, batch size 384, as in every later stage); stage 5 applied the detectability floor to truncated
periodic classes and admitted truncations from the first days of the season; and stage 6 applied the
truncation relabelling to every class and added pools that widen the separation, timescale and faint-source
coverage, $\bmlTrainFinalEvents$ million events in all, for $\bmlTrainEpochs$ epochs. The released checkpoint
is stage 6's zero-based epoch 4. Seeds are fixed ($\bmlSeedTrain$) and the data loader is seeded per worker.
The fine-tunes of \S\ref{sec:limits} and \S\ref{sec:gulls} warm-start from the released weights (or from the
gap-augmented checkpoint) and keep their best epoch the same way; the colour-calibration fine-tunes use a
learning rate of $5\times10^{-5}$, the RMDC26 fine-tunes $10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Training (local).} The network is small enough that every stage was trained on a personal
computer, on the \bmlTrainDevice. A benchmark of the training step (forward and backward pass on a full batch
of $\bmlTrainBatchBench$) sustains about $\bmlTrainEps$ events per second; the $\bmlTrainStages$ stages took
about $\bmlTrainHours$~hours of wall-clock in total. On local hardware no compute is rented, so the marginal
training cost is local electricity; we did not estimate it. The compactness of the network
(\S\ref{sec:model}) is what
keeps both training and survey-scale inference within reach of a single workstation rather than
requiring a dedicated computing allocation.
