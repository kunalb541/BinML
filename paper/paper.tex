\begin{abstract}
The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will produce more microlensing light curves than can be
inspected manually. We present \textbf{BinML}, a compact ($\bmlNparams$-parameter) neural network
for triaging synthetic \Rom{}-like light curves into six classes: no detectable event, single-lens
microlensing, detectable binary-lens anomalies (\texttt{NonPSPL}), and three broad variable-star
classes. Labels are conditioned on explicit synthetic detectability thresholds and on the portion
of the season revealed to the model. BinML recovers $\bmlCompletenessPct\%$ of detectable
\texttt{NonPSPL} events at a target purity of $\bmlPurityTarget$ (achieved $\bmlPurity$) on
complete 72-day light curves in a
held-out $\bmlNtest$-event test set whose detectable-anomaly prevalence is
$\bmlPriorPiZero\%$. Both numbers are conditional on that mixture: at a $1\%$ prevalence the
same threshold yields purity $\bmlPurityOnePct$, and holding purity at $\bmlPrevPurTarget$
instead costs completeness, which falls to $\bmlPrevCompOne$. A disjoint
$\bmlStressNatN$-million-event set drawn from the same priors reproduces this complete-season
macro-F1, whereas targeted out-of-distribution tests expose several failures. We evaluate
$\bmlCascN$ eligible simulated binary lenses in half-day increments. BinML alerts on
$\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ within the season; $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ alert
before a truth-informed, noise-free first-detectable onset (95\% CI
$[\bmlCascPrematureLo,\bmlCascPrematureHi]\%$), and non-premature alerts have a median delay of
$\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days. That scan covers eligible binaries only, so it measures timing
rather than streaming purity or alert burden; the latency supports post-hoc modelling priority
rather than real-time triggering of hours-long perturbations. Out-of-distribution tests bound the operating envelope: on faint sources anomaly precision falls
to $\bmlStressFaintPrec$, which would dominate any follow-up budget. Validation is synthetic
throughout, on a legacy-like schedule with an adopted $0.02$~mag detectability floor, so these
are benchmark values rather than \Rom{} yield forecasts. Code, weights, and reproducibility artifacts are
released openly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gravitational microlensing complements other planet-detection methods because it responds to a
lens's mass rather than its light. It is sensitive to cold, low-mass planets far from their host
stars and to unbound planetary-mass objects drifting through the Galaxy---populations that transit
and radial-velocity surveys, biased toward hot and close-in planets, recover less readily
\citep{Mao1991,GouldLoeb1992,Suzuki2016}. The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope will exploit
this sensitivity systematically. Its Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey (GBTDS) will monitor the
crowded bulge fields at high cadence in the near-infrared. It is predicted to detect of order a
thousand bound planets and to constrain the free-floating planet mass function
\citep{Spergel2015,Penny2019,Johnson2020,Sumi2023,Gaudi2012}, and the same data will yield
transiting planets \citep{Wilson2023}. In its design
\citep{ROTAC2025,STScI2025GBTDS}, the wide F146 filter samples the light curve every
$\sim12$~minutes; two auxiliary bands, F087 and F213, add colour information on staggered
$\sim$6-hour cycles.

That sensitivity brings an operational problem of scale. Genuine microlensing events are a small
minority of the survey light curves, detectable binary-lens anomalies are rarer still, and fitting
a full binary-lens model to every source is impractical. A cheap classifier can therefore rank
which partial light curves deserve inspection or detailed modelling. Timing matters to that task:
before a caustic perturbation appears, a binary lens can resemble an ordinary single lens. A model
evaluated only on complete seasons can assign an anomalous label before the evidence that defines
it has entered the observed window. Existing rapid-response systems already show the value of
low-latency anomaly detection in microlensing \citep{Udalski2003,Dominik2007,Dominik2008,Tsapras2009}.
Here we ask a narrower question: as an eligible synthetic binary is progressively revealed, when
does the classifier first cross an anomaly threshold relative to a truth-informed onset?

Machine learning has already been used at the scale relevant to microlensing surveys.
\citet{Wyrzykowski2015} searched 150 million OGLE-III sources and classified 3,718 candidates, while
\citet{Husseiniova2021} applied a machine-learning search to roughly 700 million VVV light curves;
both were completed-data searches. The random-forest microlensing classifier of \citet{Godines2019} and neural networks on
ground-based photometry \citep{Mroz2020} address related classification tasks. KMTNet's completed-event finder \citep{Kim2018EventFinder} and the subsequent
\textsc{AnomalyFinder} analyses \citep{Zang2022}, which search residuals from fitted single-lens
models, demonstrate systematic event and anomaly discovery in real survey data. For simulated high-cadence \Rom{} light curves,
\citet{Khakpash2021} developed morphology and model-fit features intended for rapid classification.
In the alert-broker setting, Fink's LIA module
performs real-time microlensing classification but does not include a binary-anomaly class
\citep{Moller2021}. For \Rom{}, \citet{Zhang2021} demonstrated real-time posterior inference for a
known binary-lens event; that is downstream parameter inference, not discovery in the full stellar
stream. Most closely, \citet{CrispimRomao2025} evaluated an isolation-forest alarm on drip-fed
synthetic LSST light curves containing microlensing, binary lenses, and variables. Their online
stage separates signal from constant sources and their physical classification is offline; our
contribution is a supervised six-class \Rom{}-like model with time-dependent labels. Related
time-domain work also studies early classification of explosive transients
\citep{Muthukrishna2019}. Architecturally we use standard time-series transformer components
\citep{Astromer2023,Vaswani2017}, and our evaluation shares the prior-shift concern highlighted by
PLAsTiCC \citep{Hlozek2020}.

Across this work we find no trained classifier that combines all of the elements BinML needs:
multi-band \Rom{}-cadence input, a binary and planetary anomaly class evaluated alongside the
variable-star populations that contaminate it, operation on partial rather than completed light
curves, and released weights. The searches above are single-band, completed-data, or
binary-blind; the \Rom{}-specific work stops at feature definition or at downstream inference
for an already-identified event. To our knowledge BinML is the first released model to occupy
that combination, which is what the rest of this paper evaluates.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we introduce \emph{detectability-conditioned
labelling} (\S\ref{sec:sims}): events are labelled by whether they cross adopted synthetic
thresholds, aligning the training target with that synthetic policy.
Second, we build a \emph{partial-light-curve cascade} (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) by making each label a
function of how much of the season has been revealed. Third, we evaluate the model at synthetic
survey scale (\S\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:limits}):
completeness at a fixed purity as the headline metric, a selection-aware reweighting that corrects
for the training distribution, and a $\bmlStressN$-million-event stress collection spanning an
independent same-prior sample and targeted out-of-range regimes.

\subsection{Six classes across three bands}
We simulate \Rom{} GBTDS light curves in the six selected classes of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}.
\texttt{Flat} sources show no
detectable event. \texttt{PSPL} events are single-lens, point-source point-lens microlensing
\citep{Paczynski1986}. \texttt{NonPSPL} events---the anomalous, science-critical class---are
binary and planetary lenses, computed with the contour-integration code \textsc{VBBinaryLensing}
\citep{Bozza2010,Bozza2018}, and are distinguished by the caustic perturbations visible in the
third panel of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}. The remaining three classes are the variable-star
populations that dominate the real bulge and are the classifier's most dangerous contaminants:
\texttt{PeriodicVar} (RR~Lyrae, eclipsing binaries, $\delta$~Scuti), \texttt{LongPeriodVar}
(Miras, semiregulars, and OSARGs---small-amplitude red-giant pulsators---whose slow rise most
readily mimics a long-timescale microlensing event), and \texttt{Eruptive} (dwarf novae and Be
outbursts). We adopt an authored truncated log-normal event-timescale distribution whose mean is
anchored to an empirical inner-bulge estimate \citep{Mroz2019}. Binary mass ratio and projected
separation are instead drawn log-uniformly over broad analytic supports; they are not a fitted
population model \citep{Suzuki2016,Zhang2021}. Variable-star periods and amplitudes are motivated
by OGLE bulge catalogues, but the generated curves are phenomenological analytic waveform families
rather than sampled empirical templates \citep{Soszynski2013,Soszynski2014}. Every event is realised
on a hybrid \Rom{}-like schedule: legacy-like $\sim$15-minute wide-band sampling and a single
72-day season, combined with 6-hour visits in each of the current F087 and F213 auxiliary filters.
The curves are degraded by photometric noise, blending, and interstellar extinction. The released
model was trained on this legacy schedule (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

The source population is likewise parametric rather than a Galactic population synthesis. The
observed F146 baseline is uniform from 20 to 25~mag and $A_{K_s}$ is uniform from 0.2 to 1.2,
sampled independently. Baseline colour offsets come from a grey intrinsic source spectrum plus the
adopted extinction law; intrinsic stellar colour and its correlations with luminosity, variability,
and line of sight are not modelled. These choices give a classifier benchmark broad coverage.

The \texttt{NonPSPL} generator contains static binary lenses (2L1S) with finite-source
magnification, but not binary-source (1L2S) events, annual parallax, or lens orbital motion. The
1L2S omission is the most consequential of the three: a binary source produces a bump on an
otherwise single-lens curve that can mimic a binary-lens anomaly, and it is a long-recognised
degeneracy with planetary-mass 2L1S perturbations \citep{Gaudi1998}. It is therefore the dominant
astrophysical false positive that our purity estimate does not contain, and adding it would
lower the reported \texttt{NonPSPL} purity by an amount we have not measured. Annual parallax
and lens orbital motion distort the light-curve shape but do not manufacture a caustic-like
feature, so we expect them to matter less over a single 72-day season. The contaminant set
likewise omits many real irregular and correlated systematics.

\subsection{Labelling by an adopted synthetic detectability policy}\label{sec:detcond}
BinML's policy-conditioned synthetic labels are set by explicit thresholds applied to simulated,
noise-free curves, rather than by generator class alone; we use ``detectability-conditioned'' as
shorthand for this policy. A microlensing event whose in-window amplitude falls below the adopted
floor is labelled \texttt{Flat}. Likewise, a binary whose caustic perturbation falls below the
anomaly thresholds is labelled \texttt{PSPL}. For binaries we measure the departure of the
noiseless curve from a refitted single-lens model and withhold the anomalous label when that
departure is too small under the policy.

The class definitions---and therefore every metric in this paper---depend on these thresholds. An
event must reach $\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{event}} \ge 500$ against a flat baseline to be a
detected event at all; a binary must reach $\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{anom}} \ge 160$ against its
best-fitting single-lens model to keep the anomalous label; and both statistics must exceed a peak
amplitude of $0.02$~mag. Both $\Delta\chi^{2}$ values are computed on noise-free curves, so neither
is a likelihood-ratio statistic: under the null each is identically zero, and each grows in
proportion to the epoch count. At \Rom{}'s sampling that makes both $\chi^{2}$ cuts close to
vestigial and leaves the $0.02$~mag amplitude floor as the operative criterion. That floor is an
adopted simulation policy rather than a measured instrumental limit. Section~\ref{sec:ablations}
measures what the labelling scheme buys against the alternative of labelling by generator intent.

The scale of the relabelling is the point. Across the test set, $\bmlRelabelled\%$ of events
receive a policy-conditioned label that differs from the class they were generated as. The effect is
concentrated where it should be. Of the events generated as binary or planetary lenses, only
$\bmlNonpsplKept\%$ show a detectable anomaly and keep the anomalous label; $\bmlNonpsplToPspl\%$
have a caustic perturbation below those thresholds and are labelled as single lenses. Similarly,
$\bmlLpvToFlat\%$ of the long-period variables vary too weakly within a single season to be
detected and are labelled flat. Training against generator
intent asks the network to assign an anomalous or variable label on about a third of its examples
even though the corresponding signal falls below the adopted policy threshold.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{outputs/figures/schematic.pdf}
\caption{BinML architecture. Each of the three \Rom{} bands is binned onto a fixed grid whose
bins carry five channels---baseline-relative mean, min, and max magnitude, observed fraction, and
an observed mask. A convolutional stem, whose non-learned min/max lanes preserve caustic extrema
by construction, reduces the bands to $\bmlNtokens$ tokens that a four-layer transformer
classifies into six classes. The whole model has $\bmlNparams$ parameters.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure*}

BinML has two parts (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}). The first is a small convolutional network---a
stack of learned filters that scan along the light curve looking for local shapes, the same kind
of building block used in image recognition. The second is a transformer, a now-standard
architecture that lets every part of the light curve ``attend to'' every other part, so the model
can weigh, for example, a brief brightening against the overall trend around it. Together they
come to just $\bmlNparams$ trainable numbers, which by the standards of modern deep learning is
tiny. The small size is deliberate. We are not short of training data---we simulate of order ten
million events---so the pressure is not to build a larger model but to build one cheap enough to
run on the entire survey, night after night. A compact network that classifies a light curve in a
single pass is far more useful here than a heavy one applied only to a pre-selected handful of
candidates.

The model reads the light curve in a form that keeps the survey's own timing. Each of the three
filters is divided into a fixed set of time bins across the 72-day observing season: the workhorse
F146 filter into $\bmlBinsF$ bins (from $\bmlEpochsF$ individual exposures taken every
$\bmlCadence$~minutes), and the two colour filters into 96 bins each. For every bin the model is
given five simple numbers: the average brightness in that bin, the brightest and faintest points
in it, the fraction of the bin that was actually observed, and a flag saying whether it was
observed at all. All brightnesses are measured relative to a single quiet-state (baseline)
level defined in F146 and subtracted from all three filters. This removes absolute source
brightness while retaining the simulated inter-band offsets, including the effects of extinction
and blending, but not an independently measured intrinsic source colour.

The training and frozen evaluations use the simulator-provided F146 baseline. A deployed pipeline
would estimate that level from a partial, possibly magnified light curve; the released inference
interface includes an estimator, whose error we have not evaluated against the generator value.

Keeping the brightest and faintest point in each bin, rather than only the average, retains narrow
caustic structure during time binning. In the largest measured case, mean pooling
would reduce a nearly three-magnitude excursion to about a third of a magnitude. We therefore
propagate exact extrema through each strided reduction stage before a learned projection combines
the channels; the extrema are not supplied unchanged to the final classifier. When a colour filter
is missing for an event---the
bluer F087 filter yields no usable epoch for $\bmlNoFilterBlue\%$ of detectable-anomaly
events behind heavy dust---the model is simply told to
ignore it rather than being fed a guess. A final pooling step combines the processed bins into a
single summary, from which a standard classifier reads off the six class probabilities. The model
uses only the binned brightnesses; it does not use the individual error bars.

This compactness makes repeated survey-scale scoring practical. Classification is a single forward
pass with no per-event fitting. On a $\bmlInferCores$-core \bmlInferCpu{} CPU with
$\bmlInferThreads$ threads, the model scores $\bmlInferEps$ pre-tokenised light curves per second
($\bmlInferMs$~ms per event) in batches of 1024. \code{validation/inference\_benchmark.py}
regenerates this together with the processor, thread count and timing protocol; an earlier
revision quoted a figure roughly three times larger whose hardware and script were not retained
and which we could not reproduce, so it is withdrawn.

\section{Partial-light-curve evaluation}\label{sec:cascade}

weak anomaly, and the single-lens control (right) acquires no appreciable \texttt{NonPSPL}

\texttt{NonPSPL} threshold crossing on eligible binaries, not the full three-state sequence.}
\label{fig:cascade}
\end{figure*}

A streaming classifier receives a partial rather than a finished light curve. Before an anomalous
feature appears, a binary lens may be photometrically consistent with a single lens, so assigning
the complete-season class to every prefix would teach the network a target unsupported by the
revealed window.

We instead make the label a function of observation time. For each binary we compute the day
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ at which its anomaly first crosses the adopted thresholds. This reference is
truth-informed: it is derived from the simulated, noise-free light curve and a single-lens fit
seeded with injected parameters, rather than from an observable broker statistic. During training
the network sees light curves truncated at random days: a
binary truncated \emph{before} $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ carries the \texttt{PSPL} label (with the
detectability floor re-evaluated on the truncated data), and earns \texttt{NonPSPL} only once the
anomaly lies inside the revealed window. The resulting training target follows the cascade
\texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}.

The prefix labels for other classes are also generator-informed. Single-lens amplitude is
evaluated from injected parameters, an eruptive source changes class at its injected start time,
and periodic or long-period variability uses an analytic amplitude--period proxy. That proxy does
not evaluate the realised waveform, phase, noisy measurements, or irregular sampling.

Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} shows the cascade at the level of individual events. As the clean binary
is revealed, BinML's probability mass moves from \texttt{Flat} to \texttt{PSPL} during the smooth
magnification and only jumps to \texttt{NonPSPL} when the caustic crosses into view at
$t_{\mathrm{anom}}$; the subtle binary follows the same sequence with a weaker, later anomaly; and
the single-lens control never acquires meaningful \texttt{NonPSPL} probability.

\paragraph{Definitions.} We fix four terms and keep the same denominators throughout. An
\emph{eligible event} is a simulated binary whose anomaly survives detectability-conditioned
labelling and has a finite onset time; eligibility never depends on the model's output. A
\emph{threshold crossing} is a revealed window at which $P(\texttt{NonPSPL})$ reaches the operating
threshold. The \emph{alert} is the first such crossing; an event with no crossing before the season
ends is \emph{right-censored}. An alert is \emph{premature} if it precedes the onset, and the
\emph{lag} is the alert time minus the onset time, negative for premature alerts.

\paragraph{Protocol.} We measure event by event rather than on isolated snapshots, because a live
stream re-evaluates every light curve as each new epoch arrives. For each of $\bmlCascN$ eligible
binaries we reveal the season in half-day steps and record the alert. The primary scan reveals
\emph{only the F146 band}; revealing all three bands is reported separately below. All
sensitivities are the same $\bmlCascN$ events reduced under different rules, from one stored scan.
The scan holds prefix traces only for eligible binaries---none for demoted binaries, single lenses,
flat sources, or variables---and its operating threshold was selected on complete-season validation
light curves. It therefore measures detection and timing conditional on binary eligibility, not the
sequential false-alert rate, purity, or alert burden.

\paragraph{When does the anomaly begin?} The generator records the truth-informed onset on a coarse
$7.2$-day grid, which rounds it \emph{upward}: an alert arriving within that window is scored
premature even though the anomaly was already detectable. The median inflation is
$\bmlCascOnsetShift$~days. Worse, the anomaly statistic is not monotone in revealed time---as more
of the season arrives, the best-fitting single-lens model can absorb an early deviation and push
the residual back below threshold, which happens for $\bmlCascNonMonotone$ of our $\bmlCascN$
events. ``The onset'' is therefore genuinely ambiguous, and we bracket it rather than choose:
\emph{first-detectable} is the first half-day cut at which the anomaly is detectable at all, and
\emph{persistently-detectable} is the cut after which it stays detectable to the end of the season.
The first is permissive, the second strict; we report both.

\paragraph{Results.} BinML alerts on $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of eligible binaries within the season;
the remaining $\bmlCascCensored$ are right-censored. The alert precedes the anomaly onset for
$\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ of eligible events (95\% CI
$[\bmlCascPrematureLo,\bmlCascPrematureHi]\%$) under the first-detectable onset, rising to
$\bmlCascPrematurePersistPct\%$ ($[\bmlCascPrematurePersistLo,\bmlCascPrematurePersistHi]\%$) under
the strict persistent definition. Among alerts that were not premature the median lag is
$\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days (first-detectable) or $\bmlCascMedianLagPersist$~days (persistent).
Because premature alerts are a small fraction, the median over \emph{all} $\bmlCascNdet$ alerts is
also $\bmlCascMedianLagAll$~days.

\paragraph{How the alert rule changes the answer.} Because the whole scan is stored, we can vary
the decision rule on the same events. Coarsening the evaluation grid from half-daily to daily
leaves the premature rate at $\bmlCascGridOnePct\%$ but costs detection
($\bmlCascGridOneDet\%$); at two-day steps it falls to $\bmlCascGridTwoPct\%$, again with fewer
detections. Requiring two consecutive crossings before alerting---one plausible debouncing
rule---gives $\bmlCascPersistTwoPct\%$ premature at $\bmlCascPersistTwoDet\%$ detection.
Revealing all three bands rather than F146 alone reduces the premature fraction to
$\bmlCascMultibandPct\%$ and raises detection to $\bmlCascMultibandDet\%$, but worsens the median
non-premature delay from $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$ to $\bmlCascMultibandMedianLag$~days. Alerting
whenever \texttt{NonPSPL} is simply the most probable class, ignoring the threshold, raises
detection to near-complete but takes the premature rate to $\bmlCascArgmaxPct\%$. The measured
timing is therefore strongly policy-dependent.

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:eval}

Three choices define what the synthetic benchmark measures.

The first is how we summarise performance. A single accuracy figure is misleading here, because
the anomalous events are so rare that a model could score well simply by never flagging one. What
a follow-up team actually needs to know is a pair of numbers. One is \emph{purity}: of the events
the model flags as anomalous, what fraction really are. The other is \emph{completeness}: of the
anomalous events that exist, what fraction the model catches. These are precisely the quantities
tabulated as precision and recall in Table~\ref{tab:perclass}, and their harmonic mean is the F1
score there (macro-F1 being its unweighted average over the six classes). These trade off against each
other---flag more events and you catch more real ones but also let in more false ones---so we fix
the purity to a chosen value, $\bmlPurityTarget$, and report the completeness reached at that
setting as our headline number. This is exactly the form in which a survey team would state a
requirement: ``keep the flagged anomaly sample at least ninety per cent pure, and report how many
detectable anomalies remain.'' This is not the same as planet completeness because
\texttt{NonPSPL} also contains stellar binaries.

The second is a partial correction for how the training set was built. To train efficiently we
subsample the abundant demoted-binary by-products, and each test event carries a weight that
reverses \emph{that} subsampling, applied to purity (which depends on the mix of false positives)
but not to completeness (a per-class property). The weighting undoes one internal sampling step; it
does \emph{not} reweight the deliberately chosen generator class quotas to a survey event-rate
prior.

The third is a reference to judge the anomaly ranking against. For each event we can compute, from
the noiseless light curve and the true parameters, its injected anomaly \dchisq{}---in effect how
detectable the anomaly would be to an observer who already knew the answer. This is a
\emph{truth-informed reference}, not an oracle upper bound: it uses information the survey never
has, and a learned model exploiting other morphology could in principle rank better on real, noisy
data. Comparing BinML against it measures how much of the injected
anomaly ordering the model reconstructs from the raw measurements alone.

\section{Generalisation and limits}\label{sec:limits}

Held-out numbers can in principle reflect memorised training examples, so we test generalisation
directly: both to unseen parameter draws from the same priors and, through the out-of-range regimes
below, to parts of parameter space the training distribution barely populates. The
$\bmlStressN$-million-event stress set is drawn from a disjoint pseudo-random stream---seed base
$\bmlSeedStress$ against $\bmlSeedTrain$ for the train/validation/test data---so its parameter
draws are independent of the training draws;
it comprises a $\bmlStressNatN$-million-event natural-population subset and a suite of deliberately
out-of-range regimes. On the natural-population subset BinML reproduces its held-out performance,
with a macro-F1 of $\bmlStressMacroF$.

The out-of-range regimes expose failures. Anomaly recall stays near its in-distribution value of
$\bmlStressNatNp$ across most regimes, but collapses to $\bmlStressWidesep$ for very wide
separations. This recall is conditional on an anomaly already passing the detectability policy, so
the collapse is a classifier failure on the retained wide-separation subset, not relabelling of
uncrossed caustics. Two contaminant regimes fail
outright: \texttt{PeriodicVar} examples whose period exceeds the season are recovered as
\texttt{PeriodicVar} only $\bmlStressLongp$ of the time (a nearly complete failure, since within one
window they are not observably periodic), and sub-day-timescale single lenses are recovered at
$\bmlStressShortte$. For faint sources, \texttt{PSPL} recall falls to $\bmlStressFaintPspl$ and
anomaly \emph{precision} to $\bmlStressFaintPrec$---noise manufacturing spurious anomalies is the
single most consequential failure mode for a follow-up programme. In the low-$q$ regime,
recall holds at $\bmlStressPlanetNp$ but precision falls to $\bmlStressPlanetPrec$.

Each failure has a plausible physical explanation---too few epochs on the peak, a signal that does
not complete within the window, photometric noise comparable to the signal---and the low-$q$ deficit
in particular is a regime the training distribution barely populates. These are the current model's
operating envelope, not established limits of the method. Robustness to missing data is more
encouraging: events observed without the
blue band are still recovered at $\bmlSliceNoBlue$, so the model degrades gracefully when colour is
unavailable.

One boundary is deliberate: BinML is a specialist for dense, nearly continuous space-based
sampling. The non-learned min/max lanes, colour bands, and fine time grid were developed on the
hybrid schedule described above. Archival ground-survey light curves lie outside this evaluated
regime. Earlier ad-hoc applications to selected KMTNet and OGLE events are not treated as results
because their event selection and predictions were not retained in frozen, hashed artifacts.

We instead use simulation to isolate one relevant cadence effect. Existing ground networks such as
the three-site KMTNet \citep{Kim2016} observe through nightly windows separated by daytime gaps. A
matched-density test compares uniform sampling with an eight-hour nightly observing window
\emph{at identical total visit counts}, so that only the gap topology differs. Anomaly recall over
$\bmlGapN$ events is $\bmlGapUniformA$ (uniform; 95\% CI
$[\bmlGapUniformALo,\bmlGapUniformAHi]$) versus $\bmlGapNightlyA$ (gapped; upper bound
$\bmlGapNightlyAHi$) at $\bmlGapVisitsA$~visits per day, and $\bmlGapUniformB$ versus
$\bmlGapNightlyA$ at $\bmlGapVisitsB$~per day; the intervals do not overlap. At
$\bmlGapVisitsC$~visits per day both schedules fail ($\bmlGapUniformC$ uniform), so below that
density sparsity alone dominates and the comparison is uninformative. In the regime where uniform
sampling still works, the gapped schedule does not. This establishes gap sensitivity in the
simulation, not the cause of any behaviour on real data.

Our hybrid schedule---$\sim$15-minute F146 sampling with 6-hour visits in each colour filter over
a single 72-day season---is not an exact historical survey design. The current Galactic Bulge
Time-Domain Survey definition specifies F146 approximately every 12 minutes, F087 and F213 each on
a 6-hour cycle staggered so that one colour or the other is obtained every 3 hours, 66-second
exposures, and six high-cadence plus four low-cadence seasons of about 72 days each
\citep{STScI2025GBTDS,
ROTAC2025}. Our per-band colour cadence therefore already matches the current design; what differs
is the F146 rate, the exposure time and the photometric calibration that follows from it, the
absence of the staggered colour interleaving, and---most consequentially---the multi-season
structure. Our results are a benchmark under a legacy \Rom{}-like schedule, not a forecast under
the survey as it will actually run.

We can, however, bound how much the \emph{sampling rate} alone matters. We repeated the entire
pipeline---simulation, training from scratch, and evaluation---at the current $\sim$12-minute F146
rate and at our legacy 15-minute rate, holding everything else fixed (same shards, seed and
$\bmlCadEpochs$ epochs; $\bmlCadNfifteen$ and $\bmlCadNtwelve$ events survived the
detectability cuts in the two arms). The two arms score closely:
anomaly average precision $\bmlCadFifteenAp$ (15\,min) versus $\bmlCadTwelveAp$ (12\,min), and
completeness $\bmlCadFifteenCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlCadFifteenPurity$ versus
$\bmlCadTwelveCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlCadTwelvePurity$, with no per-class F1 differing by more
than $\bmlCadMaxFOneDelta$. With one seed per arm this bounds the rate change as unimportant at the
resolution we can measure rather than establishing equivalence. Both rates sample an hours-to-day
caustic densely, so a $25\%$ change in epoch count adds little information. Two effects remain
untested. The exposure time and colour-band zeropoints of the current design
differ from ours (Section~\ref{sec:limits}), and their effect on the colour channels is
unquantified. More consequentially, the current design's \emph{structure}---six high-cadence plus
four low-cadence seasons with seasonal gaps---cannot be represented by our single-season,
fixed-window input without an architectural change. That, rather than re-tuning the cadence, is the
most important extension of this work.

The purity we quote is a property of the classifier \emph{and} the assumed prevalence of anomalous
events in the input stream, not of the classifier alone. Our synthetic evaluation mixture contains
detectable anomalies at a prevalence of $\bmlPriorPiZero\%$; the prevalence in a real survey stream
is set by microlensing event rates and any upstream candidate selection, and is typically far
lower. Figure~\ref{fig:prior} makes the dependence explicit. Under a pure prior shift that leaves
the class-conditional score distributions fixed, completeness at the fixed threshold is
prevalence-independent and stays at $\bmlCompleteness$. Purity is not: it falls from the
$\bmlPurity$ we report at the synthetic prevalence to $\bmlPurityOnePct$ at a $1\%$ anomaly
prevalence, and to $\bmlPurityTenthPct$ at $0.1\%$. Read $\bmlPurity$ as conditional on the adopted
mixture; a survey purity would need event-rate priors for the specific input population. Under
prior shift, fixed-threshold completeness transfers and purity does not, which is why we frame
completeness at fixed purity as the reportable quantity.

A survey team could instead re-tune the threshold to its own prevalence and ask what completeness
remains at $\bmlPrevPurTarget$ purity. For each assumed prevalence we select the threshold on the
reserved validation rows and apply it unchanged to the frozen test rows, so the completeness below
is held out rather than the best point of the test set's own precision-recall curve. At the
synthetic mixture's prevalence the answer is
$\bmlPrevCompSynth$, essentially our headline; at a $1\%$ anomaly prevalence it falls to
$\bmlPrevCompOne$; and at $0.1\%$ to $\bmlPrevCompTenth$. The degradation is arithmetic: holding
purity fixed as the contaminant pool grows requires a stricter threshold, which costs completeness.
Any deployment should plan against the figure matching its own input prevalence, not against
$\bmlCompleteness$.

This transfer is prior shift and nothing more: we rescale the class mixture and hold the
class-conditional score distributions fixed. It is the right model for a survey whose events look
like ours but arrive in different proportions, and the wrong one for a survey whose events look
different---different cadence, blending, or variables our simulator does not contain.

The colour bands carry a calibration error. Our simulated zeropoints were anchored to the published
Cycle-7 values for the wide band and scaled for F087 and F213, and an audit against the current
Roman zeropoint tables \citep{RomanTechInfo} shows the wide-band value is accurate to $0.03$~mag while
our F087 and F213 zeropoints are optimistic by roughly $0.10$ and $0.14$~mag respectively. Two
further colour-band assumptions are less defensible. Our F087
saturation limit was carried over from a much longer Cycle-7 exposure, and it errs in the wrong
direction: at equal exposure F087 should saturate at a \emph{fainter} magnitude than F146, not a
brighter one. Our sky-background ratios also fail to reproduce the published thermal backgrounds,
which make F213 substantially worse and F087 substantially better relative to F146 than we assume.
Both errors sit in the colour bands, which the model uses chiefly to reject variable-star
contaminants. Quantifying the resulting bias requires regenerating the training set and retraining,
or at minimum a colour/noise ablation; the affected quantity is most plausibly contaminant rejection
rather than the anomaly channel. The corrected values are recorded alongside the originals in the
released code so that any future retraining starts from the audited numbers.

The boundaries between our classes---flat versus event, and single lens versus anomalous---are set
not only by a goodness-of-fit threshold but also by a minimum detectable amplitude, which we fix at
twenty times an assumed one-millimagnitude systematic floor. This floor stands in for the
correlated, night-to-week photometric systematics
that do not average down the way independent per-epoch noise does, and which no real pipeline can
follow below some level; we adopt a fixed amplitude cut rather than modelling a full red-noise
process. Because that choice sits on both class boundaries at once, the absolute completeness and
purity we quote will shift if the true systematic floor differs from our assumption. Repeating the
labelling, training, and evaluation across plausible red-noise floors is therefore a prerequisite
before the numbers are used to set a survey requirement.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

BinML is intended as a cheap first-pass triage layer before full binary-lens modelling. The
completeness-at-fixed-purity framing exposes the tradeoff a survey team would need to set, and the
network's forward pass is fast enough for repeated large-scale scoring. Those properties establish
a computational benchmark rather than a deployable broker: the evaluation uses synthetic
photometry, a simulator-provided baseline, a truth-informed onset, and half-day rather than
native-cadence updates.

Which follow-up modes does this latency serve? A median alert $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days after
onset arrives once a short caustic crossing has finished, so for the brief planetary anomalies that
motivate the survey BinML is a \emph{post-hoc} identifier rather than a trigger for the feature
itself.

A few-day latency could still serve two other tasks. The first is anomalies that are not brief:
extended or repeating caustic structure, second crossings, and long-timescale binaries whose
perturbations persist for days. The second is everything that depends on
recognising the \emph{event} rather than the feature---scheduling denser photometry for the rest
of an ongoing event, initiating colour or parallax campaigns, and prioritising which light curves
receive expensive binary-lens modelling.
Triggering on the anomaly itself would require evaluating at the survey's native cadence: our grid
sensitivity (\S\ref{sec:cascade}) covers only grids coarser than the half-day one, where the
premature rate does not rise, and does not extrapolate to a $15$-minute grid with far more
evaluation opportunities.

Latency does not vary strongly with mass ratio: the median post-onset lag is within a day of the
population value above $q=10^{-4}$, though the detection fraction falls for $10^{-4}<q<10^{-3}$.
We did not stratify by anomaly duration or caustic topology, so the follow-up modes above are a
reading of what a multi-day latency can serve rather than a measurement of those subpopulations.

\subsection{Ablating the two mechanisms}\label{sec:ablations}

Both of this paper's methodological claims are testable by removing the mechanism and retraining.
Each ablation trains arms from scratch on identical shards with identical hyper-parameters and
seed, differing only in the stated variable, and evaluates them on a common held-out set from an
unseen seed base. These are small-scale runs (16 shards, $\bmlAbLabelEpochs$ epochs) rather than
production trainings, so absolute values sit below the headline numbers and the comparison between
arms is the result. The two ablations come from separate runs whose evaluation sets differ by 18
events out of $\sim$45{,}000, so arms should be compared within a run, not across them.

\paragraph{Detectability-conditioned labelling.} The intervention is the training label alone.
Both principal arms use weights derived from the policy-conditioned class balance, so they differ in
the target label but not in the weighting objective. An additional arm using label-derived weights
is retained as a sensitivity check.

Against the policy-conditioned label, macro-F1 falls from $\bmlAbLabelObsMacroFOne$ to
$\bmlAbLabelGenMacroFOne$ when the network is trained on generator intent---a gap of
$\bmlAbLabelGap$ (95\% CI $[\bmlAbLabelGapLo,\bmlAbLabelGapHi]$, paired bootstrap over the
$\bmlAbLabelNeval$ evaluation events).
Every arm is scored against \emph{both} label sets, and against the generator label the ordering
reverses, $\bmlAbLabelObsMacroFOneGen$ versus $\bmlAbLabelGenMacroFOneGen$: each arm wins on the
ontology it was trained for. The experiment shows optimization alignment, not that the
policy-conditioned ontology is objectively correct.

The damage concentrates where the relabelling acts: \texttt{PSPL} F1 moves from
$\bmlAbLabelObsPsplFOne$ to $\bmlAbLabelGenPsplFOne$ and \texttt{NonPSPL} from
$\bmlAbLabelObsNonpsplFOne$ to $\bmlAbLabelGenNonpsplFOne$. The generator-intent arm is not merely
worse but differently behaved: at the frozen threshold it flags almost every event, reaching
completeness $\bmlAbLabelGenComp$ at a purity of $\bmlAbLabelGenPur$, because it was trained to call
sub-threshold binaries anomalous.

The label-derived-weight sensitivity arm scores $\bmlAbLabelGenOwnWMacroFOne$ against the
policy-conditioned label, compared with $\bmlAbLabelGenMacroFOne$ when the weights are pinned. Weighting
does affect the operating threshold: the sensitivity arm never crosses the frozen threshold.

The mechanism is concrete. Across the final test split, $\bmlRelabelled\%$ of events carry a
policy-conditioned label different from their generated class; a fifth of all events ($\bmlGenNpDemoted\%$,
half of all disagreements) are generated binaries whose anomaly falls below the detection
thresholds. A network trained on generator intent is asked to assign those binaries the anomalous
label despite their perturbations falling below the adopted policy threshold.

Both arms are single training seeds, so the interval above covers evaluation noise, not
initialisation noise; a seed sweep is the next step.

\paragraph{The truncation augmentation: unresolved.} At a single threshold the second ablation
does not identify a timing benefit. Removing the truncation augmentation changes
the premature-alert rate in opposite directions depending on the decision rule: at the frozen
operating threshold the augmented model is better ($\bmlAbCascOnPremThr$ versus
$\bmlAbCascOffPremThr$), but under six-class argmax it is worse ($\bmlAbCascOnPremArgmax$ versus
$\bmlAbCascOffPremArgmax$), on $N=\bmlAbCascN$ binaries. It is also expensive: within-season
detection at the same threshold falls from $\bmlAbCascOffDet$ to $\bmlAbCascOnDet$, and static
macro-F1 from $\bmlAbCascOffMacroFOne$ to $\bmlAbCascOnMacroFOne$.

These changes are consistent with the augmented model saying \texttt{NonPSPL} less often, lowering
premature alerts and genuine detections together. A threshold-only comparison cannot separate that
conservatism from better temporal ordering.

\paragraph{Exploratory risk--coverage comparison.} Using stored probability traces for both arms
on the same $\bmlMatchN$ eligible binaries, we select a separate threshold for each arm to attain
each of six common within-season detection fractions (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade_matched}). The augmented arm has fewer premature alerts
from $0.50$ through $0.90$ detection: at $0.60$ the rates are $\bmlMatchOnSixty\%$ and
$\bmlMatchOffSixty\%$, and at $0.80$ they are $\bmlMatchOnEighty\%$ and
$\bmlMatchOffEighty\%$. The ordering reverses at $0.95$ detection.
The separation at the lower rates is one-sided at the level of individual events: at $0.60$
detection the two arms disagree on 16 events, and all 16 are premature for the unaugmented arm
and none for the augmented one, so the augmented arm's premature set is a strict subset of the
unaugmented arm's on this sample.

The thresholds are selected and the premature-alert rates evaluated on the same $\bmlMatchN$ events, and
both training arms use one seed, so this curve is descriptive rather than confirmatory. A causal
timing claim requires thresholds selected on a disjoint calibration sample, paired evaluation on
held-out events, and repeated training seeds.

Two exploratory fine-tuning runs remain as archived JSON summaries rather than results here: each
used a single training seed, and the retained outputs lack the provenance and per-event pairs

The natural next step is validation on real events in the model's own regime. Faint-source false
anomalies, the baseline estimate, and colour-band calibration are the specific priorities for that
validation; sparse archival data may be better served by models trained for that sampling than by
making BinML cadence-agnostic.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

BinML is a compact multi-band classifier for six classes of synthetic, progressively revealed
\Rom{}-like light curves. On the adopted class mixture it reaches completeness
$\bmlCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlPurity$ for detectable \texttt{NonPSPL} events. These values are
conditional on the simulation and its class priors; they are not planet yields or survey purity.

At half-day resolution the model alerts on $\bmlCascDetFrac\%$ of eligible simulated binaries.
Relative to a truth-informed, noise-free onset, $\bmlCascPrematurePct\%$ alert early and the
non-premature alerts have a median delay of $\bmlCascMedianLagNonPrem$~days. The scan covers
eligible binaries only, under a threshold selected on complete seasons, so it measures timing
rather than streaming purity. The matched-risk comparison between models trained with and without
truncation is exploratory---thresholds and outcomes use the same $\bmlMatchN$ events---and does not
establish a causal timing benefit.

The principal remaining tests are a mixed-class prefix scan that measures sequential false alerts
and alert burden, an estimated-baseline ablation, sensitivity to the adopted $0.02$~mag floor,
disjoint calibration and evaluation of the streaming policy with repeated training seeds,
retraining under the current multi-season survey design, and validation on real photometry. The
released code and weights provide a rebuildable static benchmark, with per-artifact provenance
recorded below.

\section*{Data availability}
BinML, its trained weights, and the full simulation-to-inference pipeline are openly available at
\url{https://github.com/kunalb541/BinML}. Reproducibility comes at three distinct levels, and we
distinguish them because they are not equally cheap.

\emph{Manuscript rebuild.} Every number, table and figure in this paper is generated by the build
script from the artifacts distributed with a release, with no access to any local output directory.
This runs in minutes on a laptop and is exercised in continuous integration from a clean
\code{git archive} of the repository.

\emph{Analysis regeneration.} The frozen evaluation artifact---per-event logits, labels, generator
classes, selection weights, event parameters and the final-test index, with a manifest recording
each array's shape, dtype and hash---supports the static results in
\S\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:limits}. The streaming results come from a separate archived scan
(\code{validation/cascade\_trace.npz}, the full probability trace for the frozen $\bmlCascN$-event
sample) and the ablations and auxiliary experiments from their own archived JSON artifacts, each
with the script that produced it. Re-deriving any reported statistic from these requires only the
reduction scripts; regenerating the artifacts themselves requires the model and the simulator, and
takes minutes to hours. Two provenance limitations remain in the archived runs. The primary trace
records the checkpoint hash but was generated from a dirty tree without recording its patch; the
matched-cascade trace records neither source nor checkpoint hashes. The labelling ablation's source
hash was repaired after the run because the in-run hash was empty. The earlier ablation run that
supplies the truncation-augmentation comparison predates this harness entirely and records no
commit, data or checkpoint hashes at all; its artifact carries a provenance block saying so. Its
labelling arms are superseded by the fixed-weight rerun, and the cascade comparison it still
supplies is the one we report as \emph{not} supporting our claim. These disclosures are retained
in the artifacts; future executions fail on an empty or mismatched source hash, but the published
experiments have not been rerun solely to replace those metadata fields.

\emph{Training reproduction.} The multi-million-event training set is too large to host and is
available on request; it can also be regenerated from the released simulation code, since each
shard is seeded deterministically by its index. Reproducing the released checkpoint, the ablation
arms or the fine-tuning runs means rerunning that generation and the training, which is the
cloud-scale cost described in Appendix~\ref{app:infra}. The build script does not do this, and no
claim in this paper should be read as implying that it does.

\appendix

\section{Simulation and training infrastructure}\label{app:infra}

For reproducibility we describe the compute configuration used to generate the data and train the
model. We did not retain billing records and therefore do not report a currency cost.

\paragraph{Simulation (AWS).} The training set of roughly $\bmlSimEvents$~million events was
generated on Amazon Web Services in the \texttt{\bmlSimRegion} region, on a small fleet of
$\bmlSimInstances$ transient (Spot) instances of $\bmlSimVcpus$ virtual CPUs each. The generator is
embarrassingly parallel: one process builds one self-contained shard of a few thousand light
curves, seeded purely by its index, so a shard that is lost to a Spot interruption costs at most
that shard and the run resumes by skipping shards already written to cloud storage. At a measured
throughput of about $\bmlSimEps$ events per second across the fleet, the full set was produced in
roughly $\bmlSimHours$~hours. Storage use and cloud price vary with provider, region, and date and
are not reconstructed here.

\paragraph{Training recipe.} The production model was trained with AdamW (learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $0.01$), batch size $\bmlTrainBatch$, cross-entropy loss with the
selection weights of \S\ref{sec:eval} and an additional factor $2$ on the anomaly class, and the
truncation augmentation applied to half of all training examples. Seeds are fixed
($\bmlSeedTrain$) and the data loader is seeded per worker. The recorded production run completed
$\bmlTrainEpochs$ epochs; the released checkpoint is zero-based epoch 4, selected by the highest
validation-set \texttt{NonPSPL} F1. Fine-tuning runs use a reduced learning rate of $5\times10^{-5}$.

\paragraph{Training (local).} The network is small enough that it was
trained end to end on a personal computer, on the \bmlTrainDevice, at a batch size of
$\bmlTrainBatch$. A benchmark of the training step (forward and backward pass on a full batch)
sustains about $\bmlTrainEps$ events per second, which puts a full training run of a few passes
over the $\bmlSimEvents$-million-event set at roughly $\bmlTrainHours$~hours of wall-clock, and the
production model was trained in this range. On local hardware no compute is rented, so the marginal
training cost is local electricity; we did not estimate it. The compactness of the network
(\S\ref{sec:model}) is what
keeps both training and survey-scale inference within reach of a single workstation rather than
requiring a dedicated computing allocation.
