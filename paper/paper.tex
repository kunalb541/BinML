\begin{abstract}
The Nancy Grace \Rom{} Space Telescope Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey (GBTDS) will
monitor hundreds of millions of stars at $\sim$15-minute cadence, yielding a microlensing
data stream far too large to vet by hand and rich enough that the science-critical events---%
binary and planetary lenses---announce themselves only during brief caustic anomalies. We
present \textbf{BinML}, a compact ($\bmlNparams$-parameter) convolutional-stem-plus-transformer
classifier that sorts three-band \Rom{} light curves into six physically-meaningful classes
(flat, single-lens \texttt{PSPL}, anomalous \texttt{NonPSPL}, and three variable-star
contaminant classes). BinML introduces two ideas aimed at survey deployment rather than
benchmark accuracy. First, \emph{detectability-conditioned labelling}: an event is labelled by
what is \emph{observable} in the photometry, not by the parameters used to generate it, so a
buried anomaly is honestly a single lens and the model is never trained to hallucinate evidence
that is not there. Second, a \emph{real-time cascade}: by keying the label on a per-event
anomaly-onset time, the model reports \texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$%
\texttt{NonPSPL} only as each class's evidence arrives, cutting premature binary alerts from
$\bmlPrematureBefore\%$ to $\bmlPrematureAfter\%$ with no loss of full-season discrimination.
On an independent $\bmlNtest$-event test set BinML reaches a completeness of $\bmlCompleteness$
at a fixed anomaly purity of $\bmlPurityTarget$ (average precision $\bmlAP$), recovering the
oracle anomaly-significance ranking (AP $\bmlAPoracle$, computed from the noiseless truth)
almost entirely from raw photometry in a single forward pass and without per-event model fitting.
A $\bmlStressN$-million-event stress test on parameter draws the model provably never saw
reproduces these numbers (macro-F1 $\bmlStressMacroF$), with the only degradations at physically
extreme corners. BinML and its full simulation-to-inference pipeline are released as open source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gravitational microlensing is uniquely sensitive to cold, low-mass planets beyond the snow line
and to unbound planetary-mass objects, regimes that transit and radial-velocity surveys reach
poorly \citep{Mao1991,Suzuki2016}. The \Rom{} GBTDS will exploit this by monitoring the
Galactic bulge at high cadence in the near-infrared, and is predicted to detect of order a
thousand bound planets and to measure the free-floating planet mass function
\citep{Penny2019,Johnson2020,Terry2023}. Its wide filter (F146) samples the peak light curve
every $\sim\bmlCadence$~minutes; two auxiliary bands (F087, F213) add colour at $\sim$6-hour
cadence.

This sensitivity comes with a data-rate and a timing problem. The survey will produce hundreds
of millions of light curves, of which genuine microlensing events are a small fraction and
\emph{anomalous} (binary or planetary) events rarer still. The anomalies that carry the planet
signal are short---often a caustic crossing lasting hours to a day on top of an otherwise smooth
weeks-long magnification. A useful classifier must therefore do two things a conventional
benchmark model is not built for: it must be cheap enough to run on the whole stream, and it
must be honest about time, flagging an anomaly only once the anomaly has actually been seen so
that a follow-up trigger does not fire on a planet that has not yet revealed itself.

Machine-learning classification of microlensing has a short but instructive history. Feature-based
classifiers \citep{Godines2019} and neural networks on lower-cadence data \citep{Mroz2020} have
been demonstrated on OGLE and ZTF; high-cadence, \Rom{}-like studies
\citep{Zhao2021} and early-recognition work \citep{Early2022} are the closest prior art to the
present problem. Transformer architectures, now standard for astronomical time series
\citep{Astromer2023,Vaswani2017}, motivate our encoder. What distinguishes BinML is not the
network but the \emph{labelling and evaluation}: we treat detectability and observation time as
first-class parts of the problem definition.

We make three contributions. (i) \textbf{Detectability-conditioned labelling}
(\S\ref{sec:sims}): events are labelled by observable evidence, removing the label noise that
otherwise penalises a model for not seeing what is unobservable. (ii) \textbf{The real-time
cascade} (\S\ref{sec:cascade}): a per-event anomaly-onset time turns the label into a function
of how much of the season has been revealed, so the model learns
\texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}. (iii) \textbf{Honest,
selection-aware evaluation at survey scale} (\S\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:results}): completeness
at fixed purity as the headline metric, a reweighting that corrects for the training selection,
and a $\bmlStressN$-million-event unseen-parameter stress test.

\section{Simulations and labelling}\label{sec:sims}

\subsection{Six classes, three bands}
We simulate \Rom{} GBTDS light curves in six classes: \texttt{Flat} (no detectable event),
\texttt{PSPL} (single-lens point-source point-lens microlensing), \texttt{NonPSPL} (binary and
planetary lenses---the anomalous, science-critical class), and three variable-star contaminant
populations that dominate the real bulge: \texttt{PeriodicVar} (RR~Lyrae, eclipsing binaries,
$\delta$~Scuti), \texttt{LongPeriodVar} (Miras, semiregulars, OSARGs---the most dangerous
microlensing impostor because a slow rise mimics a long-timescale event), and \texttt{Eruptive}
(dwarf novae, Be outbursts). Single-lens curves use the \citet{Paczynski1986} form; binary and
planetary lenses are computed with the contour-integration code \textsc{VBBinaryLensing}
\citep{Bozza2010,Bozza2018}. Event-timescale, mass-ratio and separation priors follow the
measured bulge distributions \citep{Mroz2019,Suzuki2016}; variable-star templates follow the
OGLE bulge catalogues \citep{Soszynski2013,Soszynski2016}. Each event is realised on the
\Rom{} cadence in three bands with photometric noise, blending, and interstellar extinction
drawn from survey-realistic distributions.

\subsection{Detectability-conditioned labelling}\label{sec:detcond}
The central methodological choice is that \emph{an event's label is set by what a perfect
observer could recover from the photometry, not by the parameters we injected}. A microlensing
event whose peak falls outside the 72-day season, or whose amplitude is buried under the noise,
is labelled \texttt{Flat}: no algorithm and no human modeller could distinguish it from a
constant source, so training the network to call it \texttt{PSPL} would only teach it to guess.
Likewise a binary whose caustic perturbation is everywhere below the noise floor is,
observationally, a single lens, and is labelled \texttt{PSPL}. Concretely, we compute the
$\dchisq$ between the true (noiseless) light curve and the best single-lens fit; an anomaly whose
$\dchisq$ is below a detection threshold does not earn the \texttt{NonPSPL} label. This removes
label noise at the decision boundary and, crucially, keeps the reported precision honest: a
\texttt{NonPSPL} the model ``misses'' is one no one could have caught.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

BinML is a convolutional stem feeding a small transformer encoder (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}),
$\bmlNparams$ parameters in total. The architecture is deliberately compact: the task is not
data-starved (we generate tens of millions of events) but inference-heavy, since the model must
eventually run on the whole survey stream.

\paragraph{Input.} Each band is binned onto a fixed grid over the 72-day window---F146 into
$\bmlBinsF$ bins ($\bmlEpochsF$ epochs at $\bmlCadence$-minute cadence), the colour bands into
96 bins each. Every bin carries five channels: the baseline-relative mean, \emph{min}, and
\emph{max} magnitude, the observed fraction, and an observed mask. Magnitudes are expressed
relative to a single F146 baseline subtracted from all bands, so source colour survives the
transformation.

\paragraph{Conv stem with non-learned min/max carry lanes.} The stem downsamples each band to
its token count ($\bmlNtokens$ tokens total). A learned averaging filter would smear a
$\sim$2.9-mag caustic spike down to $\sim$0.36~mag---the size of ordinary single-lens
curvature---erasing exactly the \texttt{PSPL}-vs-\texttt{NonPSPL} distinction the model exists
to make. We therefore reserve two stem channels as \emph{non-learned max/min pooling lanes}
carried unchanged through every downsampling step, so the caustic extremum survives by
construction rather than by what the optimiser happens to learn.

\paragraph{Encoder and head.} A four-layer, four-head pre-norm transformer
($d_{\mathrm{model}}=\bmlDmodel$) attends over the $\bmlNtokens$ tokens; absent colour bands
(F087 drops out in $\sim$38\% of events under extinction) are masked out of attention. A masked
attention-pooling layer feeds a flat six-way classifier. The model consumes binned magnitudes
only and is deliberately error-agnostic.

\section{The real-time cascade}\label{sec:cascade}

A survey classifier is not shown a finished light curve; it sees the season fill in night by
night. A binary lens looks exactly like a single lens during its smooth rise and only becomes
identifiable once its caustic is on screen. A model trained on complete curves learns to
\emph{anticipate} the anomaly from the smooth part---inflating early \texttt{NonPSPL}
probability and, in a deployed pipeline, firing follow-up triggers on planets that have not yet
revealed themselves.

We fix this by making the label a function of observation time. For each binary we compute the
day $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ at which its anomaly first becomes detectable ($\dchisq$ crossing the
same threshold as in \S\ref{sec:detcond}). During training we present truncated light curves cut
at random days; a binary truncated \emph{before} $t_{\mathrm{anom}}$ carries the \texttt{PSPL}
label (with the same detectability floor applied to the truncated data), and only earns
\texttt{NonPSPL} once the anomaly is inside the revealed window. The model thereby learns the
cascade \texttt{Flat}$\rightarrow$\texttt{PSPL}$\rightarrow$\texttt{NonPSPL}.

The effect is large (Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade}). The rate of \emph{premature} anomaly flags---%
calling a binary \texttt{NonPSPL} before its anomaly is observable---falls from
$\bmlPrematureBefore\%$ to $\bmlPrematureAfter\%$, a factor of several. The mean pre-onset
\texttt{NonPSPL} probability drops from $\bmlPreonsetBefore$ to $\bmlPreonsetAfter$. This costs
almost nothing on the full-season task: the missed-planet rate (a true \texttt{NonPSPL} called
\texttt{PSPL} on the complete curve) is essentially unchanged ($\bmlMissedBefore\rightarrow
\bmlMissedAfter$).

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Completeness at fixed purity.} Accuracy and macro-F1 are poor proxies for what a
follow-up pipeline actually needs, which is: \emph{of the anomalies I flag, what fraction are
real (purity), and of the real anomalies, what fraction do I catch (completeness)}. We therefore
fix the operating threshold to deliver a target anomaly purity of $\bmlPurityTarget$ (chosen on
a validation set) and report the completeness there as the headline number. It is the metric a
survey team specifies against.

\paragraph{Selection-aware reweighting.} The training set is not the survey population: rare and
faint events are up-sampled and by-product classes down-sampled for efficiency. Each test event
carries a \code{keep\_prob} recording its retention probability, and population statistics are
reweighted by $1/\code{keep\_prob}$. The reweighting is applied asymmetrically---purity and
precision are reweighted (they depend on the population mix of false positives), while recall is
not (it is intrinsic to a class). All population-level numbers below are so corrected.

\paragraph{The oracle ceiling.} As a reference we rank events by the injected anomaly $\dchisq$
computed from the \emph{noiseless} truth. This is an oracle---it uses the true parameters and is
not fieldable---so it upper-bounds any blind detector. It lets us ask not merely ``is BinML
good?'' but ``how much of the recoverable information does BinML actually recover?''

\section{Generalisation and limits}\label{sec:limits}

To test whether the numbers reflect genuine generalisation rather than memorisation of the
training distribution, we generated a $\bmlStressN$-million-event stress set from a disjoint
pseudo-random stream (seed base $\bmlSeedStress$ versus $\bmlSeedTrain$ for
train/validation/test), guaranteeing parameter tuples the model provably never saw. On the
natural-population subset BinML reproduces the held-out performance (macro-F1 $\bmlStressMacroF$).
Degradations appear only at physically extreme corners: sources fainter than $m\approx25$ (noise
produces false anomalies), very wide caustics $s>5$ (rarely crossed within the season), and
sub-day timescales (too few epochs on the peak). These are near-fundamental limits of the
photometry, not gaps the model could close with more data, and are documented as known weak
spots. Events with the blue band missing are still recovered at $\bmlSliceNoBlue$, confirming
the model degrades gracefully rather than failing when colour is unavailable.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

BinML is designed as a triage layer for the \Rom{} GBTDS: a cheap first pass that flags
candidate microlensing and anomalous events for the expensive, definitive step of full
light-curve modelling. Two properties make it fit for that role rather than merely accurate on a
benchmark. The completeness-at-fixed-purity framing lets a survey team dial the operating point
to their follow-up budget directly. The real-time cascade means the flag can be trusted as a
\emph{trigger}: a \texttt{NonPSPL} call means the anomaly has been seen, not guessed, which is
the precondition for autonomous follow-up. That BinML recovers near-oracle anomaly ranking
blind, in one forward pass, is what makes running it on the entire survey stream feasible where
per-event binary-lens fitting is not.

The clear next step is validation on real events---OGLE-IV and, ultimately, early \Rom{}
data---which is what turns ``works on simulations'' into ``works,'' and is the trigger for any
targeted retraining. The one weak spot with practical follow-up implications, faint-source false
anomalies, is the natural target for that retraining should real data show it matters.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented BinML, a compact multi-band transformer classifier for \Rom{} GBTDS light
curves built around two ideas---detectability-conditioned labelling and a real-time
anomaly-onset cascade---that make it deployable rather than merely accurate. It reaches
completeness $\bmlCompleteness$ at purity $\bmlPurity$ (AP $\bmlAP$), recovers near-oracle
anomaly ranking blind, cuts premature binary alerts from $\bmlPrematureBefore\%$ to
$\bmlPrematureAfter\%$, and generalises to a $\bmlStressN$-million-event unseen-parameter stress
test. The model, weights, and full simulation-to-inference pipeline are released as open source.
